Dames: ‘Mad, bad and dangerous to know’

by Laura Bohon
BOH00002@BYUI.EDU
Scroll Staff

Ethel Rosenberg, 35, and her husband, Julius, 34, are separated by a wire screen as they ride to separate jails in New York City March 29, 1951, following their conviction as traitors in the nation's first atom spy trial. Convicted with them was Morton Sobell, a 33-year-old radar expert. They were accused of trying to deliver war secrets, including vital A-bomb data, to the Soviet Union. AP Photo Archive
What do Medea, Catwoman and Lady Macbeth all have in common? No, not a penchant for black vinyl; they are all extremely dangerous women. Luckily for mankind, these three women are fictional. The next three women were very real, and, to steal a sentence from Lord Byron, were “mad, bad, and dangerous to know.”

The first woman has become the greatest example of cunning among Biblical figures. Her name, Jezebel, is now synonymous with “evil and scheming.”

Jezebel was a Phoenician princess who was married to Ahab, King of Israel. Her dangerous doings were of a strictly religious sense; Jezebel introduced to Israel the worst forms of Phoenician worship and highly encouraged idolatry among the children of Israel.

The marriage of Ahab and Jezebel, “more than any other single event, caused the downfall of the northern kingdom,” according to www.lds.org. With her marriage to Ahab, Jezebel managed to take down half of a kingdom.

The second woman is a bit more contemporary in her scheming. Margaretha Geertruida Zelle, also known as Mata Hari, was born in August 1876 and led a remarkable career as a spy for both the French and German forces during World War I.

Before she was a spy Mata Hari was already raising eyebrows as a dancer in France. Because of her fame, she had no problem moving through countries, even in the wake of World War I. Consequently, the French asked her to mingle with the Germans and find out anything she could.

During her first mission, however, she was arrested by the British Intelligence Service and the French forces became suspicious of her motives. It became clear that German army officers were paying her, but no one was sure for what, according to Radio Netherlands.

She became involved with men in the most powerful circles with access to military secrets, and both the French and the Germans reportedly enlisted her as a spy.

During one of her trips to France, Mata Hari was arrested and interrogated. During the interrogation, she finally admitted that she was, indeed, a German spy.

The French were convinced that she was “one of the greatest spies of the century, responsible for the death of tens of thousands of soldiers,” and so condemned her to death. Mata Hari was executed by a firing squad Oct. 15, 1917. She refused to wear a blindfold and was killed by 11 shots from a 12-man squad.

The last woman on this list of hazardous women belongs to the “dangerous to know” category, for even though she was only the second woman to be executed by the United States, evidence has since surfaced that she was perhaps innocent of the crimes for which she was convicted.

Everyone around her, though, seemed to be caught in a web of deceit, according to Douglas Linder, professor of law at the University of Missouri.

Ethel Rosenberg and her husband Julius Rosenberg were accused of espionage and treason against the United States during the height of McCarthyism and paranoid hysteria over Communism. Ethel’s brother, David Greenglass, was charged with similar crimes and testified that his sister was present during conversations about espionage, and that she typed notes on classified information.

After his sister’s death, David Greenglass said publicly that he had been pressured by the prosecution to testify against his sister and that he had no recollection of her ever being present for any espionage discussions.

Whether or not she was guilty of treason, Ethel Rosenberg refused to use her femininity to gain her freedom. She did not appeal to the jury’s emotions when she took the stand during her trial; she “seemed to display a contempt for the whole proceeding,” Linder said. She echoed her husband’s denials and version of events as she testified.

“She had nothing to do with this — she was completely innocent. I think she knew, but for that you don’t kill people,” former KGB agent Alexander Feklisov told The Washington Post.

Because she had been charged with an equal part of treason with her husband, Ethel shared his sentence. They were both condemned to death, and Ethel Rosenberg was the first woman to be executed in the United States since Mary Surratt was hanged for her role in the assassination of Abraham Lincoln.
