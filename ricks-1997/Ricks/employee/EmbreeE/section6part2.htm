Write the Resume

Employers Want to See

You've probably heard this advice before: Write your resume to fit the job and organization you want to join. But how do you do that? CPC talked to six employer recruiters and asked them: What do you want on a resume? The answers are as varied as the individuals and the companies they represent. The bottom line: No one resume will please every employer, and a little research before you apply will be valuable to the writing process. However, if you read the advice of these experts carefully, you'll find there's a lot of agreement on what a resume should and should not be.

Employers Want Substance

BURKHOLDER: Right now resumes are being scanned into an automated data base. Cover letters are also scanned in. Information can be retrieved through a keyword search, by experience, education, major, graduating year, or geographic preference. A resume should paint a picture of where a person is going. An objective is essential. It should be specific. Also, it should be easy to get a hold of a candidate--a phone number and an address for now and maybe a year out. The entry-level candidate should list: education next. Experience should be listed in reverse chronological order. Activities should come last. A resume should be one page in length.

The cover letter should be short. A line or two can accentuate the positive and confront the negative. As an example, if the letter is discussing experience it might say, "while I don't have what you said in the ad, I did do this..." It's the negatives that aren't dealt with that cause people not to get an interview. The cover letter should indicate geographic preference.

The mechanics of the resume and cover letter are important because my biggest concerns are about verbal and written communication skills. There is no excuse for a spelling error. This is a competition and the information should be done correctly.

For the experienced candidate, education follows experience. Within each paragraph, I want to see what the candidate was supposed to do and what he or she got done. Candidates should use generic terms--no jargon. Their resume shouldn't be more than two pages except for science and technical candidates who may have a list of publications.

BURKHOLDER LOOKS FOR:

Resume scannability.

Specific objective.

A cover letter that deals with the recruiter's objections.

DURANT: The organization of the resume gives me an idea of the professionalism of the individual. I look at how neat and organized the resume is. I look at business experience through internships, leadership positions, and extracurricular activities. For the entry level candidate, no objective is necessary. I don't expect one because I realize students are applying for several business positions. Students are looking at many types of careers and for a variety of opportunities. I would expect the student to know what it is they are interested in; that they have done some research to determine whether there is a career in the insurance industry for them. For someone with work experience, an objective is necessary because it helps me determine if this is a candidate for a position that I am trying to fill.

If grade point average (GPA) is above 3.0, I would like to see that on the resume. I want to see business experience, internships, language skills. A chronological resume is the easiest to read. If there has been diversified experience, sometimes resume can be set up by related experience.

For most, no more than a page is necessary. I get so many resumes that I don't want to be bothered reading more than that. For an experienced candidate, no more than two pages.

White or ivory paper and clear black type make the resume easy to read. I don't want to see mustard yellow or a blue background with clouds floating through it. That might be good for an artist, but not for a corporate job. I like to see white space--that makes a resume easier to read. I like bulleted items and an outline of experience rather than a paragraph form.

The cover letter must have a current phone number and address. One pet peeve for me is calling aunts, uncles, grandparents, or former roommates trying to find out where a candidate has moved. In the cover letter the candidate can explain that until a certain date he or she can be reached at one phone number; after that date, the new phone number is...

The cover letter should be short-- one page--too. I've gotten some that are four pages in length and that's a good way to get your resume put into the circular file. The first paragraph tells me why the candidate is contacting me, what kind of job he or she is looking for, and his or her objective. The second paragraph explains skills that the candidate has developed and how it relates to the job that he or she is seeking at our company. It tells me how the candidate's experience will make him or her a viable candidate. The last paragraph tells me where the candidate can be contacted. It is very important that the student be able to write and communicate thoughts and ideas clearly and concisely--a candidate is not writing a book.

[On both the resume and cover letter] grammar and spelling count. I circle errors on cover letters and resumes, and that generally reflects in my decision. If a candidate misspells the name of the company or my name, I wonder about research. Written and oral communication skills are very important and one of the factors on which we evaluate students. I recommend students have someone proofread resumes and use a spell-checker.

DURANT LOOKS FOR

An objective in the cover letter, but not necessarily on the resume.

Current telephone number and address.

Good grammar and spelling.

Business-like approach.

GIANNINI: We look at all majors. In the process of hiring college graduates, we focus on schools with retail or merchandising programs. For four-year schools, we look for all majors with retail experience and focus on business- related majors.

The resume should look interesting at a glance. It can't be too crammed, nor too basic. It should be well organized and visually make me want to read it. I don't want to have to decipher things. There should be thought behind the layout demonstrating creativity or style.

Pertinent information includes anything that adds to the individual's personal and professional growth and would make him or her an interesting candidate for my organization. Students should list their abilities and accomplishments on a piece of paper and then decide if those things relate to the job they want. Their background should indicate interest in customer service and retail and be related to specific jobs they are interested in.

I like facts on the resume. Who they are, how I can reach them--and it better be current. School. Degree. A chronological job history with a brief description of what they've done. Honest. Simple information.

One page is a good rule, especially if you're interested in a big, company. Be conservative. Many employers photocopy resumes and a second page could get lost. Start off conservatively. You'll lessen your chances with an oddly colored resume. Be conservative. Grammar and spelling are essential--it is indicative of double-checking your work.

The cover letter should be short and very easy to read. Don't summarize the entire resume in two paragraphs. Tell me who you are, why you are applying, and why I should read the resume.

GIANNINI LOOKS FOR

Brief, well organized resumes.

Backgrounds that show interest and relation to the job opening.

A chronological job history.

One-page resumes

MOTTOR: I always look for work experience--specifically analytical experience; someone with business experience. Someone who has spent four years in college and gotten real world experience. GPA is important. If a candidate doesn't put it down, we'll ask. We want employment history with dates and brief descriptions of jobs. We want to see clubs and leadership experience on campus because we want a well-rounded candidate.

I never look at the objective, unless it is to see which recruiter should get the resume. I go right to the experience and then to education.

Candidates should use just one page for their resume. No more. There's nothing worse than more than two pages. I prefer bulleted information over paragraph-style descriptions. And if I see something spelled wrong, to me, that shows the person is not detail-oriented. If

a candidate is trying to get an interview and misspells words, I can only imagine what the candidate will be like on the job. It is important that a candidate have someone proofread. Paper quality and color doesn't mean anything to me. I don't care if a resume is faxed to me either. I've hired just as many people from faxed paper as anything. It's what's on the resume that counts.

Experienced candidates should have the same information on their resumes. I like salary histories and requirements with their experience. It's a touchy issue and many people don't want to give everything up front. But it helps us not to waste anyone's time. We don't want to offer a position

that pays too low or offer something that a candidate is not qualified for.

MOTTOR LOOKS FOR:

Experience.

Bulleted information rather than paragraph form.

Good spelling.

For experience candidates, salary history and salary requirements.

RIZZELLO: The first items I review on a resume are the career objective and the degree. Then I need to know the background of the individual. I look at their education and key accomplishments. I want to see their work and summer experiences. A career objective is important to us because we recruit for all areas. You can have an engineering or science degree and want to get into sales. We need to know up front what a candidate wants to do.

One page is enough. We get a lot of resumes with two or three pages, but I rarely read the second or third page unless it is an experienced individual--and then I can understand two pages. For entry-level, a resume should be no more than one page.

The set up of the resume is not critical as long as the information is easy to find. Titles of sections are important and bulleted information is easier to read. It is important to consider the reader because most of us in the recruiting field read a lot and we need to look at resumes quickly. Thirty seconds is about right--but sometimes it is less if there is a knockout factor, if the candidate doesn't have the background we are looking for. So, it is important that experience and key accomplishments are easily read. You want to present your information so we can make a good match. Don't present the information to beat the reader.

I read the cover letter second if I am interested in the resume. I look at the resume to verify that the individual has the background that matches our needs. The cover letter gives me a look at the person's communication skills. Two or three paragraphs is enough. It is important that nothing be misspelled and that titles are correct. If one of these items is wrong, it is a knockout. Especially the titles. Some of our hiring managers feel that if a candidate doesn't bother to find out who they are, then why should they read the resume. And it is so easy to get titles. Get the annual report or call the company and ask the operator or ask the person's secretary. It shows a little effort to know the person's title and write a proper salutation.

The experienced candidate should use a time frame in listing experience. One line for their responsibilities, one line for key accomplishments. I need to know what a candidate did, not just his or her title. "Managed 25 people and a budget of $1 million" tells me something.

RIZZELLO LOOKS FOR:

A clear objective.

Short, bulleted experience.

Correct spelling and proper titles.

SCIARAFFA: A resume should contain information that sells the candidate by demonstrating that he or she has skills and abilities to be successful; information that demonstrates leadership, initiative, resilience, a strong work ethic, superior intelligence, and persistence. The information is meaningful to an employer if it describes the student's work experience and activities.

An objective is not necessary and could preclude a student from being considered for an opening. A candidate should let the employer review the resume and decide if the student's skills and abilities qualify him or her for an open position. The candidate shouldn't limit him- or herself unless he or she only wants to be considered for a specific position. A summary, however, is good for experienced candidates.

Resumes should be easy to read. Work experience should be listed in chronological order. If the candidate is a recent graduate and has a good GPA, that should be listed, as should any societies or honors that demonstrate superior intelligence. Generally, a resume shouldn't be more than two pages, but that should be dictated by work experience.

If a student is applying for a particular position, that information should go in the cover letter. The cover letter should include information that will make the employer aware of skills the student possesses that will enable him or her to be successful at the company.

Resumes should be perfect. They should be well organized without any mistakes. I wouldn't recommend using colored paper.

SCIARAFFA LOOKS FOR:

Information that demonstrates leadership, initiative, intelligence, and a strong work ethic.

No objective is necessary for entry-level candidates; a summary is good for experienced candidates.

Perfection in presentation: no mistakes

Computer-Friendly Resume Tips

by Joyce Lain Kennedy

What job hunters in the 1990s need to know is that computers read resumes different than people do.

Those who don't know this face risk having their resumes crash and burn in the embers of electronic storage. How big is the risk? Studies don't agree; but about half of all mid-sized companies and virtually all the giants are using job computers--or computers that scan resumes by the thousands and store their data for instant recall. Growing numbers of third party recruiting firms and state public employment services are also turning to job computers to process the legions of resumes they receive.

You can avoid the resume casualties of computer-friendly fire by following these suggestions in creating your resume:

Focus on Nouns, not Verbs

Job computers search resumes for all the "key words" they've been programmed to find--words that define the requisites of a particular job. The key words for an accountant might include "BS accounting, accounts payable, accounts receivable, IRS amendments, and CPA." If you scanned resume doesn't contain these key words, the computer passes it by . . . and you're out of the running.

While there are no absolute content rules in the resume searches by job computers, the majority of experts agree that action verbs that works so well on paper resumes lose their punch on scanned resumes. Job computers rarely search for a match on verbs like "inspired, built, calibrated, represented, or verified." Yes, your resume should include action verbs for sentence flow and human eyes; a computer just won't search for them.

The higher number of legitimate key words you have in your resume the greater are your chances of leaving e-storage and popping to the screen where humans can get a good look at you. The key words work as a kind of catcher's mitt by nabbing employers' attention.

Your counselors probably have told you to seek internships and other work experiences, as well as to join student chapters of such professional associations as the American Marketing Association or the National Recreation and Park Association. They were right for many reasons; the newest is that such activities produce marketable key words key words for you resume.

Keep graphics simple

Job computers adore simplicity. With the advent of desktop publishing, some paper resumes have become a "top-this" display of fonts and design. Wrong! The simpler, the better. By following the guidelines below, you can be sure the computer will be able to read your resume:

Use popular non-decorative typefaces.

Use a font size of 10 to 14 points.

Use a light colored (white is best), standard size, 8-1/2" x 11" paper, printed on one side.

Avoid italic text, script, and underlined passages. Capitalized words and boldface are okay.

Avoid graphics and shading. Don't compress spaces between letters.

Avoid horizontal and vertical lines, the confuse the computer.

Avoid staples and folds. If you must fold, do not fold on a line of text.

You name should be the first readable item on each page.

Resume Help for the Experienced Candidate

by Tom Smith

Remember you first resume--the one you developed with the help of your placement counselor? It served you well, producing interviews for your first job. Now you're ready to pull it out again and update it for a new employment campaign.

If you plan on merely updating your first resume, don't be surprised if hiring managers aren't especially impressed with it. Just as the job market has changed since you searched for your first job, so too has the resume.

Your new resume shouldn't be like your first resume

Experienced professionals must present to employers a slate of accomplishments that are entirely different from those of a new graduate. Students are tutored to write resumes that draw heavily on collegiate and internship experiences, and their resumes tend to be more general by nature, allowing for consideration in numerous positions.

That's not the case with experienced candidates' resumes. Research shows that employers are searching harder for candidates with proven track records in solving specific problems unique to their organizations. Therefore, a general resume of accomplishment organization is needed to show how your past accomplishments meet the employer's criteria. Here are a few of the items you'll want to keep in mind.

Summary, not objective: Whereas the resume for an entry-level position typically includes a general objective the type of opportunity desired, yo'll be better served by summarizing three or four skills that match the employer's needs. These highlights can appear in a short paragraph or quick "bullet" format.

Lead with experience, not education: There are other differences, too. Typically, new grad resumes provide education information after the objective. Although you should include this information on your resume, it's better to place it toward the bottom--your actual experience is more important than your education and should lead your resume.

Accomplishments, not just responsibilities: When detailing job history, don't fall into the trap of just listing responsibilities. Think in terms of what you accomplished by completing those tasks. In other words, don't just tell employers what you did; also tell them the outcome of your actions. "Responsible for developing departmental budge¢" could be a duty. "Proposed and tracked annual $500,000 departmental budget. Only division to meet deadlines and operate within budget during past three years," is a statement that details the results of your problem-solving abilities and clues in the employer to specific problems you can solve.

Include relevant activities, not hobbies: Clubs, organizations, and outside interests--a staple for entry-level candidate resumes--should be approached with caution by the experienced candidate. Activities that relate directly to your self-improvement efforts, e.g., continuing education, and the position you're seeking are important to include. Hobby type activities should not be included.

Your resume isn't limited to one page: The length of the resume may also be different for the experienced candidate. Whereas a two-page document might not be appropriate for a new graduate, it can serve a valuable purpose for the veteran employee. Often, the candidate has enough relevant accomplishments and experiences to expand to a second page.
