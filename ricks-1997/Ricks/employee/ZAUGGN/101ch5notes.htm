Chemistry 101

Chapter 5 notes and comments

Section 5.3

Note carefully that it takes energy to break a bond, and therefore energy is released when a bond forms.

Section 5.4

Group IVA elements, with four valence electrons, usually form four bonds. Group VA elements, with five valence electrons, usually form three bonds. Group VIA elements, with six valence electrons, usually form two bonds. Group VIIA elements, with seven valence electrons, usually form one bond. Hydrogen always forms only one bond. These generalizations are based on the octet rule.

The number of bonds mentioned above can be any combination of single, double, or triple bonds. Carbon, for example, might form four single bonds, or two double bonds, or a single and a triple, etc. (See p. 101.)

Section 5.5

When an atom doesn't form the usual number of bonds (see Section 5.4), it's usually because of the formation of a coordinate covalent bond.

Section 5.6

It is not true that you will always be given the arrangement of atoms, as the author states on page 102. See exercises 19 and 20 as examples. Here are my expectations for drawing Lewis structures: If the usual number of bonds occurs, I expect you to be able to figure out the arrangement for fairly simple molecules. In general, put the atoms needing the most bonds in the center, and those needing the fewest bonds on the outside. Even if the usual number of bonds isn't formed, if there is one atom of one element and two or more atoms of another element, assume that the single atom is in the center surrounded by the other atoms. Don't make any cyclic or ring structures at this point.

Section 5.8

The author refers to single, double, and triple bonds all as "one pair" for VSEPR purposes. This is a BAD idea. Let's count "groups" of electrons rather than "pairs". Then we won't be tempted to call a triple bond a "pair" of electrons.

Section 5.9

Study Figure 5.5 carefully. Note that the noble gases are not included, and note the electronegativity of hydrogen. It is much different than that of the halogens. Fluorine is the most electronegative element.

Section 5.10

Type of bonding is based on properties of compounds, rather than electronegativity differences. The paragraph that starts, "The numerical value..." on page 110, along with the "study help" in the margin right beside it, is not that useful.

Section 5.11

Symmetrical molecules are nonpolar, while unsymmetrical molecules are polar.
