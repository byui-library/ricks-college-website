BARRIERS RELATED TO CULTURE AND ASSUMPTIONS

The second major set of barriers are those that result from people's backgrounds and ways of thinking. They include organization cultures and subcultures, intercultural barriers, gender issues, and implicit assumptions.

Organization Cultures and Subcultures

Edgar Schein defines organization culture as . . . the deeper level of basic assumptions and beliefs that are shared by members of an organization, that operate unconsciously, and define in a basic "taken-for-granted" fashion an organization's view of itself and its environment.

Schein gives examples of two very different organization cultures. In a fictional organization he calls Action Company, there is a sense of high energy, rapid pace, intensity, and impatience, as well as high levels of interpersonal confrontation, argumentation, and conflict. The founding family group has imbued Action with the sense that truth lies not in revealed wisdom, but in "what works" in the marketplace, and that creativity and open debate, combined with intense loyalty, are the routes to success. Schein's second firm, Multi Com company, offers a sharp contrast. Here the pace is slower, more deliberate, with much more emphasis on schedules, punctuality, and formality. Insubordination is seldom tolerated. Everyone is cautious and precise in their communication, and there is less open debate than at Action. Multi employees are not supposed to give information unless asked to, and ideas coming from persons of higher rank receive more serious consideration.

Information flow is obviously more limited in Multi Company than in the open, less formal, culture of Action. Multi's ranking personnel simply do not address ideas they consider unimportant or with which they disagree. But the Action Company culture contains barriers as well. Where open debate is nearly always acceptable, certain kinds of communication become difficult. It is hard to reach decisions, to make and maintain agendas and schedules, and to state policies and goals, for example. While at Multi there are barriers to the exposure of new ideas, at Action there are barriers to stating clear plans and enforcing standards. Whether formal or open, any organization culture constrains its members' communication; members, accordingly, come to accept the basic assumptions of the culture, knowing that they must behave consistently with such assumptions if the culture is to treat them as members in good standing.

According to Schein's definition, a group of any size may develop its own unique culture. Thus, while pervasive cultures may exist organization-wide, highly diverse subcultures may exist within an organization as well. In an organization where one division or department forms a subculture like the Action Company culture and another resembles the culture at Multi's, communication between the two units will be difficult. Vijay Sathe provides an illustration from Cumrnins Engine Company, where the data processing managers valued formal systems and procedures. This ran counter to most other subcultures within the company, units that valued operating informally without systems. In an "old guard--new guard" conflict, the newer, more professionally trained managers in data processing clashed with others in the organization, straining and rendering ineffective the communication between the groups.

There are two principal ways to deal with cultural barriers to communication. First, members who have gained an awareness of the culture, its values, its formal and informal communication channels, and its centers of power, may use this awareness to determine how best to move information through the organization and get it accepted. Alternatively, a member can "constructively deviate from the culture." Methods for doing this include 1) using personal credibility and acceptance in the culture as a basis for arguing a point of view at odds with the prevailing one, 2) getting support from someone who has power within the culture, or 3) forming a coalition with others who, despite their low status, are numerous enough to influence those who hold power.
 

Intercultural Barriers

Members of organizations that operate across national boundaries must deal with clients, customers, suppliers, joint venture partners, and government officials from unfamiliar cultures. The following example illustrates the difficulties of intercultural communication:

You are negotiating a contract with a Japanese company and during the meetings there are times of silence on the part of the Japanese negotiating team. The negotiations, from your perspective as an American, appear to be proceeding at an unusually slow pace and even the simplest decisions or commitments appear to rake an inordinate amount of time. You begin to push a little harder and your frustrations mount as you begin to hear statements like "it will take a little more time" and "this is quite difficult." What should you do? What is happening to the negotiation process? How do the Japanese negotiate?

For organizations with subunits or subsidiaries located in more than one country, intercultural communication barriers may arise within the organization itself. This might seem surprising, given the strong socializing influence of organizational culture. We might expect that working for the same organization would reduce or eliminate the national cultural polarity of employees stationed in different countries. Apparently, however, the opposite is true. Andre Laurent observed managers from nine Western European countries and the United States who were working for organizations located within their native countries and compared them with managers who worked for one multinational company that had subsidiaries within each of the same ten countries. He found significantly greater cultural differences between the managers who worked within the same multinational company than between those who worked for subsidiaries located entirely within their own native countries. Possibly, pressure to conform to the culture of a foreign-based organization made the managers cling more strongly to their national cultures.

The managers in Laurent's studies revealed their differences through their agreement or disagreement with statements such as "Most organizations would be better off if conflict could be eliminated forever" and "Most managers seem to be more motivated by obtaining power than by achieving objectives.". Persons in intercultural work relationships can attempt to reduce cultural barriers by listening carefully to one another and by learning as much as possible about each others' cultures, including the communication practices each culture favors.
 

Gender-Related Barriers

Although work of almost all types is much more open to women now than it was even three decades ago, many people maintain traditional stereotypes about the sexes. According to such stereotypes, women lack, among other things, aggression, independence, decisiveness, and ambition; they compensate with high degrees of emotionality, talkativeness, tact, gentleness, and sensitivity to others. The male stereotype runs toward the opposite on each trait. Given these enduring beliefs, many people still feel that many organizational roles, including managerial positions, are more appropriate for men than for women. However, attitudes about women managers have become much more favorable in recent years. For example, a 1985 study found that only five percent of male executives viewed women managers unfavorably. Nevertheless, gender stereotypes still persist, and most people still view management in largely masculine terms.

In You Just Don't Understand, Deborah Tannen discusses ways in which gender stereotypes obstruct effective communication. Many men, she suggests, value independence and success in competition, while many women value close relationships and cooperation. Such men, according to Tannen, are reluctant to ask their bosses for appraisals of their performance, since this seems to reflect non-masculine dependence on the boss's judgment. Similarly, men who hold to the stereotype dislike asking for directions or instructions, an attitude she entitles "the 'I'll-fix-it-if-it-kills-me' urge." By comparison, women, according to the stereotype, like to give help and instructions in order to build and maintain relationships with those whom they help. Tannen observes that misunderstandings frequently arise when women attempt to help men, who in turn doubled their efforts to solve their problem independently. Tannen also cites an unpublished study by Alice Deakins of men and women executives' lunch conversations. Deakins found that when men and women ate lunch together, they avoided topics each gender group would discuss when eating alone. The observations of both Tannen and Deakins suggest that the ways people have been culturally trained to think and behave as men and as women, and to behave toward others based on gender, can significantly limit information flow and performance in the work setting. Tannen suggests that people who are aware of the stereotypes, rather than holding them as implicit assumptions, are better able to improve communication between the sexes. Research confirms that persons who have experience working with women and who have thus acquired accurate information about their skills accept them as competent and capable. In the face of effective, pragmatic evaluation, gender stereotypes are dying away.

Along with the fading but still-common problem of gender stereotyping comes sexual harassment, which impairs effectiveness and the quality of work life in many organizations. In a study of 1,232 working people in Los Angeles County, 24.6 percent of men and 34.1 percent of women reported having been sexually harassed on their current job. In addition to behavior so extreme as to meet the legal definition of sexual harassment, behavior such as flirting, making "complimentary" sexual comments, presenting sexually oriented jokes or cartoons, and wolf whistling affects the organizational work climate. The Los Angeles study found that more than three-fourths of both men and women had experienced such behavior at work. Though the quality of communication between the sexes in organizations has progressed, there is obviously still a way to go.
 

Implicit Assumptions

A major reason for the troublesome persistence of culture- and gender-related barriers is that they often take the form of implicit assumptions--beliefs people are not clearly aware that they hold. Such assumptions, are, in the words of Schein's definition of culture, "taken for granted." Someone holding a culture based assumption that disagreement should not be tolerated in organizations, for example, may simply accept perpetual, unquestioning agreement as the proper order of things and act accordingly without further reflection. People often act on the basis of assumptions they are not aware they are making. In addition to their roots in culture and in gender stereotypes, implicit assumptions can stem from an individual's personal background and habits of thought. Acting without awareness seems and is irrational, but it happens quite commonly. For example, a professor who lectures for a full class period, leaving no time for discussion, may be operating under the assumption that the students have nothing worthwhile to contribute to the learning process. While this could be a consciously thought out assumption, very often this is not the case; if asked about his or her lecture pattern, the professor would likely say, "Now that I think about it, I should have allowed discussion. Even if we hadn't covered as much material, the class might have learned more, and we'd have all enjoyed a discussion more than my droning on."

The distinguishing feature of an implicit assumption is that the individual has not "thought about it." Such assumptions may be defined as "a more or less unconscious taking for granted of certain basic but undefined conceptions of one's world." These assumptions differ from beliefs, which are consciously held and recognized as having alternatives. Because the individual is unaware of holding them, and consequently does not examine or revise them, implicit assumptions are the basis for automatic judgments and actions.

Implicit assumptions tend to impede communication by foreclosing the discussion of areas where information exchange might be helpful. In addition, a person acting from an implicit assumption often encounters surprising and disturbing responses from people who are reacting to something the originator is expressing but isn't aware of. Unfortunately, such assumptions often yield results that tend to justify them. The professor in the above example, seeing glassy-eyed students slumped in their chairs, might tend not even to consider whether such "nonentities" could enter into a worthwhile discussion.

Not directly observable, implicit assumptions are inferred from visible behavior. When a certain pattem of behavior occurs over and over in certain situations, and particularly when the behavior fails to solve problems, it is a clue that such behavior stems from rigid, unexamined, inarticulate assumptions. Implicit assumptions abound in most organizations. People who develop skill in identifying implicit assumptions acquire a valuable tool for improving their own communication and for helping others to do the same.
 

SOME GENERAL CHARACTERISTICS OF BARRIERS

The concept of barriers to high quality communication is a useful diagnostic tool. Perhaps its major advantage is that, as we have seen in the preceding sections, a barrier, once identified, helps suggest its own remedy. Besides using steps such as those suggested throughout this chapter for reducing barriers to different types of communication, those who participate in organizational communication would do well to understand three characteristics of barriers: [1] multiplicity, [2] interdependency, and [3] the nature of barriers as restraining forces.

The multiplicity of barriers means they tend to occur in clusters. When you find one, don't stop; look for others. A worker's boredom may result solely from communication underload, but the odds are good that other problems are also present. For example, the worker might strongly resent management and staff personnel. Also, he or she might belong to an informal group whose members disapprove of anyone who helps management. Improving this "bored" worker's productivity and morale could entail dealing with all of these barriers.

The concept of interdependency suggests that barriers tend to sustain and reinforce each other. For example, suppose that geographic distance and mutual mistrust hamper the quality of communication between two managers. The distance reduces the likelihood that they will overcome their mistrust, and, at the same time, their mistrust keeps them from bridging the distance to contact each other. Because of interdependency, we may act to remove one barrier, but the force of other tightly interwoven barriers may counteract our effort. Interdependency suggests that action aimed at altering several barriers simultaneously will often be more effective than trying to break down barriers one at a time.
 

SUMMARY

Barriers to communication block the flow of needed information. In organizations, two general types of barriers are organizational barriers to communication flow and barriers related to culture and assumptions. Organizational barriers to communication flow include barriers to downward, upward, and lateral communication, and barriers related to communication load. Barriers related to culture and assumptions are those arising from organization cultures and subcultures, intercultural barriers, gender-related barriers, and barriers stemming from false assumptions.

Principal barriers to downward communication are one-way communication, differences in values and perceptions between superiors and subordinates, mistrust by subordinates, and the psychic conflicts of leadership. Upward communication is often distorted by attitudes and perceptions common to subordinates, such as the desire for advancement; by attitudes and actions of superiors, such as reluctance to accept criticism; and by organizational characteristics, such as a formalistic structure or the MUM effect. Lateral communication can be much more efficient than communication that follows formal organizational channels. It is often inhibited, however, by increased specialization, a lack of management recognition and reward for such communication, and te inadequate expression of rivalry and disagreement between peers. Communication load increases as the number of decisions that must be made in a given time period increases, as quality requirements and information-processing efforts increase, and as information becomes less predictable. Reducing communication overload is a major goal for those who design organization structures and information systems. Communication underload, often the result of a lack of challenging job requirements, is a contrasting problem.

Organization-wide cultures can contain communication barriers. A formal, cautious, status-conscious culture may limit open, spontaneous expression. By comparison, an open, creative, debate-filled culture may block communication needed to make decisions, schedules, and policies. Subcultures within organizations can operate as barriers to communication between subunits whose members differ in values, assumptions, and perceptions.

Organizations that operate across national boundaries encounter intercultural differences that may become internal barriers in organizations with subunits or subsidiaries in more than one country. People from different regional and national cultures often differ widely in their assumptions about work relationships and organizational operations.

Gender-related barriers persist despite the increased acceptance of both sexes in virtually all occupations. The ways people have been culturally trained to behave as men and women, and to behave toward one another based on gender, can limit information flow and work performance. Sexual harassment at work remains a related problem.

With roots in culture, gender stereotypes, personal backgrounds, and habits of thought, implicit assumptions are taken-for-granted assumptions people are not aware they are making. Such assumptions form the basis for "automatic" judgments and actions--those that elude examination because the actor is unaware of the assumptions behind them. Skillin identifying implicit assumptions is a valuable aid to improving communication.
