SOMETHING WORTH ARGUING ABOUT

(Adapted from John Gage's The Shape of Reason)


The relationship of thesis to argument is simple: the better the thesis, the more successful the argument. What is a "better" thesis? Remember that there are other kinds of thesis sentences  for other types of writing purposes, and while an explicit thesis, stated early, tends to work best for this type of writing, there are other occasions when different approaches are appropriate. Remember also that there are many "subgenera" of argument--the editorial, the critique, the philosophical essay, the report interpreting data, and the proposal, among others. But the following advice fits generally any thesis sentence used to persuade someone or to argue a point.


1. A thesis is an idea.

2. It is stated as an assertion.

3. It is a reasoned response to a question at issue.

4. It is the central idea for a unified persuasive or argumentative essay.

To test any thesis sentence for appropriateness in an argumentative or persuasive essay, ask yourself the following:

1.  Is it an idea (not merely a topic)? Does it state, in a complete sentence, an assertion?

2. Does it answer a question that is really at issue for the audience? (What kind of question is it? One of definition, value, consequence or policy?)

3. Does the thesis say exactly what I want my essay to conclude? Are the terms I use precise and clear?

4. Has it developed out of a process of reasoning? Have I considered each side of the issue adequately?

5. Can it be developed reasonably?
