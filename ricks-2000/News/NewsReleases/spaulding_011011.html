October 11, 2001

BYU-Idaho acquires property 

along Teton and Snake rivers


REXBURG, Idaho – Brigham Young University-Idaho has purchased 120 acres of property along the Teton River and Henry’s Fork of the Snake River west of Rexburg for future academic and activity use.

The property, which was purchased from area farmer Dwight Spaulding, is adjacent to the BYU-Idaho Livestock Center five miles west of Rexburg on Highway 33. The South Fork of the Teton River forms the northern boundary of the property and the Henry’s Fork of the Snake River forms the western boundary.

The property includes some land that has been in pasture, as well as some land that has been planted with various crops in the past. Two portions of the land are forested with willows and other river bottom vegetation.

The property is also the burial place of Jenny Leigh and her six children. Jenny Leigh was a Shoshone Indian who married Richard Leigh, better known as Beaver Dick, an early-day trapper and settler. The couple lived in the area where the property is now located until Jenny and the couple’s six children died from smallpox. Beaver Dick Park, located west of the Livestock Center, is named after him. The Daughters of Utah Pioneers has been maintaining Jenny’s burial site for several years.

"We plan to cooperate fully with them in maintaining Jenny’s grave," said Anderson. "In fact, that’s one of the big reasons we bought it – to preserve the historical value of the land."

"One of the great benefits of this property is its close proximity to campus," said Devin Shaum, director of the new Student Activities Program. "This is a unique opportunity for academics and the activities program to work together in developing the programs and uses of the property."

The university anticipates saving time and travel expenses for students who are part of the activities program. Students will still be able to enjoy an outdoor experience without having to travel too far.

"This property opens up so many doors for the college," said Kevin Anderson, dean of the Division of Agricultural and Biological Sciences. "We still haven’t even finished coming up with ideas for what we can do yet."

Some of the plans for the land include preparing it for wildlife studies, hiking, canoeing, camping, biking, and horse paths, all of which are being carefully planned to minimize the environmental impact on the wildlife.

Plans are also being laid for planting 70 acres to pasture, as well as re-vegetating the northern 27 acres with trees and shrubbery that naturally grow in the area.
