Ricks College News Release, Feb. 26, 1998

From Afton to Arkansas:

First lady of Ricks comes home to West

By DON SPARHAWK
Ricks College News Bureau

Moving to Rexburg has been a bit like coming home for Susan Bednar, wife of the new Ricks College president.

Born and raised just over two hours away from Rexburg in Afton, Wyo., Mrs. Bednar says it feels good to be back in familiar surroundings. She particularly loves being so near her family again.

While growing up, she had a love for the Upper Snake River Valley of Idaho. Her grandmother, Grace Taylor Clement, lived in Lewisville, and she had aunts and uncles and cousins in the valley. As a teen-ager, she also came to Idaho Falls each week to take piano lessons from Jack Bowman.

Yet after 17 years of living in Arkansas and Texas, coming home hasn't been all that easy. She left close friends, a home filled with memories and a close- knit neighborhood.

While living in Arkansas hadn't always been easy especially during those first years when she missed her family Mrs. Bednar had become perfectly content with her life. She was happy as a wife and mother, homemaker, piano teacher and school volunteer.

Today, her life isn't so private. She's expected to be a gracious host, an extemporaneous speaker and an example wherever she goes. Speaking before several thousand students at a devotional is a "humbling but rewarding experience."

Mrs. Bednar says Afton was a good place to grow up. "It was very isolated and very small," she says. "I grew up with loving, caring, supportive parents in a gospel-oriented home."

Her father, Kay Robinson, had met Nyla Clement of Lewisville at Brigham Young University. They married and settled in Afton where he went to work at Star Valley State Bank, which had been started by her grandfather.

Her parents still live in the same house only a few blocks away from the ski hill and rope tow where she learned to ski.

At the age of 8, she began taking piano lessons. She loved the piano and is grateful to her parents who sacrificed to buy a piano and pay for lessons.

After graduating from Star Valley High School in 1970, she was off to BYU where she majored in elementary education and minored in French and music. "My folks encouraged me to study something I could fall back on if I ever needed it," she says of her studies to become a teacher.

In the fall of 1973, she met David Bednar. They belonged to the same student ward at BYU. She was particularly impressed by the reverent way he passed the sacrament on Sundays. "He was very motivated and very ambitious," she says. "But most of all, I was attracted to his spiritual qualities."

After graduation in 1974, she taught first grade for two years in American Fork, Utah. "I loved it to see the light go on in a child's mind was just exhilarating," she says.

The couple continued dating and were married in the Salt Lake Temple in March of 1975.

While she loved teaching school, motherhood soon took precedence. Three boys came along. "It was an adjustment at first," she says. "I had to grow into that role I never liked to babysit as a young girl."

Over the past 20 years, she has tried to raise sons who are sensitive and aware of spiritual things. "They have learned the power of their example, and know they are representing our family, our church and the Savior."

Today her sons are almost grown and leaving home. Twenty-one-year-old Eric, a returned missionary from Finland, is in his second year of studies at BYU. Mike, 19, is serving a church mission in Bolivia. And Jeffrey, 15, is a 10th-grader at Madison High School in Rexburg.

Mrs. Bednar says their neighborhood in Fayetteville, Ark., had many traditional families where mothers stayed at home to raise their children. These women were a great support to her.

"Southern hospitality is a real thing," she says. "The people are friendly, compassionate and service oriented. The LDS people certainly weren't the only ones who would take casseroles or a loaf of bread to their neighbors."

She says children in the South were well behaved and taught to be respectful of their elders.

Mrs. Bednar got involved with education as her children began attending school. She was president of several parent-teacher organizations and served on committees that brought about changes in curriculum and policy. "You can't know what's going on in school if you're not there," she says.

She also volunteered because she had been taught by her parents that community service was a responsibility. "It is also important to serve outside of the church," she says. "I wanted to teach that to our children."

She adds that living far away from their families caused the Bednars to stretch as a family. "It forced us to lean on each other for support."

While her husband's church callings often took him away from home on weekends, she stayed home. "We didn't travel with him very often," she says. "Our boys wanted to be in their home ward. We chose to live as normal a life as we could in our home ward."

She has held many church positions, including being a Sunday School teacher, ward organist or the Primary chorister. For the last three years, she had been the advisor for the Lambda Delta Sigma sorority at the Fayetteville LDS Institute of Religion.

She has played to be a supporting role to her husband. "But we are equal partners in the decisions we make as a husband and wife," she adds. Because he has been gone a lot, she has also learned how to be independent.

As she reflects back on her life, she can see she's been prepared for her role at Ricks. "First of all, I feel like Nephi I was born of goodly parents."

She's also had plenty of opportunities to serve in the church. "I feel like I've grown tremendously as I had the opportunity to share my testimony and be an example to others," she says.

They watched a small ward of about 75 people grow into three thriving wards and a student branch in the Fayetteville-Springdale area. She watched church members sacrifice to attend meetings, travel six hours to a temple and youth get up at 5:45 a.m. to attend early morning seminary.

As for her role at Ricks, Mrs. Bednar hopes to portray the image of a woman who is anchored in the gospel. As she and her husband have talked with students, they have emphasized the need to live on the Lord's side of the line.

"We have stressed the importance of being obedient in a wicked world and the blessings that come as we are worthy of the companionship of the Holy Spirit."
As she settles into her new home, Mrs. Bednar is getting accustomed to her new life. She is a Miamaid teacher in her ward on Sundays and she serves on the Women's Awareness Committee at the college.

"The hardest thing is the lack of anonymity and privacy," she says. "And there is an expectation to be at so many events and places. I have to juggle with the role of trying to be a good wife and mother."
