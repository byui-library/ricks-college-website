Student Employment & Career Placement

1. What Is an Internship? What Is Cooperative Education (Co-op)?

An internship or co-op is basically work experience that corresponds to your major, although some institutions give them slightly different definitions. This is generally accomplished with summer work or part-time work through the school year. The internship can be a formal, previously existing internship, or something that you create on your own.

 

2. Basically, What Is the Process that I Will Follow?

Contact the department to find out if you are eligible for an internship.
Describe the internship to the faculty co-op representative for approval.
Obtain the internship from the employer.
Register for the internship using the course catalog.
Fill out a "Co-op/Internship Agreement" (a contract between you, the employer, and the school).
Perform the internship and necessary academic work.

 

3. Why Should I Consider Getting an Internship?

An internship gives you work experience to list on your resume.
An internship helps you decide if you want to continue with a particular major.
Internships help you "get your foot in the door" at certain businesses.
Internships help you meet people in the industry who you can contact later.
Internships help you develop professional skills and work habits.
Recruiters often hire full-time employees from their intern pool.
An internship provides the chance to develop practical and marketable skills.

 

4. Where and How Do I Find the Internship?

The best way to find an internship is to contact the people that you already know in your own home town. 80-90% of the time, employers hire people that they already know.

Another option is to contact faculty members or the Career Placement and Cooperative Education Office to find out about currently existing internships.

Lastly, you can refer to an internship directory which lists internship opportunities with different firms from around the nation. These directories can be purchased or can be browsed at the Career Placement and Cooperative Education Office.

 

5. Can I Receive College Credit for an Internship?

Under most circumstances . . . yes. Most departments on campus have internships/co-ops that you can register for in the course catalog when you are registering for your other classes. Credits range from 1-10, depending on their requirements and your desired workload.

 

6. Do I Get Paid for Doing an Internship?

The good news is that most interns are paid. Although, in some cases the work is on a volunteer basis. Ultimately, you want to be paid for the internship. But, do not overlook the "long-run" and positive benefits of an internship even if such is on a volunteer basis.

 

7. Will I Need to Do Any Academic Work During the Internship?

In most cases you will need to write reports, etc. about your experience. You can find out the specifics from your faculty co-op representative.

 

8. How Does the Employer Benefit?

Cooperative education is an excellent source of temporary and potentially permanent personnel who can be evaluated before hiring. Essentially, the employer has an opportunity to examine a potential permanent employee without making a long-term commitment.
Co-op programs provide businesses with highly motivated employees.
Co-op is an efficient and low-cost way of recruiting permanent employees.
Co-op students are enthusiastic and bring new ideas.
Labor costs are lower.
Cooperative education produces better employees.
Cooperative education links employers with the educational institution.

 

9. What Is the Historical Background of Co-op?

Cooperative education had its beginning at the University of Cincinnati in 1906 under the leadership of engineering professor Herman Schneider. He developed the program as a result of his own experience while earning his way through college. Professor Schneider realized that his education had become more meaningful as a result of his work, since many facets of his profession could not be learned in the classroom.

After the success of the program at the University of Cincinnati became known, other universities began to adopt Cooperative Education.

By 1942 there were 20 institutions with Cooperative Education. Moderate growth was seen over the next 20 years, but after 1963 growth accelerated. There were nearly 400 institutions in 1972 with co-op programs, and by 1985 nearly 200,000 students were participating at 900 colleges and universities. [Cooperative Education Handbook, South Dakota School of Mines & Technology, Rapid City, South Dakota.]

 

10. One Possibility for An Internship While at Ricks.

The Student Employment Office at Ricks College has work-study jobs available to qualifying students. The State of Idaho has been very helpful by funding a "Work Study" program which entails the state paying half of a student's wage, thereby allowing companies to hire more students for less money. To be eligible you must meet the following criteria:

1) You must be Idaho resident
2) The job must be in Idaho
3) You must be in good standing
4) The job must be related to your major.

 

11. What Is the Co-op/Internship Agreement?

The Co-op/Internship Agreement is basically a contract between three people -- the student, the employer, and the college.

What Does the Student Agree to Do?
Conform with the normal work hours of the establishment, including overtime when requested. Sunday work is discouraged, but may be required at times.
Report to work on time.
Keep business matters confidential, and work in the best interests of the employer.
Keep neatly and appropriately dressed and well-groomed (Ricks College dress, grooming, and conduct apply).
Be courteous to all.
Request permission from both the employer and the Ricks faculty advisory before taking time off.
Submit reports promptly.
Student will keep a journal and/or complete report of technical growth.

What Does the Employer Agree to Do?
Designate an intern supervisor.
Employ the student for the entire period as agreed unless terminated for reason.
Pay the starting wages as per the agreement. Additional compensation may be provided at the employer's discretion.
Give the student the opportunity to work on different jobs in order to acquire and practice various skills.
Counsel with the student, answer questions, and discuss methods and operations.
Notify the Ricks faculty advisor immediately, preferably by phone, on any cause of dissatisfaction or of misconduct on the part of the student.
Provide workers' compensation or other employment benefits to the student to the extent required by law and to indemnify and hold Ricks College harmless from any liability for the loss or damage arising from the performance of the agreement.

What Does the College Agree to Do?
Designate a Ricks College faculty advisor.
The Ricks faculty advisor will contact the student and employer, discuss the student's progress, and advise relative to the program of study.
The Ricks faculty advisor will strive to promote harmony and cooperation between the employer, the student, and Ricks College.
