Sections 1.3 and 1.4

The author's problem is that he hasn't defined "substance" yet, but uses it extensively in these two sections. You might consider these modifications to the author's definitions in these two sections:

A physical property is a characteristic of a substance that can be observed without changing it into a new substance.

A physical change is a process that does not change one substance into another substance.

A chemical change is a process that changes one or more substances into other substances.

Add sublimation and deposition to the other changes of state mentioned in Section 1.4 (like freezing, evaporation, etc.). You can find their meanings on pages 158 and 160.

Section 1.5

The author's first "study help" in the margin on page 6 is simply not true. "Substance" and "pure substance" are synonymous to a chemist. A mixture is a "physical combination of two or more substances" (see Figure 1.5), so a mixture is not a single substance, as this study help would imply.

We cannot use "visible" as a means of distinguishing between heterogeneous and homogeneous mixtures. Table salt and homogenized milk are both heterogeneous mixtures, even though they look homogeneous to the eye. In a homogeneous mixture (called a "solution" on page 174), the components must be mixed at the atomic or molecular or ionic level.
