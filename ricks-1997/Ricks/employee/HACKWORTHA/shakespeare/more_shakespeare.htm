Shakespeare's Wisdom

1. 'Tis beauty that doth oft make women proud,
'Tis virtue that doth oft make them most admired,
'Tis discipline that makes them seem divine.

2. A good cause makes a strong arm.
God befriend us, for our cause is just.

4. Not the king's crown, nor the deputed sword,
The marshall's truncheon, nor the judge's robes
Become them with one half so good a grace
As mercy does.

5. For what is wedlock forced but hell
An age of discord and continual strife
Whereas the contrary bringeth bliss
And is a pattern of celestial peace.

6. Oh, what a tangled web you weave
When first you learn to deceive.

7. So full of artless jealousy is guilt
It spills itself in fearing to be spilt.

8. Unbidden guests are often welcomest
When they are gone.

9. I earn what I eat, get what I wear,
owe no man hate, envy no man's happiness,
glad of other men's good, content with my harm.

10. Honesty coupled to beauty,
is to have honey a sauce to sugar.

11. I hate ingratitude more in a man,
Than lying, vainness, babbling, drunkenness
Or any taint of vice, whose strong corruption
Inhabits our frail blood.

12. Best men are often moulded out of their faults.

13. Maids are May when they are maids,
but the sky changes when they are wives.

14. All that glitters is not gold,
Gilded tombs do worms enfold.

15. What's gone and what's past help
Should be past grief.

16. Misery acquaints a man with strange bedfellows.

17.  I know a passion still more deeply charming
That fevered youth ever felt; and that is love
By long experience mellowed into friendship.

18. The quality of mercy is not strained:
It droppeth like the gentle rain from heaven
Upon the place beneath; it is twice blessed,
It blesseth him that gives, and him that takes.

19. For they say, if money go before, all ways do lie open.

20. There is a tide in the affairs of men,
Which, taken at the flood, leads on to fortune;
Omitted, all the voyages of their life
Is bound in shallows and in miseries:
On such a sea we are now afloat,
And we must take the current when it serves
Or lose our ventures.

21. The fool doth think he is wise, but the wise man knows himself to be a fool.

22. 'Tis not enough to help the feeble up, but to support him after.

23. Wise men ne'er sit and wail their loss, but cheerily seek how to redress their harms.

24. Things sweet to taste prove in digestion sour.
Richard II.

25. To fear the foe, since fear oppresseth strength,
Gives in your weakness strength unto your foe.
Richard II

26. He jests at scars that never felt a wound.
Rome and Juliet

27. He that is stricken blind cannot forget the precious treasure of his eyesight lost.
Romeo and Juliet

28. Proceed wisely and slow; they stumble that run fast.
Romeo and Juliet

29. What's in a name? That which we call a rose by any other name would smell as sweet.
Romeo and Juliet

30. Misery acquaints a man with strange bedfellows.
Tempest

31. Do not give dalliance too much rein.
Tempest

32. Men prize the thing ungain'd more than it is.
Troilus and Cressida

33. Be not afraid of greatness: some men are born great, some achieve greatness, and some have greatness thrust upon them.
Twelfth Night

34. For women are as roses; whose fair flower,
Being once displayed, doth fall that very hour.
Twelfth Night

35. What is love? 'Tis not hereafter;
Present mirth hath present laughter;
What's to come is still unsure;
In delay there lies no plenty;
Then come kiss me, sweet and twenty!
Youth's a stuff will not endure.
Twelfth Night

36. Maids, in modesty, say 'No' to that
Which they would have the profferer construe 'Ay'.
Two Gentlemen of Verona

37. That man that hath a tongue, I say, is no man
If with his tongue he cannot win a woman.
Two Gentleman of Verona

38. O! They love least that let men know their love.
Two Gentlemen of Verona

39. They do not love that do not show their love.
Two Gentlemen of Verona

40. O, they love least that let men know their love.
Two Gentlemen of Verona

41. O, how this spring of love resembleth
The uncertain glory of an April day,
Which now shows all the beauty of the sun,
And by-and-by a cloud takes all away.
Two Gentlemen of Verona

42. Win her with gifts, if she respect not words.
Dumb jewels often in their silent kind
More than quick words do move a woman's mind.
Two Gentlemen of Verona

43. Woman sometimes scorns what best contents her
For scorn at first makes after-love the more.
If she doth frown, 'tis not in hate of you,
But rather to beget more love of you.
If she doth chide, 'tis not to have you gone.
Take no repulse, whatever she doth say;
For 'Get you gone!' she doth not mean 'Away'.
Two Gentlemen of Verona

44. How use doth breed habit in a man.
Two Gentlemen of Verona

45. Experience is by industry achieved,
and perfected by the swift course of time.
Gentlemen of Verona

46. Love comforteth like sunshine after rain.
Venus and Adonis

47. What's gone and what's past help should be past grief.
Winter's Tale

48. You pay a great deal too dear for what's given freely.
Winter's Tale
