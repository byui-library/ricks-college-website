Ricks College News Release, May 26, 1998

Expanded enrollment at Ricks College
gives big boosts to area economy

By DON SPARHAWK
Ricks College News Bureau

REXBURG, Idaho — When the fall semester begins, enrollment at Ricks College is expected to reach a record high of 8,600 students.
During the past two years enrollment will have jumped 8 percent, which is the most growth Ricks has experienced since a 7,500-student cap was placed on enrollment in the 1980s.
The growth not only is allowing more students to attend the largest private junior college in the country, but is having a significant impact on the economy of the upper valley.
"It's like adding another large company to the local economy," says Community Services Vice President Brent Kinghorn. "This addition will have a major impact on our community."
To handle the approximately 600 new students, during the past year the college has hired or is in the process of hiring 71 new people. Some of these employees have been working for nearly a year while others will begin their employment later this summer when the new school year begins.
"Many of these are professional people who are bringing with them a rich and varied background," Kinghorn says. "They're young, they're energetic and they're bringing an excitement to campus and into the community with their new ideas."
He adds that most of these families will settle in Rexburg and the surrounding communities. They are generally families with children who will add students to local school systems that are currently experiencing sagging enrollments. "They will give our schools an additional shot in the arm," he says.
Considering that the turnover rate of personnel at Ricks is extremely low, Kinghorn says most of these new families plan to make the upper valley their home for many years to come.
A breakdown of new employees shows 37 new full-time faculty members, 7 part-time faculty, 10 administrators and 17 staff members. This will bring the total employment at Ricks to 863 full-time and 192 part-time employees.
Permission from the Board of Trustees also was given to hire 200 more students on campus to fill part-time jobs (about 10 hours each per week).
School officials say the estimated economic infusion from these new employees will amount to about $2.2 million annually.
The impact on the economy doesn't stop there. The estimated 600 new students at Ricks will be spending their own money on food, housing and incidentals. Assuming an average student spends $3,100 a year, another $1.9 million will be spent locally.
Based on state of Idaho statistics, Ricks economics instructor Richard Moss says the extra $4 million a year will circulate about four-and-one-half to five times in the Rexburg area and up to seven times through the economy of southeastern Idaho.
"That's a big deal," he says. According to these calculations, this will generate $18-20 million in economic activity in the Rexburg area and up to $28 million in the broader southeastern Idaho area.
The new students are being absorbed into privately owned housing built last year and into existing housing that was not full. College Housing Director Ric Page has not heard of much new construction this summer, but knows of plans for some new housing for the following year.
Academic Vice President Don Bird emphasizes the growth will culminate this year at the 8,600-student level. In the future, new employees will be hired mainly when someone leaves or retires.
"This is about it," he says. "Any additional increase would require a whole new round of campus construction."
He explains The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, which owns and operates the college, currently has other priorities for spending, notably an accelerated temple-building program. At the present time, there is no room to handle more students in the existing classroom and office space on campus.
Bird says most of the new faculty are teaching in general education areas ranging from math and biology to religion and communications.
In determining how many new faculty to hire, he says the college estimated that one new faculty member was needed for about each additional 25 students. This will keep the faculty-student ratio at 1:25.
Bird says the new faculty are required to have a minimum of a master's degree and many of the new employees have their doctorates.
Because Ricks is considered a "teaching" college, a priority was placed on hiring faculty who will have excellent skills in interacting with students.
"Typically, they're probably in their low to mid 30s in age, probably between a master's and doctorate degree in education, and they're are all very excited and enthused about coming to Ricks," Bird says.
Some are taking some financial cuts to leave business or research jobs to teach. He says many are drawn to the area because of the quality of life that's found in a rural setting and because of the high standards that are expected of the students.
"The students are very committed to high principles and must live in agreement with the honor code," he says. "That's one of the enticing things about Ricks to them – they like the type of students who come here."
Gordon Westenskow, director of Admissions at Ricks, says the extra 600 spaces for students has helped, but not solved, the admissions crunch.
Even with the additional spaces, between 1,600 and 1,700 qualified applicants were turned away for the upcoming fall semester.
He says about 4,400 new students are expected in the fall. Another 800- 1,000 will likely be admitted for winter semester and some 800 students for summer terms, bringing the total to about 6,000 new students a year.
