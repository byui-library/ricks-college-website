September 28, 2001

BYU-Idaho students construct

new water feature

    
REXBURG, Idaho – Horticulture and landscaping students at BYU-Idaho recently celebrated the official opening of the Beaver’s Den in the Thomas E. Ricks gardens located south-east of the Spencer W. Kimball Student and Administrative Building on the university campus.

            Indeed, they had reason to celebrate.  This project, two years in the undertaking, is quite an accomplishment, according to Byron John, the faculty member who supervised the project.

            “This is a big project,” John said.  “Normally, a landscaping company would charge around $100,000 to do something like this.  For beginning students to do it, and do it well, is one heck of an accomplishment.”

            The water feature, or the Beaver’s Den as it is affectionately called for the artificial beaver’s den built into the back of the feature, prominently displays two 12-foot waterfalls and separate ponds.  Surrounding the water are large boulders, which are actually student sculptures, providing a place to sit and enjoy the quiet beauty the ponds have brought to the gardens.

            The construction of the falls followed a standard process.  First, the students excavated enough space for the ponds, and then cinderblock walls were built.  The walls are covered with a waterproof liner to keep the cinderblocks from deteriorating.  Next, the students used rubble from the excavation of the new multi-use building being constructed east of the gardens to give shape to the walls and water formations.  All of this was wrapped in chicken-wire to keep its shape until more could be done.

While all of this was happening, the students had been making trips around the region to study rocks:  what they look like at different angles, or how the texture of each rock is formed.  Sketches were made and rubber linings, or skins, were made off of real rocks.  These skins were later used on the rock formations in the gardens to give them a life-like texture.

A contractor was hired to spray concrete onto the chicken-wire shapes the students had formed.  Once the concrete had hardened, 2 to 3 inches of mortar was applied, and students began using the rubber skins they had made to add texture to the formations, and finally they used trowels to make the finishing touches.

            “All in all, this was a big project, and the students put in hundreds and hundreds of hours,” John said.  “We received thousands of dollars in donations from local businesses, and we greatly appreciate their support.  Without their donations, and the help from the college, we could never have done something on this scale.  I’m proud of the students, and they should be proud of themselves as well.”

            Jared Ricks, a sophomore from Rexburg, was one of the rotating foremen on the job.  “We would just take turns learning how to supervise others and to make sure that everything was done right.  I was able to work on the waterfalls a lot, and I really enjoyed applying what we had been taught in class.  It was great to take nature and recreate it.”

            Tiffany Astle, a sophomore from Star Valley, Wyo., was just as enthusiastic about her part in the project.  “It was an awesome opportunity, and I don’t think we’ll ever be able to do something like this again.  I thought it was neat to take the unwanted rubble from the multi-use building and turn it into something beautiful.”

            When asked if there was anything else that he felt should be mentioned, John added, “I think everyone should know that this feature, and every other feature in the garden, is done entirely by the students.  No outside work has been hired to make the gardens as beautiful as they are.  The students deserve a lot of credit for what they have done.”
