C. S. Lewis Honors

To Love is to Be Vulnerable

        To love at all is to be vulnerable. Love anything, and your heart will certainly be wrung and possibly broken. If you want to make sure of keeping it intact, you must give your heart to no one, not even to an animal. Wrap it carefully round with hobbies and little luxuries; avoid all entanglements; lock it up safe in the casket or coffin of your selfishness. But in that casket--safe, dark, motionless, airless--it will change. It will not be broken; it will become unbreakable, impenetrable, irredeemable. The alternative to tragedy, or at least to the risk of tragedy, is damnation. The only place outside of Heaven where you can be perfectly safe from the perturbations of love is Hell.
                                                                        (C.S. Lewis, The Four Loves, 169)

What You Used to Think

        To be honest, I have never heard anyone talk about love this way. I have heard about, and experienced to some degree, the pain that results when love is not returned by the loved-one. But I always believed that the best way to keep from being hurt is to toughen myself up so that my heart could be strong enough to deal with the disappointment of love "next time." I suppose I've watched others "play it safe" while in a relationship. Give some specifics. By their examples I was taught that the safe way to love is waiting to love until you are sure that someone loves you--rarely, if ever, should you initiate love. By loving this way, I would be less likely to get hurt by being disappointed or treated inconsiderately. I figured a person loved most intelligently by making oneself available for love, or shopping around for a person with whom one felt secure. That way I would not have to worry about making a fool of myself.

What You Now Know

        But if Lewis is right, shopping around in relationships for safe love can never amount to love at all. Instead, it's a way of putting love off while convincing myself that I'm looking for that perfect one. According to Lewis, there doesn't exist a man or woman with whom my love for them won't make me vulnerable to pain or heartache, disappointment or frustration. The trouble is, it's hard to open up to this kind of love since I've been hurt before. For this reason many of the relationships I have with others could be based upon my efforts to convince myself that I am open and honest, when, in reality, I am most likely selectively closed for fear of being hurt again. Getting over such fear is not an easy task. In fact, a struggle I had last week demonstrates that doing so is more difficult than I ever imagined.
