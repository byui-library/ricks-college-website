Describing periodicals

To describe a periodical article for research give:
  • Author of article
  • "Title of article"
  • Title of periodical
  • volume and issue for scholarly journals
  • date of periodical
  • pages upon which article appears.
Librarians need more complete descriptions. Entries in the library catalog which Technical Services use may include:
  • At what interval is the periodical published?
    • Some publication are ahead or behind the date on publication.
    • Weekly, monthly, quarterly, annually and semi-annually are frequency of publication.
    • Some publications change the interval of publication without notice.
    • Volume and issue numbers are called the enumeration of the periodical.
    • The date of an issue is called the chronological label.
  • What is the address of publisher or distributor?
    • EBSCO helps libraries obtain periodicals.
    • When copies are not received, EBSCO helps us "claim" or obtain missing issues.
    • Does the publisher have a telephone number or Internet address?
    • What is the cost and availability of back issues?
    • What is the cost and availability of current subscriptions?
  • In what databases does the periodical appear?
    • Is the periodical indexed as a popular magazine?
    • Is the periodical indexed as a scholarly journal?
  • What over titles does the publication have?
    • Has a change been made in the title
    • official title, if different from cover title
  • Unique number are assigned to serials: ISSN or International Standard Serial Number.

Vocabulary

    Librarians use the term "periodical" for regularly issued publications except newspapers. This term was one of the first names for this type of publication. The main feature is that this type of material is published at certain periods of time: weekly, monthly, and quarterly. Newspapers tend to be published more frequently: daily, three times a week, and weekly. The distinction between newspapers and periodicals is blurring. Format is more of a distinction here. Full sheets or "folio" and half sheets or "quarto" size tend to formats shelved with newspapers. Some newspapers are indexed in the same databases are periodicals. The New York Times has Sunday supplements shelved with our periodicals: New York Times Magazine and New York Times Book Review. Other names for periodicals were added later.

"Journal" originally was used for daily newspapers. "Journal" is also used for the daily writings of individuals that may be housed in Special Collections or reprinted as history books. Gradually "journal" was used to emphasize the news contained in the publication. Now scholarly periodicals with news for a special field of interest are known as journals. An example of a scholarly journal is the New England Journal of Medicine. Scholarly journals try to present accepted scientific belief. When controversy exists the publication may have a referee determine if enough data supports the theory. Publications which regularly use this type of arbitrator are called "refereed."

"Magazine" came from an Arabic word for "storehouse". The word "magazine" was also used for places of ammunition storage. British English still prefers the definition associated with ammunition. American English publications tend to use "magazine" for popular newstand titles. "Magazine" was used originally  in titles of periodicals that were a collection of stories, articles, essays, poems, and other literature. Magazines do not normally have the authority of scholarly journals. Many articles are not signed. Magazines may not give the credentials of the author. Magazine articles may be readable but not necessarily accurate.

Reader's Digest and other similar periodicals publish book or article excerpts. "Digest" is a term used for periodicals that have abbreviated or shortened articles. Scholars prefer to see the original when doing actual research. To keep up with the latest research, digests are used as a finding tool. Biology Digest is used by scholars to know the latest developments in biology generally. If an article in a digest looks useful, see footnotes or headings to find where the original appeared. Interlibrary loan may give you access to the original.

Value of periodicals

Periodicals contain information that cannot be found in print any other place. Events are easily found in the periodicals published at the time of the event. World Series, Super Bowl, Olympics, elections and other events that occur seasonally may be found quicker by going to periodicals for the dates than using an index. Disasters like airplane crashes, floods, fires, earthquakes, hurricanes, and volcanoes are also covered by news periodicals. Opinions and  polls give insight into the politics and social life of the decades. Fashions, essays, biographies, and reviews of books, movies, music, and plays reflect the values of the time period. Trends in business and economy are popular features. Statistics, prices, charts and graphs are common in research reports. Consumers obtain travel information, maps, product evaluations, and changes in laws and regulations from periodicals. How to do it information in periodicals covers recipes, patterns, and repair instructions. Pictures of people, places,  and things are found in periodicals.

Arrangement

Libraries use call numbers to place their periodicals by subject with books. Only a few of our periodicals appear with general book collections. The main call number areas here for periodicals in our general collection are:
 BX 8601 for older copies of periodicals published by the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints
 C-F for genealogical and historical periodicals
 Government periodicals for the State of Idaho use call numbers for subjects.
 Federal government periodicals are arranged by issuing agency or SuDoc call number.
The vast majority of our periodicals are arranged alphabetically. Alphabetical order requires some rules.
The unit on catalog has examples of rules @ catalog.htm#Arules
    Titles starting with Journal of may be filed under the name of the organization at other libraries.
        American Medical Association, Journal of.
The library's list of periodicals or serials list may have cross-reference to direct you to "see" a different version of the name.
Periodicals have frequent title changes. Librarians may handle problems that result from title changes in a number of ways.
Catalog entries may link records for the different titles that a publication has had. With subject arrangement the problems are not as great for shelving materials as they have frequent title changes. The call number remains similar and so changed titles are often next to the old title. State publications use call numbers that usually put changed titles next to each other. Idaho Wildlife Review (GDS SK 387.A26) was published from 1948 to 1977 and was followed in 1978 by the title Idaho Wildlife (SK387.I33).
Preceding and succeeding entries are made in the catalog to link periodical titles.
             Preceding titles are those given to earlier copies.
MARC record shows "780      0 0           $a Idaho wildlife review $x 0019-1248"
             Succeeding titles are later titles for the same material.
MARC record shows "785      1 0           $a Idaho wildlife $x 8755-2469"
             Notes  may show varying titles by which a publication is known.
The unique number assigned to serials, ISSN or International Standard Serial Number appears on MARC records and is often searched by workers in Technical Services and Interlibrary Loans. Inside the cover of the magazine the eight digit number may be found with other publication addresses and numbers. Idaho Wildlife has  8755-2469 as its ISSN.

A certain number of issues are grouped into volumes. Libraries often bind periodicals that they wish to keep for many years.
We bind only those which have local interest beyond the current time. The Ensign, New Era, and Friend are bound since 1970 when they began. Relief Society Magazine, Improvement Era, Instructor, and Children's Friend are the preceding titles before 1970.

PERIODICAL INDEXES

"Index" comes from a Latin word which is translated "to point out." The index of a book points to the pages that discuss a person, place, or subject. The library catalog acts as an index to the materials in the library. An index has more detailed descriptions than catalogs usually. Our catalog does not give the detail that indexes to books and periodicals give.

Great ideas and tools often originate with students. William Frederick Poole was a student. He discovered that periodicals had a wealth of information He created his own index to periodicals. Other students used the index until it was almost worn out.
In 1848 he published his index. Colleges and scholars purchased the index.

Demand for new editions grew and publishing companies became involved.

    Choosing an index

    When deciding which index to use, look at the nature of your subject.
    Would a  popular magazine, such as Newsweek, cover the subject?
  • Reader's Guide to Periodical Literature indexes popular magazines.

    • Look at the nature of your research.
    Are you trying to write a scholarly paper?
    An index for the field of study would have more scholarly articles.

  • Education Index covers scholarly periodicals for educators.

    • Use the library catalog.
    The catalog has subject headings for "Periodicals -- Indexes."
    A subject keyword search will give titles of indexes.

    Look at the time period when articles would be written on the subject.

  • Information on the Teton Dam failure in Rexburg, Idaho would be found in the indexes of 1976.
  • Information on the Olympics appears every four years.

    • Choose annual or quarterly cumulative index to save time.
    Paperback indexes may be published every two weeks with quarterly and annual cumulative issues.
    "Cumulative" means that all the information for the year or quarter is combined in one book.
    The new cumulative index replaces previous indexes.
    I start with the biggest paperback.
    Search only biweekly issues which are not yet replaced by a cumulation.

    Choose computer technology to search several years at a single time.
    Internet databases and CD ROM indexes can help you determine if a topic is too broad or too narrow.
    Searches halt when a certain number of items have been retrieved.
    When a search reaches the default, narrow the search by adding words or choosing more specific words.

    Going directly to unindexed periodicals may be more efficient. This month's home or visiting teaching messages do not need an index as much as the table of contents for the Ensign. Conference talks may be more efficiently found on the Internet. Internet versions of publications often have an "archive or back file." These electronic versions may have search options. The Official Church Home Page http://www.lds.org has a search options which cover 30 years of periodicals and the auxiliary manuals.

    General search strategies

    If too few periodical articles are retrieved:
    • use help screens or tutorials to improve searches
    • change the words used
      • eliminate words
      • search words separately rather than as a phrase.
      • look for cross-references for better search words.
    • try a subject search or other options
    • try more databases including back files
    • use more sophisticated symbols or searches
      • expand the search by using OR as a Boolean operator
      • use truncation symbols
        • Asterisk (*) or question mark (?) allows searches of abbreviated words
        • Comput* would search any word that starts with those same 6 letters
    If too many periodical articles are retrieved:
    • use help screens or tutorials to improve searches
    • look for subdivisions
    • look for more specific words or cross-references
    • add keywords to narrow search
    • try advanced searches
      • restrict the keywords to subject or titles
      • AND is the Boolean operator to require all keywords to be found
      • NOT is a Boolean operator to exclude words
      • ADJ is an abbreviation that requires words to be adjacent
    Boolean operator is a term used to describe words that create a set or group of results.
    Internet may use a plus (+) sign or a minus (-) sign as Boolean operators.
