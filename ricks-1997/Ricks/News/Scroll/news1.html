America meets the Euro Answering the basic questions of the new worldwide currency
By Sara Yates, Scroll Staff




by Jan Pettersson Scroll Photo illustration

What is it and who is involved?

Jan. 1, 1999, marked the day of a new unified Europe. For the first time in history, the majority of European countries will be using a common currency.

Eleven countries, including Austria, Belgium, Finland, France, Germany, Ireland, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Portugal and Spain, came together and eliminated foreign exchange.

The euro, about $1.20 U.S., will exist only electronically until Jan. 1, 2002, when Euro notes and coins will enter circulation. Retailers and banks will start the seven-month transaction of getting the European currency into the hands of its spenders.

Francs, lire, Deutsche marks and other member states' local currencies will be museum relics by July 1, 2002, according to an article in Newsweek.

Who thought of it first?

All countries of the European Union, except Britain and Denmark, agreed to a single currency upon signing the Maastricht Treaty in 1992, according to "A People's Europe" campaign headed by Denzil Davies, Alan Simpson and Llew Smith. John Major of Britain chose not to participate in the treaty provisions so the government would have to obtain consent of Parliament before joining.

The only conditions are known and referred to as "convergence criteria."

These conditions, according to "A People's Europe" campaign, require: 1) low inflation, probably between 2 and 3 percent 2) the public debt of a country must not be more than 60 percent of that country's Gross Domestic Product 3) the government borrowing deficit of each country must not be more than 3 percent of that country's Gross Domestic Product.

How will it happen?

The only plausible way for the euro to work would be to get all countries incorporating it to have similar numbers when it comes to economy issues: stock, stability in currency, deficit spending, etc.

It would be comparable to trying to get Texas and Virginia to grow at relatively the same rate in the United States.

In order to accomplish this, the public cost, or expenditure, has to be cut to reduce the borrowing deficit from 6 to 3 percent. This means public cost would have to be reduced by about 18 billion pounds.

That kind of cut could devastate public services. The cut would be equal to the total education budget for all primary and secondary schools; or more than the budget for law and order and the fire services.

Since this is unfeasible to most countries, the alternative is personal taxation or a combination of tax increases. The standard rate or taxation would increase between 17.5 to 24.5 percent to account for all 18 billion pounds.

How will it affect me?

While the euro may not directly affect the day-to-day living of residents of Rexburg, it will affect many, probably many more than one might suppose.

Think of how many missionaries you know in European countries or those vacationing in one of the "Euro 11." How will it affect students that study abroad, or if you belong to a military family that might get stationed outside the United States.

Ricks College may be affected with a "devalue in the exchange rate," but that will not be determined for quite some time according to the Dean's office and head of International students.

There is no question that the euro will rival the dollar. The United States currency is stable only because so many people within and without the country rely upon it. Countries around the world hold stock and do business with the dollar.

Having the euro will stabilize the European countries and many might start trading and doing business with the euro instead.

"The euro will be to the dollar what Airbus became to Boeing," Norbert Walter, chief economist of Deutsche Bank in Frankfurt said. This means the Euro will bring new competition to the U.S. dollar and may equal changes in exchange rates.
