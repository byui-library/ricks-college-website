BYU-Idaho introduces a new style guide focusing on school unity
It’s all about the look

by Megan Perkins
PER00008@BYUI.EDU
Scroll Staff

The Public Relations Department of BYU-Idaho is encouraging the implementation of a new style guide, Merv Brown, team leader over University Communication and creator of the style guide, said.

The style guide is “the whole identity for the institution,” Brown said. It is a reference tool that gives directions on how and when to use different forms of communication such as logos, typography and trademarks to create a unified and clear identity for BYU-I.

“[The style guide] is to create one clear message to the community at large,” Brown said. “That image needs to communicate what we are all about — the student.”

The creation of an image or “brand” for the university is something that is especially important in this time of transition, Brown said.

“As we become [a four-year university], we want to make sure that we send out a consistent message across the board,” he said.

‘[Using the style guide is about] coming together as a ‘we’ rather than individual ‘me’s,’” he said.

The Public Relations department has been working on the style guide since September; it was released in January, Brown said.

Recently, Brown has been working with faculty to train them in proper use of the style guide. He hopes to convey to all faculty the importance of using the style guide in printed material.

There are three different identities that the style guide creates for the university. Anything that is published in association with BYU-I will have the look of the specific identity it falls under.

The first is the Official Identity, which was developed to “reflect a dignified and scholarly image” and “should be used on all formal communication pieces representing the university in an official capacity,” according to the style guide.

The Official Identity is best suited for official university communication. Examples of this type of communication are letterheads, envelopes, business cards, memos, division and department logos, fax cover sheets and other university communication of the official nature.

The second identity is the Promotional Identity, which is used when a more relaxed yet compelling message is needed.

“The main philosophy behind the promotional identity is a focus on students,” according to the style guide.

The Promotional Identity is best suited for brochures and flyers, catalogs, directories, advertising campaigns, banners and posters, billboards, slides and media, direct mail campaigns and the Internet.

The third identity is the Special Identity. This identity is used for group events and promotions that may need an individual identity but still belong under the BYU-I umbrella.

A printed copy of the style guide has been given to every organization on campus and can be downloaded from the Internet at www.byui.edu/style, Brown said.

The style guide is continually being updated. It is in the process of implementation campus-wide.
The Internet Services Team and Instructional Technology Center is currently involved in updating the BYU-I Web page to fit the new style guide.

“This is what they call a branding, so the Web site will actually match up with the university publications,” Bob Reed, Internet Services manager, said.

The Internet Services Team is currently working on two specific projects regarding the style guide. The first is writing a Web section of the style guide to actually be put into the style guide handbook. The second is developing tutorials to enable people to apply the BYU-I style to average pages.

More than 300,000 Web pages need to put into the BYU-I style, Reed said. It has taken them three to four months to get to this point.

The BYU-I home page is scheduled to be completed by Fall 2002.
