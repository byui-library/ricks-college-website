Compact Discs

 

Most individuals in the 30 something and under crowd are familiar with CD's. Those same individuals may mention such things as computers and music as being related to the term CD, and they would be right. However, while CD's are firmly entrenched in both the music and computer realm, they continue to grow in both the entertainment and multimedia fields as well.

Compact disc (Note that the "c" at the end of disc is usually used when referring to optical media, while a "k" is usually included when magnetic media is involved.) technology is relatively new. In the 1970's, Laser discs attempted to enter the media market. They met with limited success, and disappeared. Then, in the late 1980's, compact discs debuted, first in the music, and then in the computer market. Their success was nothing short of phenomenal. Nearly overnight they replaced the vinyl record, and made dramatic inroads in the cassette tape market. Today, many computer programs, and virtually all multimedia programs are sold in CD format.

Strengths of Compact Discs

There are a number of reasons that point to the success of compact discs.

Audio Superiority

CD's are capable of 16 bit, stereo sound. Something that vinyl records and cassettes never were capable of.

Storage Capability

A single CD can easily hold over an hour of high quality stereo sound, or over 170,000 pages of text, or nearly 1,000 graphics, or 30 minutes of medium resolution graphics.

In comparison, a high density floppy disk can hold only 350 pages of text or 30 seconds of sound.

Durability


The surface where data is stored is never touched by anything other than light. This lack of a friction causing reading medium (like a phonograph needle) means that the disc is never worn.

Additionally, the hardened plastic used to construct the CD, and the protective cases provided mean that, with proper care, a CD is virtually indestructible.

 

Types of Compact Discs

Audio
As mentioned earlier, CD's allow audio to be stored in 16-bit, stereo sound. The sound is clearer, richer, and contains less "noise" than found in any other medium used for audio. Because of the optical nature of the medium, and the use of "oversampling" by the drives, the sound remains pure even if scratches exist on the CD. Audio CD's can be played on any CD-ROM drive, providing the computer has audio CD drivers.

Photo
Photo CD's were developed by the Kodak company. Photographs can be stored on a CD in multiple resolutions. These images can then be viewed on monitors or imported into presentation or multimedia software. Images stored on CD's are superior to all images scanned except by the very best of scanners. Photo CD's are playable on any CD-ROM drive that is "multisession" capable.

CD-ROM

Literally, Compact Disc - Read Only Memory, means that whatever is stored on the disc cannot be altered while on the disc. Also, nothing can be added to the disc. It is "read only." The term CD-ROM is usually used to refer to those discs that contain computer applications or resources.

 

Compact Disc Drives

If you browse computer catalogs, or computer advertisements, you will undoubtedly notice numbers attached to the descriptions of CD drives. These numbers refer to things such as seek time and transfer rate. The descriptions below will attempt to help you understand what is being referred to by these terms and the numbers used to refer to them.

Seek Time

This refers to how long it takes the laser beam (used to read the disc) to move to the spot on the disc where the data is stored. In all cases, this speed is measured in milliseconds (thousandths of a second). In every case, CD drives are many times slower than hard drives. The very fastest CD drives have seek times measured in the 150-170 millisecond range. A typical hard drive will perform the same function in less than 30 milliseconds.

Transfer Rate

Transfer rate refers to how fast the data can be read from the CD and sent to the microprocessor in the computer. It is the transfer rate that is commonly used to measure the type and speed of a CD drive. These designations and transfer rates are demonstrated below:

Double speed 2x 300 kilobytes/second
Triple speed 3x 450 kilobytes/second

As with seek time, transfer rate is far slower than that of a typical hard drive, which may have a typical transfer rate of 3 megabytes per second or faster.

Remember your metric numbering system! Kilo = Thousands. Mega = Millions. So a fast CD drive is still at least 300% slower than an average hard drive.
