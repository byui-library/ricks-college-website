Effectively Diagnosing and Treating the Attention Deficit Disorder

    Attention Deficit Disorder (ADD) is a disorder in the brain which causes a chemical imbalance. Even though there is much about this disorder that physicians know there is still much to learn. But what physicians do know is ADD causes such symptoms as hyperactivity, inattentiveness, easy distractability, ability to become upset or excited very easily, and even aggressiveness or negative behavior. (Pellok 443) These behaviors can vary in severity depending on the individual. Although people of all ages are prone to have ADD, children, ranging from grade school to adolescence, are at the forefront, reports the Education Department. Though there has not been a standardized way of diagnosing ADD effectively there have been medications, such as Ritalin, that have been produced to help counteract ADD. The way these medications help counteract ADD is they filter out all or most of the unwanted stimuli in the brain, which causes the individual to concentrate better and simply "sit still." Ritalin does however have some minor side-effects, some of which include: nervousness, aching joints, loss of appetite, changes in heart rate, nausea dizziness and others. (Hales 62)

    However, with this general information available about ADD and its medications, that the studies have shown the number of ADD diagnoses given in the past four years have skyrocketed and Ritalin production has gone up 450%. (Armstrong 424) There has also been a one to two percent increase in the number of adults diagnosed with ADD, which is between 1.5 to 2.5 million Americans. (Miami 1A) The recent statistics show that there has been a drastic increase in the number of people who are diagnosed with ADD. In addition to this, studies have proven that there is an ever-growing popularity in the diagnosing of this disorder. Children's parents and teachers are more often noticing inattentiveness, and concluding that the child is hyperactive. Because of such observations they take their child into the physicians office and have them checked for ADD. The results of these office visits are obvious when considering the production level of Ritalin in the past 4 years, which has been 450% more than years previous. (Armstrong 425) These numbers may suggest that there is a large amount of "over" prescribing. There needs to be a better, more effective way to test an individual for ADD, before medications such as Ritalin are prescribed.

Prescribing and Diagnosing of ADD

    Recently, studies have shown that there has been between sixty to ninety percent of the children who have been diagnosed with ADD have been given Ritalin and other similar drugs as treatment (Miami 1A). There are those who think that since Ritalin works it should be used whenever ADD shows up, to stop it. One problem with diagnosing ADD so readily is many children diagnosed with ADD while young, are later found to have lost the symptoms. Another problem with a generalized method of diagnosing ADD, is that the severity is overlooked and Ritalin prescriptions are too frequently handed out.

    Doctors and parents need to recognize that taking care of the problem and diagnosing isn't that easy. There should be more to diagnosing, so the diagnosis focuses on those who need the medication. There needs to be a separation of those with severe ADD from children with less severe focusing and hyperactivity problems. This latter group may not require Ritalin, but can be treated using non-medicated methods. There are other treatments depending on severity of the individual. For example, some child may need more attention after class, and others may need help staying interested in what is going on. (Slovut 1E)

    Dr. Mark Stein, who runs a clinic for people who have ADD at the University of Chicago, explains that Ritalin and other drugs should not be prescribed because they are the "easiest way to go." (Miami 1A) He later states that the real problem with the rise in cases and medications is because of the generality of the evaluations. He also says that the kind of evaluations which are performed to diagnose those who might have the disorder are subjective and "comprehensive evaluations are rarely done." (Miami 1A)

    Along with this Thomas Armstrong, who received his Masters Degree in Special Education, also questions the type of testing that Doctors use to diagnose ADD. He relates to us that one of the most widely used assessment test has been the behavior rating scales. (Armstrong 425) Armstrong argues that the assessments are subjective, or require too much opinion from the individuals doing the assessing (teacher, parents, doctors). With these types of "subjective assessments" there is an important problem. Those parents and teachers doing the grading might make false assumptions, because their diagnosis' are based totally on opinions. In many cases, their motives underlying these assumptions are, to simply get the disturbing child out of the one teachers hands to another, or to keep the child quite at home. By connecting the child's behavior with Ritalin the surfaced problem gets taken care of, however, the deeper problem is overlooked. For example, in many cases the real problems why students don't pay attention or act-up is because they don't understand something and feel lost so they feel the easiest way to deal with this is to give up, and cause some commotion to try and make-up for the lacking attention.

    To counteract this diagnosing problem, Armstrong gives an effective idea for testing to find these real underlying problems. He suggest a more "objective style" test, where grading is from one to five. He suggest that numbers, be applied to such words a fidgety. With this number scale, the person doing the assessing will be able to tell how much the child is fidgeting and when. This type of assessment will also enable the teacher or parent to see when the child is getting out of hand. Perhaps the child is being disruptive because of an assignment that isn't interesting, or because the child has to do something they don't want to. (Armstrong 425) This type of assessment seems to prove why the child acts the way they act, using logic and fact. After the assessor looks at the child's assessment, maybe after a few days, they can find patterns of when the child is acting up, and then start treating the problem areas. In most cases this type of diagnosing can identify the problem and use treatment without using Ritalin and other medications.

    Armstrong has another argument against these subjective, behavior rating scales. He feels that those who are making the diagnoses don't consider what the hyperactive candidates being compared to.(Armstrong 425) These opinions of what the "proper" child needs to act like are completely up to the assessor. These opinions, which many feel are unfounded because they are imaginary standards, serve as the testing ground for those who are to be diagnosed with ADD. As a cause of these imaginary standards and opinions of these assessors, more children are being tested for ADD. Because the assessor feels the child may not be focusing as well as they should in some areas, or be disruptive in other areas. But the child is, in many cases, not interested or just feels lost. And to a large degree this is why we have many children being diagnosed everyday with ADD.

    It is no wonder Ritalin production has been up 450% in the past four years, and the numbers will continue to rise if this keeps going on.

    To give a real life case of "over" diagnosing with this "quick-fix" flagrant attitude, Robert Reid, an Associate Professor of Special Education at the University of Nebraska, was performing studies on ADD in different towns. One town in particular he noticed that after a doctor had recently moved into this town there was a very large increase in the number of cases of children with ADD. (Miami 1A) Surely the children didn't become hyperactive around the doctor, but it was most likely his "opinions" that lead to the assumptions. Professor Reid later stated "the problem is there is no objective diagnosis. It is all subjective."

    Along with the proper diagnosis of an individual there are considerations for this behavior which aren't related to ADD, but will in many cause symptoms like ADD to pop-up. Dr. Mark Stein, a psychologist, says that teachers, doctors and parents need to make sure that the child isn't suffering from a hearing problem, problems at home, or any sort of other learning disability, aren't the cause of the child's misbehavior or inattentiveness. (Miami 1A) An example that effectively illustrates this point is when a child went into a physicians office to be checked for ADD. After 5 minutes he diagnosed the child with having ADD. Shortly thereafter the child was discovered as having borderline mental retardation, by another physician. It is correct here to say this was an example of the "Quick-Fix" attitude. Luckily, the parents did get a second opinion.

Other Methods of Counteracting ADD

    There have been different proven methods and scenarios in which the child diagnosed with ADD will drop all or most symptoms. The first of these methods is being a "one on one" setting with the child. It has been reported that 80% of the children that have been diagnosed with ADD have lost all symptoms while sitting one on one with the physician. Furthermore, the child will lose the symptoms especially when interacting one on one with their father. (Armstrong 425) In one case a young man who has been diagnosed with ADD experienced such an effect. He noticed how his actions changed when his father would come home from work. He would act better around his father because his father was, in his opinion, much more authoritative than was his mother. He noticed that his father, as well as most fathers, had a much lower tolerance for misbehavior. He later stated, "I knew that if I acted up around my father that he wouldn't be as easy going as my mother, and I could expect punishment shortly thereafter if I didn't settle down." (Ray 91) This method of being "one on one" has also proved beneficial in the school setting. With this method in mind, if a teacher were to give a child more attention after class, the teacher could sit down and give the student their full, "one on one" attention. The student's focus will improve, and there also be a higher retention level, while helping the child and the teacher understand one another better.

    Another method takes place when the student is working on assignments or activities where they have a high level of interest in the activity. (Armstrong 424) In this scenario a young man, who had ADD, told of how effective this type of treatment was in many cases. He related that if something caught his interest he wanted to learn all he could about it so he could better understand it himself. (Ray 92) This type of activity involves using simultaneously, more than one of the five senses. Of coarse this would take creativity on the teachers part but it has proven to be effective.

    Another scenario where a child's symptoms don't show up is when they are being paid to work or "assess their attention." (Armstrong 424) This method has proved effective because the child is given a greater motivation or incentive to do the work. This method has proven true in an individuals case where he said he didn't feel that it was really work because there was an incentive for focusing on a task and doing right. He mentioned how the incentive of money, made working hard and doing the job right much more enjoyable and tolerable. Because at that time the purpose was made more enjoyable with the money, other than just doing the job by itself. (Ray 91) Here he also mentioned the incentive of getting a raise or other reward if he worked hard enough. As this method has proved for many, the rewards for most tasks will be a great motivator to the individual. This method also causes the individual to channel their attention in one direction effectively.

    In these past four years there has definitely been a drastic increase, of up to 450%, in the production of Ritalin and in the diagnosing of ADD.(Armstrong 425) The studies also show that the types of tests which are performed are simply subjective, or "opinion based," not "objective," based on fact or reason.(Miami 1A) And with this, these assessments are also lacking in their ability to differentiate the severity in an individual. These testing standards need to be raised to a higher, more effective level where the tests are more objective. With the objective style test the behavior of the individual can be traced to certain times and during certain activities throughout the day, while also rating the severity of the behavior. (Armstrong 426) After these assessments have been made then parents, teachers and doctors should decide on the type of treatment, medicated or nonmedicated. If the nonmedicated treatment is used then the parents teachers and doctors can decide if the child needs help after class, needs more time "one on one," and even a simple job to that will help focus the attention of the child.(Armstrong 424) Even though Ritalin is an effective drug, and it has the potential to help those who need it, its consumption should not be to the extent in which it has been for these last four years. Studies show it to be 450% more than years previous. However, there are many who benefit using the alternative methods for dealing with this disorder. And as much as Ritalin has helped those who truly need it, these nonmedicated methods have proved to help many counteract their ADD without the "Quick-Fix."(Armstrong 426)
