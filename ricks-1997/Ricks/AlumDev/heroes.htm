Gladys Walz and Grace Walz Jackson

Through careful planning and the smart investment of their earnings, two school teachers arranged for a significant gift to Ricks College through their jointly prepared wills.

Gladys M. Walz and Grace Walz Jackson were sisters born in Burton, ID, a rural area west of Rexburg. Both attended Ricks College and went on to receive master's degrees from Columbia University. They, in fact,  provided service to those around them in many levels of education throughout their lives-and still do through the endowment they established.

These two remarkable women have been described by relatives and others close to them as beautiful, well-read, humorous, frugal, independent, well-traveled, women of accomplishment and exquisite taste. They both loved the theater and were active in professional and civic circles. "Those women were businesswomen as well as teachers," their niece, Beverly Ricks says, "They invested well and they lived well."

Neither had any children and only Grace married later in life. As a result, they entered into a somewhat unique arrangement by preparing joint wills. Their's, however, was not unlike wills married couples prepare. In doing this, each provided for the other at the death of one, followed by modest bequests to nieces, nephews and various favorite charities after the second passed away. Ricks College was designated to receive the remainder- and bulk-of the assets for the establishment of an endowment to provide scholarships for students studying in the elementary education program. It remains the largest endowment of its kind at Ricks.

The Elementary Education Department Chair, Linda Lloyd states, "The Walz endowment enables us to award scholarships that are based on leadership skills, service rendered to the community, and ability in working with children, instead of just looking at their GPA. The scholarships we are able to provide through their endowment also helps our students tremendously because some are able to continue their education instead of stopping to earn money."

Through their forethought and generosity, Gladys and Grace have perpetuated their dedication to education. Their influence and passion for education continues to be felt beyond their lifetimes, not only by their many great-nieces and nephews now, but also the students of Ricks College. Many more future teachers are being taught because of the legacy Gladys and Grace left behind.

Forest & Doris Stonemets

Forest came from a poor family in Oklahoma and worked hard after moving to Colorado as a very young man. He said that one day during the Great Depression, while digging trenches for a footing on a home, he stopped to wipe his brow, and his boss hit him with a shovel and told him to get back to work. He decided, then and there, that would never happen again and he created a "go to hell fund."

From then on, he would invest a certain amount from each paycheck he received. With his earnings he bought up farms and started several small businesses. He always worked hard and long hours. One of the businesses he started, distributing the local newspaper evolved into him being employed with that newspaper. As part of his reward for being such a hard worker the owner gave him stock. When Gannett bought the paper the stock split several times.

He met an LDS Foundation representative when he decided to gift much of his estate to the building of the Lord’s kingdom. Always interested in others and helping individuals he decided he ask the representative how he could help the students of the Church and his 40 year old niece, who needed a retirement program. He discovered the Deferred Gift Annuity, he could set up an annuity with the Church, which would start paying her an income when she was 60 years old and until that time the funds invested would be accumulating, tax free, for his niece. After her needs were fulfilled the remaining funds would go for scholarships to help needy students at Ricks College.

Marion and Edith Woodmansee

Until recently, the Woodmansees continued the burdensome duties of managing rental properties. Brother Woodmansee says, "When you get to be 76 or 77 years old you really don’t have any business crawling around on roofs and under houses. I think it’s time to slow down and relax and enjoy life. I think stress shortens people’s lives. Now we’re not tied down with properties and various other things; I can putt around the garage and the garden, and enjoy life."

With the help of representatives from the Ricks College Development Office, the Woodmansees recently set up a charitable trust to bless the lives of students at Ricks. After gifting their rental properties to the trust, Brother Woodmansee sold them. "I avoided both capital gains and the state taxes," he explains. "As far as I’m concerned, it’s a win-win-win situation—for us, because we now have an annual income pretty near what we had before; for our heirs, because they’ll be saved the concern of dealing with maintenance of the properties; and in the end, the students at Ricks will benefit." He then adds with a smile, "I don’t know when that will be though; I come from a line of folks who live long lives."

While the Woodmansees enjoy the control they have maintained as trustee, they are pleased with the peace of mind they now have, knowing all the good that comes from their trust. In the end, as he puts it, "It all belongs to the Lord anyway. I feel like I’m taking care of my family and returning it to the Lord at the same time."
