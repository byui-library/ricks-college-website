Secluded poet’s writing published after death

by Rebecca Long
LON01004@BYUI.EDU
Campus editor

Emily Dickinson dedicated her life to writing. Arttoday.com
Emily Dickinson, born Dec. 10, 1830, in Amherst, Mass., penned these words, probably from within the seclusion of her room.

Before the age of 30, Dickinson erased herself from the social scene and dedicated her life to writing and a life of being alone.

Literary critics regard her as an inspired and gifted poet. Some suggest that her privacy allowed her to focus more intently on her work.

She broke the rules — using dashes and unusual capitalization, ignoring rhyme scheme and implementing unheard of metaphors — and created a beautiful kind of poetry distinctly her own.

Dickinson never married and was rarely seen by anyone outside of her immediate family. The reasoning behind her painfully secluded life remains a mystery even today.

She did keep contact with several people though, particularly Thomas Wentworth Higginson, through frequent letters.

Although he recognized Dickinson’s literary talent, Higginson advised Dickenson against publishing her work. It is because of this advice that so little of Emily’s poetry was printed before her death. Fewer than 10 of her nearly 1,800 poems were never printed.

Her entire life was spent in Amherst, but her works now span the globe. She died May 15, 1886, at the age of 55.

It was her sister Lavinia that found Emily’s extensive poetry collection after her death and arranged for the works to be published.
