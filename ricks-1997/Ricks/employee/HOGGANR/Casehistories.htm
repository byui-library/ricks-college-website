Landslide (Mass Wasting) Case Histories
Geology 104
Dr. Roger D. Hoggan

Armero, Columbia - 1985

In Columbia a group of active volcanoes lies west of Bogota. One of them, Nevada del Ruiz, has a history of eruptive activity back to 1595. In late 1984, the dormant volcano became active with clouds of ash and steam and continued active through autumn 1985. People in Armero, down valley from the volcano, grew alarmed. Local authorities reassured them even though geologic studies disclosed Armero was built on earlier mud flows. At 3 p.m. on November 13 a technical emergency committee urged that Armero be evacuated but the warning was not heeded. That night the volcano erupted. Water released from melting ice and snow near the summit sent huge waves of muddy debris down the slopes into surrounding valleys. The largest flow moved rapidly toward Armero. Just at 11 p.m., while most citizens were sleeping, a turbulent wall of mud came rushing out of the canyon and covered the city. At least 23,000 people were buried in sulfurous volcanic mud. A city ceased to exist.

Rissa, Norway - 1978

A farmer made a small excavation for a new barn and piled up the excavated material along the shore of a nearby lake. The extra load on quick clay along the edge of the lake started a small slide which increased to more than a kilometer up the valley behind the farm house. Entire buildings were carried intact toward the lake by the fluidized mass traveling at rates up to 20 km/hr or more.

Yungay, Peru - 1970

In 1970 a large earth quake occurred in Peru. A debris avalanche, 80 m high, roared more than 3.5 km down the steep slopes of Mt. Huascaran at speeds of 400 km/hr. The villages of Yungay and Ranvahirca were destroyed and 75,000 people killed.

Aberfan, Wales - 1966

In this coal mining town, the debris left over from mining coal was piled up in large artificial hills around the town. One morning, in October a major slope failure occurred on one of the hills. The hills held back water from the mine operation. The fluidized mass of material engulfed part of the town including an elementary school where 116 children and 5 teachers were killed; 144 people were killed in all.

Gros Ventre Canyon, Wyoming - 1925

Prolonged rains and melting snow started a large debris flow in this canyon near Jackson, Wyoming. A porous sandstone overlying clay layers sloped toward the canyon. An estimated 37 million cubic meters of rock, regolith and organic debris moved rapidly down-slope and created a natural dam on the Gros Ventre River. Two years later the dam failed causing a flash flood through Kelly, Wyoming killing 9 people.

Vaiont Dam, Italy - 1963

The worlds worst dam disaster occurred October 9, 1963 when the slope above the Vaiont reservoir came loose and moved rapidly down slope filling the canyon below with 238 million cubic meters of rock debris. The world=s highest thin arch dam held but the rock infilling displaced the water in the reservoir causing a wave of water 90 m high to spill over the dam and down the canyon. Compressed air from the rapid slope movement blew rocks and water up the far side of the canyon. Approximately 2,600 people lost their lives. Three years prior to the slope failure creep on the slope measured 30 cm/week increasing to 25 cm/day. The slope consisted of limestones and clay layers with open fractures and sinkholes, all inclined toward the reservoir. The reservoir filled and in September prior to the slide heavy rains increased the weight of slope materials.

Rio de Janeiro, Brazil - 1988

This beautiful city of 6 million people is surrounded by granite peaks that have been logged for fuel and lumber and cleared for agriculture. More recently favelas or shanty towns have been built on the steep slopes. Roads have been built to the shacks and the toes of many slopes have been cut off. In February, 12 cm of rain fell in 4 hours resulting in flooding and mud slides that killed 90 people, leaving 3,000 homeless and costing $100 million to rebuild.

Thistle Slide, Utah - 1983

In April 1983 a massive landslide moved across the canyon causing a natural dam 60 m high. The slide backed up the Spanish Fork River and flooded the small town of Thistle, Utah. A major railroad and highway that go up the canyon were also covered. The slide cost $200 million to repair the damage. A preexisting slide in a rock unit called the Arapien Shale was reactivated. High precipitation was recorded prior to the slide. The slide could have been prevented at a cost of $500,000.

Madison Canyon, Montana - 1959

A large 7.5 magnitude earthquake on an August night produced a massive rock slide that collapsed into the Madison River canyon damming up the river. Foliated metamorphic rock with dolomite at the base make up the canyon wall. The rapidly moving mass of rock debris produced a compressed air blast in front of the slide. The natural dam produced a lake 100 m deep until the Corps of Engineers cut a drainage channel through the slide to prevent flash flooding. The slide buried 28 campers. A plaque marks their resting place.

Flood Disasters - Case Histories
Geology 104
Dr. Roger D. Hoggan

Buffalo Creek, West Virginia - 1972

The Buffalo Creek Valley is sinuous and curves around spurs of hard bedrock which cause narrowing of the valley. The narrow valley averages 400 feet in width and is filled with farmhouses, villages, roads and a railroad. The soil is thin and mostly impermeable. On February 26 the rain had been falling for three days resulting in 3.7 inches of rain and streams were flooding at a 10-year level. Early that morning a coal mine retaining dam collapsed and dumped 32 million gallons of water onto a tributary that drops 250 feet in an eighth of a mile. All or parts of 16 mining towns were destroyed as a wall of water 10-20 feet high moved down the narrow valley. One hundred eighteen people died, 500 houses were destroyed and the damage was over $50 million.

Rapid City, South Dakota - 1972

Over ten inches of rain had fallen in 24 hours on Rapid City, a town with over 45,000 inhabitants. Streams were expected to reach a 40-year level. About midnight Canyon Dam washed out above Rapid City. A warning giving 20 minutes to prepare went out over the radio but most people were asleep. Cars, tents and trailers were swept away along Rapid Creek. Mud covered one-fifth of the city and ten percent of the houses were destroyed with damage over $163 million. Over 242 people died in Rapid City and over 500 were missing especially along the camping areas bordering the river. Drinking water was polluted, power failed and most communication was out.

Big Thompson Canyon, Colorado - 1976

Up to 25 cm of rain fell above Loveland, Colorado on the night of July 31. The runoff collected producing a wall of water up to 10 m high flowing down Big Thompson Canyon. Hundreds of residents, campers and tourists were caught in the flood with no warning as boulders and rock debris were carried along in the water. Over 139 people died, many never found, and damage over $35 million resulted as highways, roads, bridges, homes and businesses were affected. Flooding was at a 500-year level.

Teton Dam Failure, Idaho - 1976

About noon on Saturday, June 6 an earth fill dam approximately 10 miles upstream from Rexburg, Idaho gave way releasing 80 billion gallons of water into a narrow canyon which opened into the Snake River Plain. A wall of water 10-15 feet high traveled across fertile fields, through Sugar City and Rexburg where 3,000 homes were destroyed along with businesses, agriculture, railroads, etc. Eleven lives were lost and damage was over $1 billion. Studies disclosed problems with piping through large fractures in the Huckleberry Ridge Tuff which lined the canyon walls as well as compactibility problems with the dirt-fill of the dam. Spring run-off was about 200% of normal, filling the reservoir sooner than expected. A spillway culvert was not operational at the time of the failure. Residents had approximately one hour warning to move to higher ground. The flood was at a 500-year level.

Mississippi River Flooding - 1993

A major climate anomaly that dumped excessive rain in the upper Mississippi Valley during the summer of 1993 will likely be called the flood of the century. The ground was already saturated when over 90 cm of rain fell in 4 months in some areas. 80% of man-made levees lining the river failed and flooding exceeded a 100-year level. Farmland and towns along the river were inundated causing 50 deaths and over $10 billion in damage. Approximately 55,000 square km of flood plain was covered with water that lasted from June to early August. Several towns along the river have decided to relocate on higher ground. Much of the flooding was due to man-made flood levees which restricted the normal flow down the flood plains.

Las Vegas, Nevada - 1975-1980's

Major man-made developments obstructing the natural drainage channels have contributed to flooding problems in Las Vegas. Run-off from surrounding mountains drains through the Las Vegas area to Lake Mead. In 1975 thunderstorm activity sent run-off down a wash near Caesar's Palace where hundreds of cars were damaged and many were carried downstream. Losses were over $5 million. Storms, again, in 1983 and 1984 resulted in tens of millions of dollars in damages. Rapid development in the Las Vegas basin does not allow drainage through the area.

Volcano Disasters - Case Histories
Geology 104
Dr. Roger D. Hoggan

Mt. Vesuvius, Italy - 79 A.D.

The most famous volcanic eruption in recorded history occurred when Italy's Mt. Vesuvius exploded and buried the Roman towns of Pompeii and Herculaneum under mud and hot ash, killing at least 2,000 people.  The volcano had been dormant over 800 years.  

Mt. Tambora, Indonesia - 1815

Mt. Tambora on Indonesia's Sumbawa Island exploded, ejecting 25 cubic miles of volcanic material, the greatest volume of any eruption since ancient times. The explosion caused whirlwinds and tidal waves that killed 12,000 people, and great clouds of dust and ash plunged the island into unrelieved darkness for three days. The enormous quantities of ash thrown into the atmosphere disrupted weather patterns all over the world. In much of the United States, the summer of 1815 was known as "the summer that never was." Snow storms raged in New York in June, and killing frosts scoured the Midwest through the summer.

Krakatoa, Indonesia - 1881

Krakatoa, a volcanic island in Indonesia, blew apart in the greatest and most devastating eruption of modern times. The explosion was heard 2,000 miles away. It blasted a hole in the ocean floor more than 1,100 feet deep and 6.5 miles wide. Islands 50 miles away were showered with fiery boulders weighing as much as 70 pounds. The explosion caused 130-foot tidal waves that wiped out hundreds of villages on nearby islands, including Java and Sumatra. More than 36,000 people drowned. 17.5 cubic miles of volcanic debris were thrown into the air. Dust covered the entire earth for several years, noticeably lowering temperatures everywhere.

Mt. Pelee, West Indies - 1902

Long-dormant Mt. Pelee exploded on the West Indies island of Martinique, blasting dust, steam and gas into the town of Saint-Pierre. The temperature of the cloud was believed to be as high as 1,800º Fahrenheit. Within minutes, the town was a smoldering ruin, and 29,933 people had been killed. Only four residents are known to have survived, among them a convicted murderer who had been imprisoned in a dungeon at the time of the eruption.

Mt. St. Helens, Washington - 1980

Mount St. Helens, in southwestern Washington state, exploded in a cataclysmic eruption, killing an estimated 77 people, ejecting 2 ½ cubic miles of ash and rock, and totally destroying 156 square miles of the most beautiful forests in the country. Thousands of square miles in six states were smothered in ash. Giant mud flows poured down the slopes of the mountain, wiping out rivers and destroying bridges, homes and industrial facilities. Early surveys indicated that the Mount St. Helens eruption of May 18, 1980 will rank as the greatest single disaster in the history of the United States.

Mt. Pinatubo, Philippines - 1991

The combined effects of ash fall, debris flows, lahars and a typhoon resulted in the deaths of 600 people and displacement of 60,000 refugees when a strato volcano, Mt. Pinatubo, erupted in June 1991. Most of the deaths were due to the collapse of building roofs as heavy, wet ash collected up to 30 cm thick. An eruption column 400 km wide and 34 km high put ash into the atmosphere that caused slight global cooling for a year after. Lahar material continues to be reactivated during each year's rainy season which affects villages and population. Many thousands of lives were saved by early prediction of the eruption and evacuation of the inhabitants around the volcano. Clark Air Force base was closed as a consequence of the damage.

Lake Nyos, Cameroon, West Africa - 1986

A rare volcanic disaster occurred in 1986 near the town of Nyos which lies near the base of a volcanic mountain in Cameroon, West Africa. More than 1,200 people were killed along with thousands of animals when a cloud of lethal carbon dioxide was released from the bottom of Lake Nyos which occupies the crater of the volcano. A bubble in the lake bottom released the CO2 which suffocated the inhabitants.
The gas is denser than air and moved down the slopes and through the town. The release of the gas was predicted to occur 20 years later than when it did.  Studies of gas, temperatures and density of the lake were used in the prediction.

Earthquake Disasters - Case Histories
Geology 104
Dr. Roger D. Hoggan

Kourion, Cyprus - 365 A.D.

On July 21, 365 A.D. the Greco-Roman city of Kourion on the Mediterranean island of Cyprus ceased to exist due to an earthquake. The epicenter determined by building collapse studies appears to be 30 miles southwest of the island. Wholesale destruction of buildings including a temple to Apollo indicate an intensity of IX to X on the modified Mercalli Scale which relates to a magnitude 7 on the Richter scale. Simple block buildings that depend on the weight of the structure and mortar between the blocks to sustain them, collapsed like children's blocks. The number who died is probably in the thousands. A tsunami was also generated which impacted other coastal towns in the region.

Basel, Switzerland - 1356

On the night of October 18, 1356 a large earthquake struck Basel, Switzerland caused by movement on a nearby fault. The resulting damage suggests an XI on the intensity scale. Bern, Switzerland, 40 miles to the south, suffered damage to a cathedral and eighty castles within a radius of 25 miles of Basel recorded damages. Approximately 200 to 300 people died and resulting fires burned for several days.

Shansi, China - 1556

Wind deposited loess or silt is common in this area of Northern China. Consequently many people carved their homes into the thick deposits. On the morning of January 23, 1556, an earthquake estimated at 8 on the Richter scale hit the region collapsing the dwellings and causing great landslides. The Chinese Imperial records put the number dead at 830,000. This is the highest number of deaths due to earthquakes in recorded history. Later, in 1920, another quake (8.5 magnitude) struck nearby and another 200,000 died who had lived in the loess caves.

Port Royal, Jamaica - 1692

Approximately 2,500 people died and one third of the city of Port Royal sank into the sea due to earthquake-caused liquefaction on June 7, 1692. This English port was built on a large sand bar enclosing a bay. Buildings prior to the quake tended to settle and suffer damage due to building block separation. The Richter magnitude of the earthquake was probably an 8 based on the destruction described.

New Madrid, Missouri - 1811

An apparent failed plate boundary underlies this region of the Mississippi River Valley. An associated fault movement produced a seismic event that shook the area in December 16, 1811. A tremor with a magnitude 8 or greater resulted that altered the landscape in the area. Some ground surface was lowered as much as 15 feet and created new lakes. Other areas rose up 20 feet. The course of the Mississippi River was changed and part of the town of New Madrid disappeared in the river. Several large aftershocks were felt 400 miles away in Cincinnati, Ohio, 800 miles away in Washington D.C. and buildings were damaged in Louisville, Kentucky. The tremors were felt over 1,000,000 square miles. The resulting death total is not known.

San Francisco - 1906

Lateral movement of 20 feet on the San Andreas fault in California generated an earthquake estimated at 7.5 magnitude on April 18, 1906. Much of San Francisco was leveled as the ground shook for 40 seconds. Liquefaction took the harbor dwellings. Fire due to ruptured gas lines burned out of control for 3 days consuming much of the city. Damaged water lines were unable to supply water to fight the fires so fire lines had to be made by dynamiting buildings. Death estimates as high as 3,000 were recorded and 28,000 buildings were destroyed. This was the greatest loss of life due to an earthquake in U.S. history.

Tokyo, Japan - 1923

An estimated magnitude 8.2 earthquake struck Tokyo September 1, 1923. The epicenter was beneath a bay 50 miles south of the city. A huge conflagration from 134 fires caused a firestorm which sucked everything combustible into the inferno. The engulfing flames burned 40,000 people gathered in one place while a 36 foot high tsunami swept homes, bridges, roads, railroads along the coast. Total deaths rose to 143,000, mostly from the flames. The water supply had been disrupted as in San Francisco.

Chile - 1960

The calm May 21 Saturday morning was disrupted by a 9.5 earthquake measured on the movement magnitude scale near Santiago, Chile. Pressure between two major convergent plates finally gave way, vibrating all the way to the earth's core. Several shocks were felt which caused the land to sink as much as 7 feet and rise as much as 13 feet in different places. Landslides were numerous even causing a natural dam that blocked a major river. A destructive tsunami spread up the coast and across the Pacific Ocean at speeds up to 442 miles/hour. The tsunami reached Hawaii in 15 hours and killed 61 people then and 8 hours later struck the Japanese coast killing 185 more. Total fatalities reached 5,700 and 58,000 homes were destroyed. A volcano in Chile became active and erupted, apparently caused by the earthquake.

Alaska 1964

The Good Friday earthquake occurred in Alaska on March 27, 1964 causing the largest ever to strike North America. It was an 8.5 on the Richter scale and 9.5 on the moment magnitude scale. This quake also vibrated through the earth like a bell. A total of 48,000 square miles of sea floor and land dropped while 60,000 square miles rose. Valdez and Anchorage were hit hardest. Valdez, a port city, was totaled as it was struck by a seiche and later by a tsunami that left 32 dead. Oil fires broke out at Seward, Alaska where large oil storage tanks ruptured. Ground shaking in Anchorage lasted from 30 to 60 seconds causing large buildings to topple and 70 homes built on the Bootlegger clay to slide toward the coast as the clay liquefied. Total dead reached 131. The tsunami moved down the west coast to kill people as far away as Crescent City, California.

Mexico City - 1985

More than 10,000 people died and 3,000 buildings collapsed in Mexico City as an 8.1 Richter magnitude earthquake struck on September 19, 1985. The epicenter was 220 miles away near the west coast where the Cocos plate is subducting. Mexico City, with 20 million people is built on water-saturated lake fill deposits. Shaking was intensified as surface waves (L-waves) hit the saturated deposits. The earthquake was selective in that most of the buildings that collapsed were between 6 and 15 stories high. Apparently the buildings that height resonated with the seismic waves, thus increasing their movement.

Loma Prieta, California - 1989

This earthquake occurred during the World Series game on October 17, 1989 and had its epicenter along the San Andreas Fault south of San Francisco. More than $6 billion in property damage and 67 deaths resulted. Most of the damage was along the soft sediments surrounding San Francisco Bay. A double decker viaduct pancaked on top of commuters below and parts of the Bay Bridge collapsed. The earthquake was predicted to happen 30 years later than it did due to a seismic gap along the fault in that region. It was a 7.1 Richter magnitude earthquake.

Northridge, California - 1994

The largest earthquake in the Los Angeles area of California occurred at 4:30 a.m. Monday, January 17, 1994 on a hidden thrust fault under the San Fernando Valley. The movement magnitude scale 6.1 quake originated from a focus 11 miles below the surface on an undetected fault. In the epicenter at Northridge 13,000 buildings were severely damaged along with 11 major overpasses. Damages were between $13-20 billion. Only 60 people died in the earthquake.

Kobe, Japan - 1995

This city, resting on soft river sediments and reclaimed land in Osaka Bay shook from 10-15 seconds until the fill liquefied. More than 152,000 buildings were destroyed and 5,378 people were killed. Most of the fatalities were in older crowded neighborhoods where wood frame houses predominate. The quake registered 6.9 on the movement scale but destruction was extensive. Elevated railway systems were knocked out and pre 1980 buildings that were constructed before a safer building code existed collapsed or were damaged. The earthquake is the costliest yet at $100 billion. The Pacific Plate subducts under Japan in this region and produced the catastrophic release of seismic energy.
