Course Description

This course will explore the theological philosophy of C.S. Lewis as an insightful writer who defended Christianity as a philosophy of life. The class will address the question: What use is C.S. Lewis to members of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-Day Saints?

Course Purpose

       During this course we will explore Lewis' insightful doctrinal perspectives while considering how these may enable members of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-Day Saints to understand the revealed gospel of Jesus Christ more insightfully. Our particular focus will be to read and study in order to recognize how Lewis' faithful questioning enabled Lewis to discover the significance of the Christian doctrines and principles he explored. Just as Lewis faithfully explored what he had been taught, we may understand the significance of the Revealed Gospel as we learn how to question what we have been taught in faithful ways. The purpose of this course, then, is to explore fundamental principles and doctrines of the Revealed Gospel of Christ through faithful questioning in an effort to come to understand Jesus Christ and how His atonement may be experienced more meaningfully in our lives.

Method of Student Responses

        The method of study in this course will be both challenging and exciting. It will be challenging because most students are not required to read in the manner I hope to teach in this course. It will be exciting, however, as you learn to break old habits of reading in order to gain personal insight into the reading we do.

        The basis of this insightful method of reading is learning how to respond to a particular topic in class by discovering connectedness between the topic and something not directly related to it drawn from the books (and selections) available to you. Many of the connections you make will come from interrelating the Lewis texts you will purchase. But connections will also be made from other books you have read or are reading in your life. For instance while talking about the problem of pain (see Problem of Pain) a student could draw upon Lewis' discussion of faith (in Mere Christianity) to understand how faith enables one to learn needed lessons from pain even though our initial reaction to pain is to ignore it or find some way of eliminating it. It would also be possible to consider the first verse in First Nephi chapter 1 in order to understand what blessings come from affliction and pain if experienced appropriately.

        This method of reading to make connections is largely made up of what, in the Church, you have heard referred to as "cross referencing." In this class we will expand your understanding of cross referencing by doing more than linking similar ideas together. Although the cross referencing we will do may take the form of simple amplification, we will do even more with it than this. We will expand it for the purpose of posing questions not addressed in one particular text, and to draw examples from other sources to illuminate the discussion in any one particular source while exploring perspectives that may challenge Lewis' or our own views of the doctrines we study.

       By the time the course is finished, it is my hope that you will have experienced an integrated introduction to the thinking and commitment of C.S. Lewis to Christianity, while, at the same time, having developed your thinking and commitment to the Revealed Gospel of Jesus Christ as the only genuine philosophy of life there is by which men and women may enjoy true happiness in this life, and eternal joy in the life to come.

Composing a Personal Intellectual Journal

        As I've said, I want you to think seriously about Lewis' Christian philosophy: what he thinks and why he thinks it. In order to do this you've got to commit yourselves to learn to think like Lewis--or at least think with Lewis as you work to understand the way he reasons through the Christian problems and questions he addresses. Discovering and appreciating someone like Lewis in this way, however, is not possible without purposeful, personal writing. And there is more involved in this kind of writing than many students think.

        Writing becomes purposeful and personal only when we record what we're really thinking and feeling in response to something we have read or experienced in some way. Then, by caring sufficiently about ourselves to reflect upon what we wrote, we are able to ask ourselves questions that probe whether we really believe and understand what we wrote and why we believe it. When we rewrite to discover answers to these types of questions, we discover that we don't merely "have thoughts." More importantly, we "hold convictions." Learning to articulate these convictions serves to reveal these conviction forcefully to us . Only in such a revelation can we choose consciously to continue to hold our convictions as they are--or give them up for better ones. The basis for making the best choice for either holding or giving up personal convictions stems from the ability to perceive the best good from all you read, think, write, and do.

        The way I'm proposing we work at developing better convictions is by writing particular kinds of papers. In saying so, you need to understand that in order for you to learn what I'm teaching, you must write quite a bit. But I don't have to read and grade everything you write in order for what you write to be meaningful to your education. If you write for yourselves seriously and committedly while developing your Intellectual Journal (we'll discuss this later), you will get more out of this course because your ability to think, to gain insight through Lewis' writings, and your ability to share the significance of your insights will vastly improve.

        If approached honestly, each entry you write in your Intellectual Journal during this class will enable you to do better on future entries by giving you thoughts and ideas to expand upon. Inasmuch as these entries are different in some ways from the traditional essays you were taught to write in high school, I will instruct you further when assignments are given. But let me caution you: Since each assignment builds on previous assignments, procrastinating doing your best from the outset, will keep you from accomplishing your best work in the long run.

Class Attendance

        You should consider what we cover in class as the sole instruction you will receive to be able to succeed in this class. As you will soon find out, there is no text for you to read to be able to learn how to study Lewis in the way we will explore his ideas during the course. In fact your notes will be the only means of instruction to draw upon when you are writing or otherwise studying for each class. For this reason missing any class will greatly decrease your chances of doing well in this course.

        In addition to physical attendance, decide now to participate actively in class or group discussions in their various forms. Do so to learn both for yourself and to contribute to someone else's learning. The questions you raise or don't raise; the confusions you voice or don't voice; the frustrations or anxieties you share or don't share--all these will effect your own and your classmates' understanding of the material. You cannot decide to be "neutral" in this course. For whatever you do will effect us all for good or for ill.

        Therefore, decide right now that this class will be compellingly interesting, because of your commitment to attend each class prepared and willing to contribute through your questions and comments. Decide not to sit back and "learn" what the teacher has to say--assuming that it's my responsibility to make the course interesting. The truth is the most interesting classes are those in which the teacher and the students commit to honestly doing their best each day, working hard against the tendency to procrastinate their own learning. When students do not procrastinate their learning, they are less likely to keep their thoughts to themselves during class time. Instead they are willing and even anxious to share what they think in relation to what the teacher says or in response to their classmates' opinions. If you will commit to this I can promise you that this class will be one of the best classes you will take this semester. Why? Because you will make it so.

The Role of Error in this Course

        To do well in this course you must be courageous enough to be responsible for your own education. Taking responsibility for your own learning is possible only by taking responsible risks. Responsible risks are taken when an engaged and committed mind is trying to break away from unthinking habits, but has not, as yet, discovered or understood the ideas and principles necessary to accomplish the escape. Risking in this way is likely to produce error. I call it "responsible error."

        Genuine education is based upon responsible error. But it is not to be confused with "irresponsible mistakes." Such mistakes are committed by those who are not engaged in learning much at all, but who go through the motions of completing assignments or contributing to class discussion just so the teacher has something to base a grade upon. Students who make irresponsible mistakes spend their effort and time pretending to be interested in education. The truth is they are here at Ricks (or where ever) to prove they already know the principles or perspectives of the course, or to show they don't have to learn them to get by. This is particularly true for honors students. They are prone to make-do with what they already know, learning how to comfortably ignore the challenges of present possibilities by secretly holding onto instruction, experiences, and opinions that have gotten them the grade in the past.

        Fight this tendency to pretend to learn. Fight this tendency to conform your present life to habitual patterns of thinking, feeling, and writing. This will be challenging to you. It will require you to trust yourself more than you have trusted yourself before. Such trust will enable you to discover who you really are, who you really have been, and who you can become. For this purpose listen in class, and read and write, to discover ideas that are new to you, or ideas you've heard before, ideas that are good, but to which you haven't as yet committed yourself. Write in and out of class to explore what you think, what you feel, and why. As you do, your responsible errors will eventually lead you to genuine knowledge, competency, and confidence. Carl Jung, a noted psychologist, observed: "Knowledge rests not upon truth alone, but upon error also." This is a great truth.

Your Final Grade in the Course

        Your final grade in this course will be determined first by totaling and averaging the grades you receive on exams you take, on class assignments, and on an intellectual journal you will submit at the end of the semester. I will then consider these grades in relation to what I'll explain as your "personal learning pattern." Thus every paper you write is significant. Every journal entry, important. Every exam you take is critical. Don't put off doing your best in whatever we are working on during our course of study.

A Final Thought

Consider these statements on Intellectual Courage:

Intellectual courage is the power and determination to follow loyally and reasonably one's own beliefs and principles, irrespective not only of the disapproval of neighbors [friends, family, or enemies], but also of painful disturbance in one's own mind.

Intellectual courage is undoubtedly the most difficult and, therefore, the rarest of human achievements. Few will seek a new intellectual position. Fewer will maintain it even if reached by honest effort because to do so will usually require the additional courage to reconstitute one's old ideas in the light of new knowledge.

                                            (Quoted in Obert C. Tanner's Christ's Ideals for Living, 55)

        I want you to do a lot of thinking about intellectual courage as we study C.S. Lewis, because intellectual courage is the basis upon which I will teach everything introduced to you during this course. Indeed, intellectual courage is the only basis upon which genuine education can be enjoyed. It is also the only basis upon which honest communication may be shared. This course is dedicated to both. By participating in genuine educational experiences and sharing what we are learning, we learn what we really know and what we need yet to know in order to gain confidence. This is the basis of meaningful writing and study in college and in life. It is the focus of this course. It is education in the highest sense of the word. I am committed to it; I hope you will become committed to it yourselves.

Brother David L. Ward
