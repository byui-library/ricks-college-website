ISRAEL

1. The Coastal Plain.

From Mt. Carmel to the Yarkon River (border of Philistia) and from the Med to the hill country of Samaria is a region of 55 x 10 miles called the Sharon Plain.

Three ridges of hardened red sand run parallel to the coast with the two modern highways in the valleys between them. In ancient times the Sharon Plain contained thick, almost inpentrable oak forests. The ridges of the mountains were a corridor on which many armies of the past have marched and by which large caravans brought trade goods into the area from north and south. These coastal plains are now the most well developed part of Israel

Caesarea

A seaport on the Med. About 30 miles north of Joppa and 70 miles northwest of Jerusalem, built by Herod the Great in 22 BC. It was the capital of the Roman government in Palestine for about 500 years. Named in honor of Caesar Augustus it had lavish palaces, public buildings, a theater, a hippodrome, and an amphitheater. It also a wonderful system of sewers under the city streets.

The Harbor built here was considered a great feat. It was always safe from the sea's storms because of the breakwater constructed by putting down layer after layer of heavy stones into the deep water. A wall and towers, a promenade along the breakwater and dwellings for mariners were also provided.

We have no record of Christ going here, though other Christians were brought here for trial and it became a center of Christian persecution in later years. Herod Agrippa I was "smitten by an angel here (eaten of worms--Acts. 12:19-23), and Titus celebrated the birthday of his brother Domitian by having 2500 Jews fight with beasts in the amphitheater. Titus was the soldier who finally put down the rebellion of the Jews in 70 AD. It is ironic that it was in this same city that they began that rebellion four years earlier. Rabbi Akiva, one of Israels most prominent at the time, was martyred here after Bar Kohkba's abbortive uprising, and many a Jew and Christian were thrown to the lions here in years thereafter.

In the 3rd century AD the Christian scholar, Origen, established his school here. Eusebius, another noted scholar of the Great Apostasy period was bishop of Caesarea. In 548 AD a massacre of Christians was carried out here by Jews and Samaritans. The city passed into Moslem hands in 638 AD and was the scene of another Christian massacre in 1101.

At Caesarea the first archaeolgoical evidence of Pontius Pilate was uncovered when an inscription bearing his name beside that of the emperor Tiberius was found. Another point of interest is that a famous cup known as the sacro cantina (now housed in Genoa, Italy) was found here. It is supposedly the glass "cup" used by Christ at the last supper--the cup of the "Holy Grail."

Megiddo

Since the beginning of the century, several archaeological expeditions have exposed no fewer than 25 strata of civilization--from the 4th millennium BC to the 4th century BC--in the historical layer-cake mound of Megiddo that archaeologists call a tel. The ancient city owed its great importance to its strategic position at the mouth of the pass, the main branch of the international highway between Egypt and Mesopotamia. Pharaohs and Assyrian satraps rushed their armies up and down these passes on their way to attach the other, and Megiddos coveted location made it as much a target for foreign conquest as it was the center of power of its own region.

The ramp up to the flattop mound brings you into the partially restored remains of a Late Canaanite (Late Bronze Age) gate, quite possibly the very one that unsuccessfully defended Megiddo against the Egyptian pharaoh Thutmose III in 1468 BC, as described in his victory stele. Above it is a larger and later gate, almost identical to those found at Hazor, in the north, and Gezer to the south. Long identified with King Solomon (10th century BC)--all three cities were his regional military centers--the gate has been related by some scholars to the time of Ahab, king of Israel a century later.

Evidence of prehistoric habitation on the site has been found as well, but among the earliest remains of the city of Megiddo are a round altar dating from the early bronze age and the outlines of several early bronze age temples, almost 5000 years old, visible in the trench between the two fine lookout points.

The connection to King Ahab (9th century) is strong at Megiddo. Ruins of stables, also once thought to be Solomon's, are now definitively dated to the reign of Ahab, who is known to have had a large chariot army. But nothing at Megiddo, is as impressive as its water system. Before its construction, again apparently by Ahab, the citizens of the town had to leave the safety of their walls to draw water from the subterranean spring. In a masterful stroke, the Israelite engineers sunk a deep shaft and a horizontal tunnel through solid rock to reach the spring from within the city. With this access, the spring could be permanently blocked outside the defensive walls, securing the city's vital water supply. The spring, at the end of the tunnel, is nothing more than a trickle today, perhaps blocked by earthquakes of later centuries. Look for chisel marks and ancient steps hewed from rock as you descend 180 steps through the shaft, traverse the 65-yard-long tunnel under the ancient city wall, and climb up 83 steps at the other end.

The Jezreel valley has so often heard the clash of arms that the very name of the site that commands it has become synonymous with the final cataclysmic battle of all time. In 1918, General Allenby swept up the pass by way of Megiddo to outflank the Ottoman Turks and seal the British victory. He was subsequently knighted, taking as his title viscount of Megiddo.

Nazareth

Nazareth is located about twenty miles west and south of the Sea of Galilee, at an elevation of 1300 ft. Above sea level. The majority of Nazareths modern day inhabitants are Arabs, and most of them are Christians, in keeping with the Christian tradition associated with the town. The Jewish presence is located in upper Nazareth, a modern suburb founded in 1957 as an absorbtion township for new Jewish immigrants. Mt. Tabor is to the east and Mt. Moreh is to the south. Nazareth may have existed by a different name in Old Testament times, but if so, it was disinhabited about the the time of the Assyrian conquest in 732 BC. It probably was not occupied during the Persian period but was resettled in the Hasmonean period. During the Roman period, NT times, the village may have been five or six acres in size, and it was a village of such small size it was often held in derision (John 1:46) The larger city of Sepphoris, four miles to the northwest, was the chief center of Lower Galilee in Jesus' day.

Several different Christian churches have buildings in Nazareth but by far the most impressive is the Catholic Church of the Annunciation. It was built by the Fransciscans in the 1960's, and is the largest Christian Church in the Near East. The facade has reliefs of the angel Gabriel bringing his message to Mary.

Also featured are reliefs of the four gosel writers, Matthew, Mark, Luke and John, and the names of the twelve apostles, with Paul replacing Judas Iscariot.

A sixth century Synagogue is preserved in the old city, and is a reminder of Jesus' confrontation with the Nazarenes of his day in their synagogue.

The Province of Galilee

This is where Jesus spent most of his time as he prepared both his Apostles and the people for who he was, what he was to do and how they were to follow.

In New Testament times it was the most northerly of the three divisions into which Palestine west of the Jordan was divided, and included the territories of Issachar, Naphtali, Zebulun and Asher. The district was about 60 miles by 30. It contained some of the best lands and busiest cities in Palestine, and was thickly populated by a hardy, warlike race. It was crossed by important highways leading to Damascus, Egypt, and estern Palestine. Along these roads caravans would be constantly passing, and such a place as Nazareth would be kept in close touch with what was going on in other parts of the world.

Galilee had an excellent climate, mild near the seacoast, hot in the Jordan valley, and bracing in the highlands. The land was fertile, especially in the plain of Esdraelon, and produced large crops of olives, wheat, barley, and grapes. By the Sea the fisheries were a great source of wealth and provided a large export trade.

On the death of Herod the Great in the year of our Lords birth, his son Antipas became tetrarch of Galilee and reigned until AD 39, when he was banished by the Emperor Caligula.

The area was the first to fall to the Assyrians, from 730BC and some of its population was deported and pagans took their place. Lamartine recalled it this way: "almost always an intelligent eye discovers a secret and profound analogy between the land and the great man, between the scenery and the actor, between nature and the genius formed and inspired by it."

The Sea of Galilee.

Also called the Sea of Chinereth, and the Lake of Gennesaret or Tiberias; 12 ½ miles long, and 7 ½ miles wide at its greatest breadth, it lies 680 feet below sea level; the heat is very great, and the cold air rushing down from the hills often produces sudden storms. The river Jordan flows right through it, from north to south. In our Lord's time there nine cities on the shore of the Lake, including Capernaum, Bethsaida, and Gadara, each with a population of 0ver 15,000 people. The lake and its shores were crowded with busy workers. The best fishing ground was at the north end, and a large quantity of pickled fish was exported to all parts of the Roman Empire.

Tiberias. The town was built on the ruins of an old Jewish cemetery and was founded by Antipas around 19 AD. It is likely that skilled carpenters like Joseph and his son Jesus would have been used on such a site, though many refused to work here because of the defilement that would come through living "among the dead." Early it was shunned by the "non-Romanized" citizens which explains why it is not often mentioned in the Gospels. The move of the Sanhedrin from Yavneh (where it was taken after the destruction of Jerusalem) to Tiberias in 135 AD was the beginning of the first real Jewish presence. Part of the Jewish Talmud was written down here, and it was here also that the Masoretes (Transmission) who were accomplished in the study of the Hebrew Bible, established the vocalization of the biblical text with a new system of punctuation. From the 4th century, the town became of Judaisms four holy cities. It is also famous for its hot springs (upto 140 F) with medicinal properties at Hammat Tiberias to the south of the town. These luxurious establmishments were first built by Antipas to try and get people to settle there. There is a mausoleum of the famous 2nd century Rabbi Meir Ba al Haness (the worker of miracles) located here and further north people venerate the tomb of Rabbi Akiba, the illustrious doctor of the Jewish tradition, who inspired the second Jewish revolt against the Romans in AD 132-35. Moses ben Maimon (Maimonides) the great Jewish philosopher of the Middle ages asked to be buried here, in the very heart of the old town. Legend has it that after his death in Cairo (he had fled spain), his body was put on a camel's back, but when the camel reached Tiberias, it refused to go any further. He was high priest at the time of the first Jewish revolt and obtained permission from Titus to open a religious school at Yavneh, thus preserving Judaism and the study of the law into the distant future.

Capernaum--"Village of Nahum."

A town on the north western shore of the sea of Galilee. At the time of Jesus it was probably one of the most prominent in the district. It was the home of Peter and Andrew and of Matthew. Many of our Lord's miracles were worked here. Capernaum was, according to Matthew "Christs own town" after he was rejected at Nazareth. The lake and the highway known as the Via Maris which ran past the city, assured this border post thriving trade and business. The House of St. Peter, in which Jesus lived while his Apostle was lodged by his in laws at Bethsaida, is now covered by a church with basalt walls which still show the 1st century ruins. One room has walls decorated and surrounded by later constructions, a sign of special attention which proves it was venerated from earliest times of Chrsitianity. About 100 yards from the house is a large white limestone synagogue where the sermon known as the "bread of Life" was purported to have been spoken. The present ruins were built on the foundation of the synagogue of Christ in the 4th century. You can still see the black basalt foundations of the older building. The synagogue featured a columned prayer hall with stepped stone benches along the length of the walls. The columns supported a gabled roof, and possibly a second story gallery around the prayer hall, from which the women could observe the prayer hall service. There was also a courtyard annex attached to the east side of the synagogue. The courtyard, which was probably used for schooling and social events, was open to sky with a column supported, covered portico on the north, east and south. Running the length of the southern exterior of the synagogue and annex was a long porch with steps at the east and west end. The three large doors in the southern wall of the synagogue were its main entrances. Worshippers face south, out of these doors, toward Jerusalem, during prayer.

For the majority of his three year ministry Capernaum was Jesus' headquarters in Galilee, but although many great miracles took place here Jesus pronounced judgement against the city because of the peoples' general unwillingness to repent (Matt. 11:23).

The name Capernaum is derived from the New Testament Greek spelling of the city's Hebrew/Aramaic name, Kfar Nahum, which means the village of Nahum. The site was known for centuries in Arabic as Tell Hum. The Franciscan order acquired the site in 1894 to protect it from the constant looting of its ancient building stones by arabs. Visible remains of Capernaum's limestone synagogue were reburied by the Franciscans to protect them from further destruction. Restoration was first begun in 1920 and 1926. The next (and most recent) restoration was done in 1968.

Bet Shean. (House of Safety)

Bet Shean was an ancient city that occupied a large tel, and later the area below the tel. It is about 350 feet below sea level and is located about twenty miles south of the sea of Galilee on the old road to Jericho and was connected to the Jezreel valley as well. Thus it was of prime military importance and enjoyed one of the longest uninterrupted periods of occupation in the history of the Holy land. In addition, the thriving lower city, which was called Scythopolis, developed at the base of the tel during the Hellenistic and Roman periods and continued through the Byzantine period and well into the Islamic periods.

The city is first mention in the conquest lists of Thutmose III of Egypt (the document was housed in the temple of Karnak at Luxor--1468 BC), the el Amarna letters, the conquests of Seti I and Rameses II (1286 BC), and the attack of Shishak in 925 BC. Because of its strategic position it was of great important to Egypts pharoahs trying to control Canaan.

In Bible history Bet Shean first appears as a site the invading Israelites could not conquer and control during the time of Joshua and the Judges (See Josh. 17:11; Judges 1:27). It was later listed as a city of the Philistines, where the headless bodies of the unfortunate King Saul and his sons, including the heroic Jonathan, were hung on the city walls after their death on Mt. Gilboa, which is located nearby to the southwest (1 Samuel 31:12). During Israelite

times it did not play as prominent role because it was well within the borders.

Sometime during the Hellenistic period (332 BC) the city's population shifted from the tel to the large area at the base of the mound, particularly on the south side. Thereafter, the upper part had few buildings and was home only to a Greek shrine and later a Roman temple dedicated to Zeus. It was then it became know as Scythopolis. The name means the "City of the Scythians" perhaps a reference to ethnic Scythian soldiers who were stationed at the site during the Hellenistic period.

Scythopolis was a thriving independent city during Roman times (63BC-AD 324). It was the largest of ten such cities known as the Decapolis (ten cities) and was there capital. The Decapolis region is mentioned in the New Testament as a place where Jesus' great works gained famed, and from which multitudes traveled to hear him (Matt. 4:25). Scythopolis boasted a theater with a seating capacity of 6000. It is the largest discovered in the holy land. There was also a large oval ampitheater that could seat up to 8000 for sporting events and gladiator contests.

The most impressive remains date from the Byzantine period (AD 324-640) and include a large public bath house which covered an area of 1.5 acres. It featured three saunas (caldaria), numerous water pools for bathing, decorated dressing rooms with benches, and a colonnaded portico (palaestra) for physical activities which surrounded the complex on three sides.

Two wide public streets have also been unearthed. Both were in use during the Roman period, but reached a higher degree of opulence under the Byzantines.

During the Islamic period the city fell into deterioration and was called Beisan (Bet Shean). The luxurious bath houses were converted into stables. In AD 747 an earthquake destroyed much of the remaining beauty of the city and when it was rebuilt during the Abbasid period (AD 750-969) none of the monumental architecture was restored. The declined continued during the periods of the Crusaders and the Mamelukes, until the site was abandoned.

Qumran

Community center of the Essenes who produced the famous Dead Sea Scrolls. They lived in natural caves in the adjoining cliffs, in tents, and in underground chambers cut in the soft marl. They gathered here for all the religious and economic activities of the sect. The well-preserved ruins, located on a little plateau on the north-west shore of the Dead Sea, make it easy to visualize the daily life of these people whose austere dedication excited the admiration of the Romans statesman Pliny the Elder and the Greek orator and philosopher Dio Chysostom.

Late in 1946 Arab boys of the Ta'amireh tribe of the bedouin were exploring caves in the area of Khirbet Qumran (ruin of Qumran). Throwing rocks into one of the caves, they were surprised to ehar the sound of pottery breaking. Upon entering the cave, now known as cave one, they round the rubble of broken jars and other debris on the floor. A few jars were intact. In one of them the boys found three parchment scrolls written in Hebrew dated to the first century BC. The largest of the scrolls was a complete Book of Isaiah, the oldest ever discovered.

Four other scrolls were later found in the rubble on the floor of the care. Later still, tounsands of fragments of other parchment manuscripts would be extracted from other caves in the area, and sold by the bedouin to interested individuals. It was over a year before the scrolls from cave one found their way into the hands of scholars in Jerusalem, but by the spring of 1948 the news was released to the world. They became known as the Dead Sea Scrolls.

The people of the Dead Sea Scrolls have been of special interest to some Latter-day Saints. The Qumran leadership consisted of three priests and twelve laymen, as compared to the LDS First Presidency and Council of the Twelve. The overseer of temporal affairs at Qumran has been equated with the office of a Bishop. There was a heavy interest in the Temple and in the Melchizedek Priesthood, and the sect thought of themselves as receiving new revelation.

Such similarities, however, are largely superficial. The Essenes were very different from the early Christians, and thus from Latter-day Saints. For example, they did not proselyte, marriage was not a priority, their Sabbath laws were more extreme than even the Pharisees, they rendered no care to the poor or the handicapped, and were taught to hate their enemies. There is no indication that the people of Qumran had anything to do with Jesus Christ or his early Church.

Herodion

The remains of Herodion, a unique palace-fortress complex built by Herod the Great around 23 BC, are located about three and a half miles southeast of Bethlehem on the edge of the Judean wilderness. This unusual royal complex was the largest of several which Herod had constructed during his 36 year reign in the Land of Israel. The Greater Herodion covered some 45 acres, making it the third largest in the Roman world, and actually consisted of two separate but connected complexes--the Upper and Lower Herodion.

The lower Herodion was probably Herod's main summer residence aside from his palace in Jerusalem. The palace at the Lower Herodion was over 400 feet long and richly decorated and furnished. Unfortunately not much is left of the structure. On the north side of the palace is curious flat area 1100 feet long but only 80 feet wide, with remains of a monumental building at its western end measuring 45 feet square. The buildings purpose is not clear, but the long, narrow area may have been a course for parading or sporting activities.

The most visible remains at the Lower Herodion belong to the huge pool northwest of the main palace. The man-made pool measures 135 by 210 feet, and is nine feet deep. Although its function as a water storage reservoir is obvious, the pool was also a pleasure lake for swimming and sailing. In the center are the remains of an artificial island 40 feet in diameter. In Herod's day it was a colonnaded pavilion, no doubt used for the entertainment of the king and his family and guests. The island pavilion was probably reached by small sailing boats. Surrounded by colonnaded galleries, formal gardens and orchards, the lower palace and its sumptuous pool must have exhibited a degree of richness and luxury rarely seen, but befitting a monarch of Herod's reputation.

The upper Herodion was a circular palace fortress which sat atop the cone-shaped mountain directly south of the lower main palace. This was a protective complex to which Herod could escape should his reign be threatened while he was at his luxurious summer retreat. Originally there had been two high hills at the sight, but neither was tall enough for Herod's purposes, so he had massive amounts of Stone and soil taken from the top of one to build up the top of the other. Thus the upper third of the Herodion's volcano-like shape was actually man made. The low mound of the supply hill is still visible to the east.

The circular citadel at the top of Herod's new hill measured some 200 feet in diameter, and was protected by four towers at the points of the compass---three semi-circular towers, and one much taller round tower on the east side. The upper portion of the round tower, which no longer exists, must have contained several levels of apartments as well as battle stations. Its mammoth, solid base still bulges into the center of the fortress courtyard, and is the most obvious feature of the excavated ruins.

The cylinder like fortress stood 90 feet high by itself, but the artificial mountaintop was heaped up against its outside wall for htree or four stories, so that only the top third was visible above the summit. The fortress wall was actually a double wall with a 9 foot wide space between the outer and inner walls. This area was divided into five or six interior levels, each with corridors and rooms used for storage of food, supplies and weaponry in case of seige. Soldiers or guests could also be quartered in the rooms of the wall.

Water for the palace-fortress was stored in four large cisterns carved into the solid rock deep within Herodions northeast side. During the first Jewish revolt (AD 66-70) the uppermost cistern was accessed by a narrow tunnel. During the second Jewish revolt (AD 132-135), when the mountain fortress lay largely in ruins, a complex system of tunnels was dug which connected all of the cisterns. These tunnels apparently afforded the Jewish defenders of the mountain, lead by Shimon bar Kohkba, to quickly move troops to different strategic areas on the Herodion's slopes. The cisterns and tunnels have been cleared by modern excavators.

Inside the fortress at the top of the mountain were the royal living quarters. A Roman style bath was built in the northwest quarter, which included a dressing room, a tepidarium (warm room), a caldarium (sauna) and a frigidarium (cold water bath). In the southwest quarter was a triclinium style room probably used as a reception or dining room. This large room was converted to a synagogue 70 years after Herod's death, which Jewish zealots occupied the fortress from AD 66-70 during the First Jewish revolt.

The eastern half of the fortress interior consisted of a colonnaded courtyard and garden 40 feet wide and 107 feet long. Even under seige Herod could enjoy the pleasure of his gardens. At each end of the courtyard was a semi-circular niche, called an exedra, where a statue could stand. The only entrance to the fortress was through a gate just east of the northern exedra. From there a stepped tunnel ran 200 feet through the artificial mountain to its exterior, then another 300 feet down the remainder of the slope. The historian Josephus described the steps as being made from white marble.

Josephus also reports that his own burial be at the Herodion. Dreadul circumstances had lead to Herod's death---bowel pains, venereal disease, dropsy, racking cough, works, and constant itching that drove him to madness. Upon his death at Jericho in 4 BC, the monarch's body was brought to the Herodion on a solid gold bier encrusted with jewels. A magnificent military procession accompanied the body along the way, and interred the dead king according to his wish. But inspite of the exhuastive excavations that have been carried out at the site, neither Herod's tomb nor his remains have ever been found.

Jerusalem. The history of the city stretches back to the third millenium BC, to a time designated by historians as the early bronze age. From that time, around 2500 BC, until the reign of David in 1000BC the city was located on the southern spur, which lies completely outside the modern city wall to the south. After David conquered Jerusalem from the Jebusites he made it the capital of his Israelite Empire. During the reign of Solomon, beginning around 970 BC, the hill north of David's city, Mount Moriah, was annexed to Jerusalem and the Temple of Solomon built there. It was probably sometime after 800 BC before the city began to expand to the hills west of the City of David and Moriah to areas now known as Mt. Zion and the Jewish quarter.

After the fall of Jerusalem to the Babylonians in 586 BC, the city sat desolate for fifty years. Beginning in 537 BC, under the administration of the Persian Empire, Jews began to return and to rebuild Jerusalem. A second temple was built in the same place that Solomon's Temple had stood, and the wall of the city was reconstructed. However, the city was smaller in size than before the Babylonian conquest. Persian period Jerusalem only occupied the City of David and Mount Moriah. It was not until after 300 BC, during the Hellenistic Period, that the city began again to expand to the western hills. Steady growth through the Hasmonean and the early Roman (Herodian) Periods brought the city to a population of possibly 100,000 during New Testament times.

Jewish Jerusalem was destroyed by the Roman legions of Titus in AD 70. It was rebuilt on a much smaller scale as a Roman city during the reign of the Emperor Hadrian (AD 117-138). Hadrian changed the name of the city to Aelia Capitolina, a reflection of his family name--Aelian. It is to this period that the Ecce Homo Arch and the stone pavement under the sisters of Zion Convent belong, as well as the old Roman Gate and Cardo which have been restored at Damascus Gate. Following the second Jewish revolt, also known as the Bar Kokhba Revolt (AD 132-135), Hadrian had banished all Jews from Jerusalem and Judea. He also decreed that the name of the Holy Land be called Palestine, the Greco-Roman name for Philistia, a narrow province on the coastal plain. Prior to Hadrian, the Holy land had been known as "the land of Israel." (Matt. 2:20-21), or by the names of its various provinces, such as Judea, Samaria, and Galilee.

The cities name reverted back to Jerusalem when the Roman Empire adopted Christianity during the time of Constantine (AD 326). Christian shrines such as the Church of the Holy Sepulchrie, the Church of Hagia Sion, the Nea Church and others, became the major landmarks of the city. The city again grew southward toward modern Mt. Zion, and the city was expanded in that direction. The cardo of Hadrian was also extended southward during the Byzantine period, and remnants of that extension are visible in the Jewish quarter today. The Byzantine city was sacked during the Persian invasion of AD 614, then fell to the Muslims in AD 640, when Omar made Islam the ruling force in Palestine.

The Muslim name for Jerusalem is "al Quds" which means "the Holy City." Judeo-Christian personalities such as Abraham, Moses, David, and Jesus were venerated by Muslims as prophets, but the main Islamic shrine in the city was built in honor of Islam's prophet Muhammad. The Dome of the Rock, built between 687 and 691 by order of Caliph Abd al Malik, marks the place of Muhammad's legendary trip into heaven on the back of a winged horse. The rock inside the dome, which is associated by Muslims with Abrahams near-sacrifice of his son Ishmael (as contrasted to the Biblical account with Isaac) is also said to be the spot from which Muhammad rode into heaven. The event was admittedly visionary, since Muhammad never visited the city during his lifetime.

The original Israelite Jerusalem, the City of David, was about 15 acres in size, with a population of maybe 2-3000 people. Solomonic Jerusalem would have been about 32 acres with 4-5000 inhabitants.

Hezekiah's Tunnel. As Jerusalem expanded westward from David's city the extra water of Gihon was needed for use by it's greater population. The warren's shaft system was to small to accomodate the numbers and the Kings Pool was in a difficult location. Furthermore, since the Siloam channel was outside the city walls, the threat of seige made it and the King's pool and unreliable source in time of war. Expecting an attack by the Assyrians, King Hezekiah had a new water system built shortly prior to 701 BC, known today as Hezekiah's tunnel and the Pool of Siloam.

The tunnel is 1750 feet long, and was dug right through the ridge beneath the city of David, out of the limestone which made up the mountain. It is considered one of the great hydro-engineering feats of antiquity. Hezekiahs workers seem to have followed the natural course of fissure in the mountain, through which a small amount of water may have been seen to be seeping. To save time they worked from both ends. A series of sharp turns and false starts in the tunnel alerts the explorer that the meeting point has been reached. The first 65 feet of the tunnel on the Gihon end belongs to the original Jebusite system feeding Warren's shaft. Visitors will note that the ceiling of this first segment is more expertly cut than the floor, indicating the Jebusite tunnel was very shallow, and was made higher by Hezekiah's workmen. An opening in the tunnel wall about shoulder high indicates the point where water once flowed to the collection pool below Warren's shaft, and a sharp turn to the south (left) marks where Hezekiahs workmen began their new tunnel.

Visitors to the tunnel also note the very high ceiling at the western end, which suggests it was originally cut too high for the water to flow, necessitating more work to lower the floor level. At this end, in 1880, a Hebrew inscription was found describing construction of the tunnel. It is called the Siloam inscription and is presently housed in the Istanbul Museum in Turkey (there is a replica in the Israel museum). It says:

"This is the story of the cutting through. While (the stone cutters were swinging their axes, each toward his fellow, and while there were yet three cubits to be pierced through, (they heard) the voice of a man calling to his fellow, for there was a fissure on the right--and on the day of the piercing through, the stone cutters struck through each to meet his fellow, axe against axe. Then ran the water from the spring to the pool for twelve hundred cubits, and a hundred cubits as the height above the head of the stone cutters."

During the Persian period (beginning 539 BC), following the return of the Jews from Babylonian captivity, a second temple was built in the exact place where Solomon's Temple had stood. It was called the Temple of Zerubbabel, but is more often just called The Second Temple. It was finished around the year 518 BC and the account of its construction is found in Ezra 1-6. Nevertheless, it was built by the offerings, work, and faith of the people, and was an acceptable sacrifice.

The second temple endured through the Persian period and also the Hellenistic Period (332-167 BC), and was the edifice defiled by Antiochus IV (Epiphanes) the Seleucid Syrian monarch whose excesses against the Jews sparked the revolt which let to the Hasmonean Period of independence. The same temple, regained by the Jews from the hands of the Syrians, was rededicated during the winter of 165 BC. Tradition states that the lamp of the Temple burned for eight days on a single day's supply of oil, which was considered a miracle. The Feast of the Dedication (Hebrew Hannukah) grew out of this event into a yearly Jewish celebration from that time on (John 10:22).

Herod the Great desired to remodel the Second Temple, and expand the Temple Mount to twice its previous size, in order to better accomdate the thousands of people who came to Jerusalem three to four times a year for Jewish festivals. The unpopular monarch could not win the people's approval for the project until he had assembled all the materials and workmen in advance. Construction began around 20 BC. The old temple of Zerubabbel was completed dismantled, and Herod's splendid new edifice erected in its place, an effort which required 18 months. Priests were trained as masons (stoneworkers) to construct the building, in order to assure that the holy precinct was not trodden upon by unauthorized men. Jewish tradition asserts that during the entire period of construction it rained only at night, which was also considered a miracle, and a sign that the work had divine approval.

The rest of the temple complex, including the building of massive new retaining walls with columned porticoes, and a grand basilica along the southern extension of Herod's platform, continued for an additional eight years. The Temple of Herod was the temple in which Jesus and his Apostles taught many times. Their teaching activities, like the teaching activities of many other Jewish rabbi's and celebrities, was largely confined to the porches along the walls of the Temple Mount, which were covered porticoes with roofs supported by tall columns.

The temple of Herod was the spiritual center of Judaism during the Second Temple period. Though the synagogue grew in importance during this time, the temple remained at the heart of Jewish worship for it was only in the temple that the sacrifices required by the Law of Moses could be performed. The Temple was also the focus of the activities of the three major feasts and pilgrimages of the Law of Moses: The Passover, Pentecost, and Tabernacles.

General Purpose:

A. for Israelites to fulfill their obligations, such as the offering of the first fruits, the tithes and the wave offerings and obligatory sacrifices. (See later segment on offerings).

The law of Moses required that all men make a pilgrimage to the temple three times a year to celebrate feasts or festivals of Passover, Pentecost, and Tabernacles. Jews would also come to the temple "to offer either the obligatory sacrifices or those which he volunteered as thank-offerings or as whole-offering. C. To participate in Temple worship alongside the priests, especially in the form of deputations. APPEARANCE AND DESCRIPTION

The Temple was built upon a hill that was biblically known as Mt. Moriah. It was to here that Abraham brought Isaac to be offered as a sacrifice to the Lord (Gen. 22:2). Surrounding the Temple were several courts. The Temple with its courts was enclosed by a large massive wall. All together, the Temple, the courts, and the large outer wall were known as the Temple Mount; the Hebrew term for the Temple Mount is literally "the mountain of the house."

South and west of the Temple Mount lay the city proper. Directly south of the temple lay the original city of Jerusalem, often called the city of David. This was the city rebuilt by the Jews upon returning from the Babylonian captivity. Just west of the city of David is Tyropoeon Valley. The poor lived in this section of Jerusalem. One of the major water supplies of Jerusalem lay on the southeastern end of this section: the Pool of Siloam (John 9:1-7). On the northeast end of the poor section and on the southwest end of the Temple Mount was the Hippodrome, or the place where the horse races took place. West of the poor section lay Mt. Zion. Upon this hill the rich aristocracy lived. Herod's palace was located here as well as the high priest. North of Mt. Zion lay another section of Jerusalem that was known as the new city. Immediately north of the new city was Golgotha or Calvary.

On the northwest corner of the Temple Mount was the large and imposing Antonia Fortress, the Roman garrison. This may have been the Praetorium mentioned in the gospels (Mark 15:16) where Jesus was taken before Pilate (the other possible location proposed by scholars was Herod's palace. Luke referred to this structure as the castle (Acts 21:34, 37; 22:24; 23:10). It was set higher than any other structure in Jerusalem in order for the Romans to keep a watchful eye upon all Jerusalem.

East of the Temple Mount was the Mt. of Olives with the Kidron valley running between. On the western slope of the Mt. of Olives was the Garden of Gethsemane. Two bridges spanned the valley from the eastern wall to the Mt. of Olives (Mishnah Shekalim 4:2; Yoma 6:4; Parah 3:6). One crossed the valley from the Eastern Gate (which was directly east of the Temple Proper, and was known as the Red Heifer bridge. It was across this bridge that the Red Heifer was taken to be sacrificed (the ashes of this sacrifice were the principle ingredient of the "water of impurity" used for the purification of uncleanliness caused by contacting death - see Numbers 19:1-10). The second bridge was located on the southern end of the eastern wall. It was known as the Scapegoat Bridge (for on the Day of Atonement the goat upon which Israel's sins were symbolically placed was taken across this bridge and then led into the wilderness - Leviticus 16).

Generally, Israelite worshipers entered the Temple Mount from the south through a triple gate and ascended a large flight of stairs). To exit the Temple grounds one must pass through the exit platform on the south side, down a flight of stairs and out a double gate.

Referencing the Palestinian Talmud, Safrai has noted: "Everyone, priest or layman, took a ritual bath, even if he were clean, before entering the Temple." This could be done in the ritual baths associated with the Temple, especially those located next to the southern entrance of the Temple. Safrai also states: "It was customary for visitors to the Temple to wear white rather than coloured clothing, for the former was held to indicate modesty and piety: pious people were careful always to wear white. Before entering the Temple courts they removed their shoes, and laid aside their staffs, their money belts, their cloaks and bundles" (Safrai, p. 877; Safrai gleans this information from the Mishna and Talmud which may suggest that this was the ideal but may not have always been practiced.)

The stairs leading up from the triple gate opened into a large open court known as the Court of the Gentiles. As the Salt Lake Temple is surrounded by Temple Square, so this court surrounded the Temple proper (which included the inner courts and Sanctuary) and received its name from the fact that gentiles could proceed no closer to the Temple proper than this court.

Surrounding the Court of the Gentiles were porticoes two columns deep and each 25 cubits high upon which lay a flat roof. The rituals associated with the Mosaic Law were not performed in the Court of the Gentiles; "rather, its colonnades served as a gathering place for the people before and after worship, or for those who ascended the Temple Mount to hear the words of the Law. . . Business transactions relating to the Temple, as for example the purchase of sacrificial doves, oils, wines, and even the money changing, were not conducted in the inner courts but rather on the outer court of the Temple Mount . . ." (Safrai, pp. 865-866).

The Temple proper was separated from the Court of the Gentiles by a balustrade that was chest-high. There were gates through which patrons passed in order to proceed to the inner courts and the sanctuary itself. By each gate there was a sign warning Gentiles not to pass any further. Archaeology has uncovered one of these warning notices which states:

No foreigner is to enter within the forecourt and the balustrade around the sanctuary. Whoever is caught will have himself to blame for his subsequent death.

Beyond the balustrade preventing gentiles from passing lay the Temple proper (or sanctuary) with its several courts. "Though the temple area was entered from the south, the temple proper, where the altar and the sanctuary were, faced east and was entered from that direction. Thus Jewish worshippers walked from the southern end of the temple area towards the centre, turned left, and then proceeded from east to west. They passed the balustrade and its warning notices, went up a flight of fourteen steps, crossed a terrace ten cubits deep, went up another five steps and came to the inner wall . . ." (Sanders, Judaism: Practice & Belief, p. 61). Beyond this wall lay the Court of Women so named because women could pass no further than this area. The main entrance into the Court of Women was through the eastern gate (Fig. 3, no. 19) which was gold and silver plated. Secondary doors lay on the north and south.

On the western end of the Court of Women was a flight of 15 stairs in a semicircle that led to the Gate of Nicanor (also known as the "Beautiful Gate" - Acts 3:2,10). Beyond this gate was the Court of the Israelites followed by the Court of the Priests. The Court of the Israelites "was actually that portion of the Court of the Priests open to all male Jews . . . The Court of the Israelites was long and narrow . . . set off from the Court of the Priests by blocks of large polished ashlars and according to others by the levities' stand and stairs leading up to it, so that the Court of the Priests was 2 1/2 cubits higher than that Court of Israelites" (Safrai, Encyclopedia Judaica Vol. 15, p. 966). Within the Court of the Priests was the sanctuary. In the forecourt of the Court of the Priests between the Court of Men and the Sanctuary was the massive Altar of Burnt Offerings upon which all animal sacrifices were offered. Between the altar and the sanctuary was the large laver where the priests washed their hands and feet North of the altar was the slaughtering area for the animal sacrifices.

Within the wall surrounding the Court of the Priests were various chambers. Each chamber was used in the various actions associated with the rituals of the Law of Moses with the exception of the Chamber of Hewn Stone. It was in this chamber that the Sanhedrin met. Besides the Gate of Nicanor, six other gates lead into the Court of the Priests. Beginning on the north-west end, the names of the gates were as follows: the Gate of Flame, the Gate of the Offerings, and the Gate of the Kindling. Continuing on the south-west side: the Gate of Fuel, the Gate of Firstlings, and the Water Gate.

The most imposing feature of the Temple Mount was the sanctuary or Temple proper. It was no less than a hundred cubits high. The structure was divided into three rooms: the vestibule, the Holy Place, and the Holy of Holies. Only door posts separated the vestibule from the Holy Place while a veil (which consisted of two curtains) separated the Holy Place from the Holy of Holies. Entrance into the sanctuary was through a flight of stairs and beyond a massive gate which opened into the vestibule. Beyond the vestibule lay the Holy Place, a long rectangular room within which stood the golden candelabrum (near the southern wall), the table of shewbread (near the northern wall), and the altar of incense (in front of the veil). The Holy of Holies (sometimes called the Most Holy Place) was a square room . In the Tabernacle built by Moses and the temple built by Solomon, the Holy of Holies housed the Ark of the Covenant, the symbol for God's throne. But in Herod's Temple, this room lay empty, the Ark of the Covenant having been lost. However, the Mishnah states: "After the Ark was taken away a stone lay there from the time of the early Prophets and it was called Foundation. It was three fingerbreadths high" (Yoma 5:2).

Degrees of Holiness

It should be noted "that the temple area consisted of areas of increasing sanctity and the admission was progressively restricted." (Sanders, Judaism: Practice and Belief, p. 70). In fact, the view of the Mishna is that there were ten degrees of holiness in ancient Judaism (Kelim 1:6-9). Israel is more holy than any other land. Walled cities within Israel are more holy than Israel itself. Jerusalem is more holy than any other walled city. The Temple Mount is more holy than Jerusalem. The rampart upon which the temple proper is built (separated from the court of the Gentiles from the balustrade) is more holy than the Temple Mount. The Court of the Women is next in holiness. The Court of the Israelites surpasses the Court of the Women in holiness. The Court of the Priests is more holy than the Court of the Israelites. The area between the Altar of Burnt Offering and vestibule is holier than the Court of the Priests in general. The Holy Place is still holier than the space. Finally, the Holy of Holies is the most holy of all.

This holiness was symbolized by elevation. Walled cities were always built on hills. Jerusalem was built on Mt. Moriah and Mt. Zion. The Temple was built on the highest part of Mt. Moriah. A long stairway had to be traversed to reach the Temple Mount. The Temple proper was approached only by ascending the stairs surrounding the rampart upon which it was built. Once again, the Court of the Women was accessible only by mounting stairs as was the Court of the Israelites, the Court of the Priests, and finally the sanctuary itself.

Accesibility within each holiness was dependent upon ritual fitness and purity. Israel was more holy than any other land because it was the promised land, the land of the Jews, while all other lands were gentile. . .walled cities were holier than the land itself for lepers could not live therein neither could a corpse be buried within the walls. . .the temple mount was more holy because men or women with any excessive or unnatural discharge of body fluids were not able to pass further. . .the rampart was more holy than the Temple Mount because gentiles could pass no further. Ao so it went. The closer to the holy of holies the greater the ritual fitness required. This was so because the Holy of Holies was the place of God's presence and the symbol of his kingdom "and there cannot any unclean thing enter into the kingdom of God" (1 Nephi 15:34).

The Priesthood

During the Second Temple period the temple was under the charge of the Priests and Levites. "These priests and Levites (who numbered many thousands) were divided into twenty-four courses or teams (1 Chron. 24:1-19), which came up to the Temple in rotation for a week's period of service, each course being further divided into 'fathers' houses' that served on successive days throughout the week. (Russell, The Jews from Alexander to Herod, P. 120-121).

The priesthood had a three-fold hierarchy with each order separate and distinct and with each having clearly defined duties. The high priest stood at the head of the hierarchy. The chief feature of his position was the conjunction in one person of a political and a priestly dignity. Not only was he the supreme officer in the field of religion who alone had the right to perform certain clutic acts of the highest ritual significance, such as the offering of the sacrifice on the Day of Atonement; he was at the same time the political leader of the nation, the head of state, in so far, that is, as it was not under the domination of foreign overlords. In the days of national independence, the hereditary Hasmonean High Priests were simultaneously princes and kings; later, the High Priests were Presidents of the Sanhedrin as well as the supreme representatives of the nation vis-avis the Romans even in political matters. One consequence of the High Priest's distinguished social position was that he officiated as a priest only on festive occasions. By law, he was obliged to do so only on the Day of Atonement, when he presented the great sin-offering of the nation to God (Lev. 16); according to later practice, he also offered the daily sacrifice (which will be discussed later) during the week preceding the Day of Atonement. Otherwise, he was completely free to sacrifice whenever he wished. According to Josephus, he did this as a rule every Sabbath day and on the feasts of New Moon and New Year.

The uniqueness of his position also found expression in the special purity and holiness required of him (even above other priests) as well as in the magnificent vestments worn in the performance of his priestly duties.

Next to the high priest in rank were the priests. The main duties of the priests cnetered on the sacrifices (only priests could sacrifice) and the care of the vessels of the sanctuary (the candelabra, table of shewbread, and the altar of incense). Even within this order there was a hierarchy. The order of precedence went as follows. First, was the Captain of the Temple who had supreme charge over the cultic actions of the Temple proper and who also was the chief of police on the Temple Mount. Next, was the temple overseers (at least seven in all), who held the keys to the gates and supervised the physical arrangements of the Temple. Finally, there were the three treasurers who adminstered the Temple income. In the New Testament these men comprised the "chief priests" mentioned in the New Testament. They were all part of the Sanhedrin, the body of 70 and 71 men who were the ruling body of the Jews.

The Levites were that portion of the Tribe of Levi who were not descended from Aaron. Typically, their function in Temple worship was to take care of much of the menial work such as preparing firewood, cleaning utensils, gatekeepers, etc. They also played a major function in the actual worship services of the Temple as singers. They were also teachers of the Torah (five books of Moses).

It received its monicur for two traditional reasons: 1) Early in the morning and late at night the wall is covered with drops of dew which legend says are tears that the wall sheds while weeping for Israel in her exile. 2) the second reason is that the Jews come here to bewail the loss of their temple. Today the tears shed are tears of joy, for Israel has returned from her exile and is once more able to pray at the wall. Before the six day way of 1967, Jews were not able to visit the wall.

Here one sees bits of paper (containing prayers) placed in the cracks of walls. Water Pools

In Jerusalem of the Herodion period, which is also the New Testament Jerusalem, there some fifteen large water pools. Except for the pool of Siloam these pools were filled with rain water which was brought to them via natural topography or by aqueduct. These reservoirs were mostly available for public use. (Most houses in this period also had private cisterns for water storage).

The Pool of Bethesda has been excavated near Saint Annes Church by the White Fathers. The excavations show that the pool was built about 20 feet below present ground level. John 5 gives an unusually detailed description of this reservoir and it is the one where Jesus heals a crippled man. Today it is located inside St. Annes complex.

The five porches mentioned by John allude to Bethesda's design as a double pool, with columned proches around all four sides and a fifth columned porch in the middle dividing the northern half of the pool from the southern half. The entire complex measure about 390 feet long from north to south. The northern porch measured about 16o feet, the southern about 195. The depth of the pool was from 50 to 60 feet deep! Only a small portion of the northern half of the pool is visible today. The name Bethesda is Aramaic and means House of Mercy, an allusion to the Temple that stood nearby, because this pool was used to wash the sheep (thus its other name "sheep Pool") before their sacrifice at the temple. (Sheep were not immersed in the pool, but water was drown therefrom for washing the animals before use. The sheep market where the priests purchased the animals was nearby.

John 5:1-16--Jesus healed a lame man at the troubled waters of the pool of Bethesda.

The Pool of Israel was located along the eastern end of the north wall of Herod's Temple Mount. It held excess water which was diverted from the Pool of Bethesda, and because the runoff silt was deposited in the pool of Bethesda, the water of the Pool of Israel was much purer, and was used primarily for consumption at Feast times, and for many ritual washings performed at the Temple. The pool was 360 feet long, from east to west, and 125 feet wide. Its depth was nearly 70 feet. In addition to water storage, the pool also served as a defense moat protecting the east end of the vulnerable northern wall of the Temple Mount (the Antonia Fortress protected the western end of the northern wall). The stone quarried from the Pool of Israel (and that from Bethesda) was most likely used for building walls of the Temple Mount and Aontonia Fortress. For public health reason most of the pool was filled in during the 1930's, and is now the location of a parking lot inside Lion's gate to the south.

The Pool of St. Mary is located outside the eastern city wall, just north of Lion's Gate. The reason for its name is not known. It was apparently dug out during the Second Temple Period, and was also fed by excess water diverted from Bethesda. The reason for its location is also unknown, but it may be that after the city wall surrounding the Bezetha precinct of Jerusalem was built (this occurred decades after Jesus), and direct access to the Pool of Bethesda cut off, the need existed for a pool outside the wall which serviced not only the sheep market, but also inhabitants whos homes were outside the wall. This limited purpose may also answer for the relatively small size of the pool. The empty pool can still be seen today, the best view being from atop the city wall just north of Lion's Gate.

The Struthion Pool was also one of the smaller public pools of the city, and seems to have been dug primarily to service the Antonia Fortress. The pool is mentioned by Josephus as being open to the sky during the seige of AD 70, therefore the old idea that it was merely a cistern of the fortress is untenable. This conclusion lends to the proper identification of the so-called Antonia Pavement, which was built over the Struthion during the reign of Hadrian in the second century AD, and which could not have been the stone pavement on which Jesus was judged and scourged (although it was certainly with a few dozen meters of the correct site). The pool is located to the northwest of the Antonia wall and was fed by a conduit from the collection pool known as Lacus Legerii near Damascus gate. The Struthion can be viewed today beneath the Sisters of Zion convent in the Muslim quarter, near the start of the Via Dolorosa. The traditional site of Jesus' judgement, in the Antonia Fortress, was situated just to the south on the grounds now occupied by the Omariye Boys School.

The Leger Pool, called in Latin Lacus Legerii (Lake of Leger) was probably also constructed during the reign of Herod, as the city of Jerusalem expanded rapidly northward. It was a large trapezoid-shaped reservoir for storage of rainwater runoff in the city's northern precincts. Located slightly northwest of Damascus Gate, part of the deep recesses of this pool can still be seen.

The Serpents Pool (sultan's pool). Dating from second temple times, the Serpents pool lies outside the western city wall in the valley of Hinnom. Stone from its original excavation was probably used in construction of the city wall and Herod's Palace complex. It also added to the defense of the west side of the city, which prior to the pool's digging had been flat and shallow. The pool stored runoff which came down the Hinnom Valley from areas to the northwest. The reason for its name is unknown. The alternate name, Sultans pool, arises from the restoration and reuse of the pool under Sultans Barkuk (in 1398) and Suleiman the Magnificent (in 1538). Today the depression of the pool is a major landmark west of the Old City, and has been redesigned as a large outdoor theater for concerts and special events in Jerusalem.

Walls and Gates

The current walls surrounding the Old City of Jerusalem were built between 1537 and 1540 by order of Suleiman the Magnificent, the Sultan of the Ottoman Empire which controlled Palestine from 1517 to 1917. The total length of the wall around the city is about 2.5 miles and the area of the Old City is just over a quarter of a square mile. It is now possible to walk around the city on the ramparts of the Turkish wall from Lion's Gate on the east side all the way around the north and west sides to Dung Gate on the south side. Approximately 29,000 people make their residence in the Old City today. The majority are Arab Muslims, with a small representation of Christians of differing ethnicities. A significant number of Jews have moved back into the Old City since Jerusalem's reunification in 1967, for the most part residing in the restored Jewish quarter.

There are seven open gates in the Turkish walls which currently offer access to the different quarters of the Old City (Golden Gate, which is closed, and the traditional pinnacle of the Temple, opens onto Temple Mount).

Lion's Gate is the only open gate in the eastern wall of the city. It is sometimes called St. Stephen's Gate. However, there was no wall or gate at this location in the days of Jesus or Stephen, and the actual location of Stephen's death was outside the gate now called Damascus Gate.

It was built with reliefs of Lions on the gates facade because of a dream of Sultan Suleiman. Through this gate the Israeli Army gained first access into the Old City on June 6, 1967. South of the entrance about one half block is the Gate of the Tribes, which opens into the temple area. Further along Al Mujahadeen St. And across the street is the Church of St. Anne. The Church is one of the finest examples of Crusader construction in the Holy Land. It was built in 1100 on the site of the fifth century Byzantine church, which had in turn been built over a cave believed to be the home of Joachim and Anne, parents of the virgin Mary--hence the site of the immaculate conception (the doctrine that the Virgin Mary was kept free form original sin from the moment of her conception in Saint Annes womb). At the right center of the Church are steps leading down into the Grotto of the Virgins birth. At the end of the 12th century the church was turned into a school of Islamic studies by Saladin. The "white Fathers" a Greek Orthodox missionary order, took possession of the site in 1878.

Herods Gate is found in the northern wall toward the eastern end. It sits across the road from Saladin Street, East Jerusalem's main business artery. The gate dates from 1538, during Suleimans construction of the wall, and features the defensive corner turn in the gatehouse. It was blocked up during the 1880's, but later reopened as the Arab population outside the city wall grew. There was no city wall or gate in the area of Herod's Gate during Jesus' day, but the area was enclosed within the Third Wall of Jerusalem built by Herod Agrippa shortly thereafter. The wall of Aelia Capitolina and Byzantine Jerusalem followed the present line in this area, and it was at the location of modern Herod's Gate that the crusaders (under Tancred) broke through the wall in 1099 to conquer the city. The name "Herod" was associated with the gate and the area of medieval pilgrims, who were referring to the presence of Herod Antipas at Jerusalem during the passover of Jesus' arrest and trial.

Damascus Gate is in the exact location as earlier gates in the wall dating all the way back to the Herodian period, the time of Jesus. It is the middle one of the three gates in the northern wall, and was constructed by Suleiman in 1537 as the showcase gate in his new wall, the gem of Ottoman Jerusalem. It features ornately pointed turrets and towers which are not found on other gates of the city. It also employs a defensive corner turn in the gatehouse. The gate's name refers to the Syrian capital which lies 300 miles to the north on the road which leads out of the gate.

Other current names for Damascus gate are "Shechem Gate", as it is called by Jews, in reference to anicent Shechem which is located 40 miles to the north, and "Bal al Ammud" (gate of the column) in Arabic, which refers to the ancient pillar which sat just inside the gate in the plaza of Hadrian's Aelia Capitolina. The gate was called Neapolis Gate by the Romans of the second century, and the Byzantines called it both Galilee Gate, and St. Stephens Gate, due to the plausible tradition that Stephen had been martyred at a nearby place of stoning.

The first gate to be built here was the Herodian period exit through which the Savior must have carried his cross on is way to Golgotha, which is still visible behind the bus station across the road to the northeast. Little remains of that earliest gate, but significant portions of the second century gate built by Hadrian have been excavated and restored. The Hadrianic gate was a triple gate, with a large arched opening in the middle, flanked by two shorter arched gates. It is the shorter eastern gate which is visible below the modeern gate to the left. Ashlar blocks with a Herodian-style margin (either reused from Herod's walls or imitated by Hadrian) can still be seen in the second century gate. The ancient gate may be entered by visitors who will discover a restored portion of Hadrian's cardo, as well as an interesting museum concerning the city wall in various periods.

New Gate is also located in the northern wall of the city, just across the road from the French Hospice of Notre Dame. No gate was originally built here by Suleiman, but the completion of Notre Dame Hospice and monastery in 1887 brought a request for easier access to the Christian Quarter for visiting catholics. In 1889 permission was given for a breach in the wall to be made, and this the New Gate was constructed, giving quicker access to Christian shrines in the Old City.

Jaffa Gate is the only opening in the western wall of the Old City. It was constructed in 1538 as part of the wall built by Suleiman. In Jesus day the gate associated with the name Jaffa Gate was located about 50 meters east of the modern opening. Both the ancient and the modern gates are directly north of the area where the palace of Herod the Great stood. Herod's Palace complex, including courtyard and wall, was about 250 meters (820 feet) long from north to south, and featured a citadel with three massive towers at its northern end. One of those towers, Phasael's Tower, may be seen just insider Modern Jaffa Gate to the right (south). The lower courses of stone in that tower actually date to the Herodian period.

Jaffa gate itself is small, featuring a corner turn inside the gatehouse. The large 38 foot wide opening in the city wall just south of the gatehouse was made in 1898 in preparation for the visit of Kaiser Wilhelm of Germany

Matt. 21:12-16--Moneychangers cast out for the second time.

The Fourth Day Before the Passover

On his way from Bethany he curses the fig tree.

Matt. 21:23-23:1-36--He teaches at the temple. The day starts with a challenge of his authority by the priests. He teaches the Parables of the Two Sons, the Wicked Husbandmen, and the Wedding of a Kings son. As he is questioned about different issues he teaches the new law and ends with a denunciation of the hypocrisy of the Pharisees and Scribes who issue the challenge. They teach the law but do not keep it. He then laments for Jerusalem because of this wickedness that has entered into her.

The Third Day Before Passover

He gathers his Apostles and teaches them on the Mt. of Olives. We do not know the exact spot.

Matt. 24:1-51 JSM in the Pearl of Great Price--He answers questions about the second coming. The inspired retranslation of this in Joseph Smith Matthew in the Pearl of Great Price is the better account.

Matt. 25:1-46: Three important parables about preparation for that coming are given; The Ten Virgins, The Talents, and The Sheep and the goats.

The Second Day Before Passover

Matt. 26:2--Jesus' prophecy of his coming death by crucifixion.

Matt. 26:3-5--the conspiracy at the Palace of Caiaphas.

Matt. 26:14-16--Judas conspires to betray Jesus.

Eve of the Passover

John 13:1-17:26--The discourses and teachings of the Last Supper.

Matt. 26:30-57--The Garden of Gethsemane. Matthew indicates that this is where Jesus teaches Peter that when he is converted he is to convert others and that he will deny him thrice. His suffering follows, then Judas' betrayal and the Saviors arrest.

Matt. 26:57-68--Jesus is taken to Caiaphas' palace for a hearing. It is here that Peter makes his denials and the soldiers mock Christ (Luke 22:63-65).

Matt. 27:1-34: The next morning Jesus has another hearing before Caiaphas, then Pilate, (during this time Judas takes his own life out of remorse) then Herod where he is mocked and sent back to Pilate. Barabbas is released, Jesus is scourged and sent to Golgotha.

Matt. 27:57-66--Jesus is buried in the tomb of a friend and the chief priests and Pharisees seal it.

Matt. 28:1-20 and John 20:19-25--Events after his resurrection and the forty day ministry.

Luke 24:50-53--The Ascension.

Matt. 27:35-56: Jesus is crucified.
