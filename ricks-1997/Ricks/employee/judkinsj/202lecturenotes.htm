What is the role of the SLP or audiologist?

Sometime in your life you will have the opportunity to communicate with people who struggle to communicate with you. Here are some guidelines you may find helpful.

The most important thing is to acknowledge your uncertainty and fear, and then try to relax. Focus on the person rather than on your own nervousness. Remember, despite the speech, language, or hearing disorder, the individual is a person just like you; and he or she has many attributes besides the communication disorder.

Maintain eye contact with the person. This is a basic nonverbal way in which we bond with others - it shows that we are "open for business."

Give the person enough time and opportunity to talk. It may take longer for the individual to transmit a message. Speech is not an option for all persons who have communication disorders. There are other systems (signs, symbol boards, electronic devices) to assist in communication.

You may have to listen more carefully than you usually do. Be aware that this requires more effort - listening closely is hard work.

Focus on what the person is saying rather than how he or she is saying it.   The message is what is important, not its form. In fact, it is sometimes helpful to rephrase what was said so that the speaker knows the intended message has been transmitted.

If you don’t understand what the person is trying to say, tell him or her. Don’t pretend that you understand when you don’t. Ask the person to repeat if necessary. 

Never fill in a word or assist an individual unless he or she asks for help.    Offering assistance before it is requested - simply assuming that help is needed - can be demeaning and frustrating.

Speak directly to the person, not to a companion, even if the individual is using an interpreter. Never, under any circumstances, talk about the person in his or her presence.  In some instances, it may be helpful if you talk more slowly and more simply.

But don’t talk down to the person or adopt a patronizing manner. It may also  help to use gestures along with your verbal message.  Finally, let your language affirm the entire person, not just the communication disorder. Put the person first, not the disability. The way we refer to individuals with disabilities may shape our images of them. They are people, mothers, fathers, friends, bosses, cub scout leaders, and many things. Referring to the communication disorder label first, takes away from their humanity.

What can parents do?

Listen patiently to what your child says, and not how it is said, responding to the message rather than to the disfluency.

Allow your child to complete his or her thoughts without interrupting.

Keep natural eye contact while your child is talking.

Avoid filling in or speaking your child’s thoughts or ideas. Let the words be your child’s own.

After your child speaks, reply slowly and unhurriedly, using some of the same words.

Wait a second or so before responding to your child. This helps him or her to relax and slow thins down, and should help his or her speech.

Spend time each day devoted to talking with your child in an unhurried, easy, relaxed manner.

Find ways to show your child that you value the time you have together.

Definition: Cerebral Palsy-term used to describe a number of permanent neuromotor impairments acquired before the age of 3

Classification by variety:

Spasticity-characterized by highly tensed contractions of muscle groups

Athetosis-characterized by writhing, shaking, involuntary movements of the head, limbs, or the body

Flaccidity-characterized by passive relaxation; uncontracted, limp in appearance

Ataxia-characterized by lack of ability to coordinate muscle groups

Classification by body parts affected

Monoplegia-one limb

Hemiplegia-one half of the body (right or left)

Diplegia-one half of the body (upper or lower)

Quadriplegia-all four limbs

Cognition-mental retardation may occur in as high as 50% of the cerebral palsied population, but in many cases, problems with standardized tests make valid assessment very difficult. Many possess normal intelligence, but are judged incompetent due to appearances.

Emotional-anxiety in performing daily motor-related activities. Learned helplessness & passive acceptance of whatever is inflicted upon them.
