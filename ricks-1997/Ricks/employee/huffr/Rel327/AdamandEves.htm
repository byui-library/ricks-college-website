Adam and Eve’s Journal

By Mark Twain

 

EXTRACTS FROM ADAM’S DIARY            

 

MONDAY—This new creature with the long hair is a good deal in the way.  It is always hanging around and following me about.  I am not used to company.  I wish it would stay with the other animals…Cloudy today, think we shall have rain…we?  Where did I get that word?  I remember now—the new creature uses it.

 

TUESDAY—Been examining this great waterfall.  It is the finest thing on the estate.  The new creature calls it Niagara Falls—she names everything that comes along, before I can get in a protest.

 

WEDNESDAY—Built me a shelter against the rain, but could not have it to myself in peace.  The new creature intruded, she is always talking.  I have never heard the human voice before, and here upon the solemn hush of these dreaming solitudes it offends my ear.

 

FRIDAY—The naming goes on recklessly on, in spite of anything I can do.  I had a very good name for the estate:  GARDEN OF EDEN, but the new creature says it looks like a park.  Consequently, without consulting me, she has named it NIAGARA FALLS PARK.  And already she has put a sign up:

 

KEEP OFF THE GRASS

My life is not as happy as it was.

 

SATURDAY—The new creature eats too much fruit.  We are going to run short, most likely.  And she talks!  It used to be so pleasant and quiet here.

 

SUNDAY—Pulled through.  This day was selected and set apart as a day of rest, but this morning I found the new creature trying to clod apples out of that forbidden tree.

 

MONDAY—The new creature says its name is Eve.  I have no objections.

 

TUESDAY—She says this park would make a tidy summer resort.  What is a summer resort?  But it is best not to ask her; she has much a rage for explaining.

 

SATURDAY—I escaped last Tuesday night, and built me another shelter in a secluded place; but she hunted me out and came making that pitiful noise again, and shedding that water out of the places she looks with.  I was obliged to return.

 

Sunday—Pulled through.

 

Monday—I believe I see what the week is for: it is to give time to rest up from the weariness of Sunday.  It seems a good idea…she has been climbing that tree again.  Clodded her out of it.  She said nobody was looking.  Seems to consider that a sufficient justification for chancing any dangerous thing.

 

Tuesday—She told me she was made out of a rib taken from my body.  This is at least doubtful, as I have not missed any rib…

 

Saturday—She fell in the pond yesterday when she was looking at herself in it, which she is always doing.

 

Sunday—Pulled through.

 

Tuesday—She has taken up with a snake now.  The other animals are glad, for she was always experimenting with them and bothering them; and I am glad because the snake talks, and this enables me to get a rest.

 

Friday—She says the snake advises her to try the fruit of that tree, and says the result will be a great and fine and noble education.  I told her it would introduce death into the world and I advised her to keep away from the tree. She said she wouldn’t.  I foresee trouble.

 

Wednesday—I escaped last night, and rode a horse as fast as he could go, hoping to get clear of the park before the trouble begins, but it was not to be.  As I was riding through a flowery plain where thousands of animals are usually grazing, or playing with each other, all of a sudden a tempest of frightening noises broke out.  Every beast was destroying its neighbor.  I knew what it meant—Eve had eaten that fruit, and death had come into the world…The tigers ate my horse, paying no attention when I ordered them to desist, and they would have eaten me if I had stayed—which I didn’t.

 

                I found a place, outside, the park, and was fairly comfortable for a few days, but she has found me out.  I am not sorry she came, for she brought some of those apples and I was hungry.  It was against my principles, but I find that principles have no real force except when one is well fed…she came curtained in boughs and bunches of leaves, and when I snatched them away and threw them down, she tittered and blushed.  I had never seen a person titter and blush before.  She said I would soon know how it was myself.  This was correct.  Hungry as I was, I laid down the apple half-eaten---certainly the best one I ever saw—and arrayed myself in the discarded boughs and branches.

               

                After this we crept down to where the wild-beast battle had been and collected some skins, and I made her patch together a couple of suits proper for public occasions.  I find she is a good deal of a companion.  I see I should be lonesome and depressed without her, now that I have lost my property.

 

TEN DAYS LATER—She accuses ME of being the cause of our disaster!  She says that the Serpent assured her that the forbidden fruit was not apples, but chestnuts.  I turned pale at that for I have made many jokes to pass the weary time.  “There,” she said, with triumph, “that’s just it; the Serpent mentioned that very jest, and called it the First Chestnut, and said it was coequal with the creation.”

 

NEXT YEAR—We have named it Cain.  She must have caught it while I was up country trapping.  It resembles us in some ways, and may be a relation.  That is what she thinks, but the difference in size warrants the conclusion that it is a new kind of animal—a fish, perhaps, through then I put it in the water to see, it sank, and she plunged in and snatched it out before there was opportunity for the experiment to determine the matter.

 

                The coming of the creature seems to have changed her whole nature.  She thinks more of it then she does of any of the other animals, but she is not able to explain why.  Sometimes she carries the “fish” in her arms half the night when it complains and wants to get to the water.  At such times, the water comes out of the places in her face that she looks out of, and she pats the fish on the back and makes soft sounds with her mouth to soothe it, and betrays sorrow and solicitude in a hundred ways.  She never took on about them like this when their dinner disagreed with them.

 

SUNDAY—She doesn’t work Sundays, but lies around and likes to have the “fish” wallow over her; and pretends to shew its paws, and that makes it laugh.  I have not seen a fish before that could laugh.  This made me doubt…I have come to like Sunday myself.  Superintending all the week tires the body so.

 

WEDNESDAY—It isn’t a fish.  I cannot quite make out what it is.  It makes curious devilish noises when not satisfied, and says ‘goo’ when it is.  It is not one of us, for it doesn’t walk; it is not a bird, for it doesn’t fly; it is not a fish, though I cannot get a chance to find out whether it can swim or not.  It merely lies around, and mostly on its back, with its feet up.  I have not seen any other animal do that before.

 

THREE MONTHS LATER—The perplexity augments.  I sleep but little.  The creature has ceased lying around, and goes about on its four legs now.  Yet it differs from the other four-legged animals, in that its front legs are usually short, which causes the main part of its person to stick up uncomfortably high in the air.  Maybe it is of the kangaroo family.  It must be five times as big now as when it came.

 

THREE MONTHS LATER—“The kangaroo” still continues to grow, which is very strange and perplexing.  I never knew one to be so long getting its growth.  It has fur on its head now; not like a kangaroo fur, but exactly like our hair except that is much finer and softer, and instead of being black is red.  I caught a true kangaroo, and brought it in, thinking that this one, being lonesome, would rather have that for company than have no kin at all, but it went into such fits at the sight of the kangaroo that I was convinced it had never seen one before.  I pity the poor noisy little animal, but there is nothing I can do to make it happy.  I wanted to let it go, but she wouldn’t hear of it.  That seemed cruel and yet she may be right.  It might be lonelier than ever without it.

 

FIVE MONTHS LATER—It supports itself by holding to her finger, and thus goes a few steps on its hind legs, and then falls down.  It is probably come kind of a bear; and yet it ahs not tail—as yet—and no fur, except on its head.

 

A FORTNIGHT LATER—I examined its mouth.  There is not danger yet; it only has one tooth.  It has no tail yet.  It makes more noise now than it ever did before—and mainly at night.  I have moved out.  But I shall go over, mornings, to breakfast, and see if it has more teeth.  If it gets a mouthful of teeth it will be time for it to go, tail or not tail, for a bear does not need a tail in order to be dangerous.

 

FOUR MONTHS LATER—I have been off hunting and fishing a month, meantime the bear has learned to puddle around all by itself on its hind legs, and says “poppa” and “momma” it is certainly a new species!

 

THREE MONTHS LATER—It has been a weary, weary hunt, yet I have had no success.  In the meantime, without stirring from the home estate, SHE has caught another one!  I never saw such luck.  I might have hunted these woods a hundred years; I never would have run across “that thing.”

 

NEXT DAY—I have been comparing the new one with the old one, and it is perfectly plain that they are the same breed.  I was going to stuff one of them for my collection, but she is prejudiced against it for some reason; so I have relinquished the idea.  The old one is tamer now and can laugh and talk like a parrot.  The new one is as ugly now as the old one was at first; has the same sulphur and raw-meat complexion and the same singular head without any fur on it.  She calls it Able.

 

TEN YEARS LATER—They are BOYS: we found it out long ago.  It was their coming in that small, immature shape that puzzled us.  There are some girls now.  Able is a good boy, but if Cain had stayed a bear it would have improved him.  I see that I was mistaken about Eve in the beginning; it is better to live outside the Garden with her than inside it without her.  At first I thought she talked too much, but now I should be sorry to have that voice fall silent and pass out of my life.  Blessed be the chestnut that brought us near together and taught me to know the goodness of her heart and the sweetness of her spirit!

 

EVE’S DIARY

 

When I look back, at the garden is a dream to me.  It was a beautiful but now it is lost, and I shall see it no more. 

 

                The Garden is lost, but I have found HIM, and am content.  He loves me as well as he can; I love him with all the strength of my passionate nature.  If I ask myself why I love him, I find I do not know—and I do not really much care to know.

               

                It is not an account of his brightness that I love him—no, it is not that.

               

                It is not on account of his gracious and considerate ways and his delicacy that I love him. No, he    

                Has lacks in these regards.  But he is improving.

 

                It is not on account of his industry that I love him—no, it is not that.  He has it in him, and I do

                Not know why he conceals it.

 

                It is not on account of his education that I love him—no, it is not that. He is self-educated, and

                Does really know a multitude of things, but they are not so.

 

                It is not on account of his chivalry that I love him.  He told on me, but I do not blame him; it is a

                Peculiarity of sex, I think, and he did not make his sex.

               

                Then why is it that I love him?  Merely because he is masculine, I think.

 

He is strong and handsome, and I love him for that, and I admire him and am proud of him without those qualities.  I would love him even if he were a wreck and I would work for him, and slave over him, and pray for him, and watch by his bedside until I died.

 

Yes, I think I love him because he is mine, there is not other reason, I suppose.  And so I think that this kind of love is not a product of reasoning and statistics.  It just comes—none knows whence—and cannot explain itself.  And doesn’t need to.
