When considering the theme, femmes fatale, one starts with ancient stories written two to three millennia before Christ. Yet in that early, pre-dawn haze, when shifting shadows were thick and when the fairy voices were ambiguous and weak, one can only infer how things were.

Created by men, the early myths and stories were likely repeated, embellished, and altered by women as they taught their children. But the stories were framed by men who saw themselves as superior to women.  Once the stories were internalized in a culture, both men and women learned their identities through the telling and interpretations of the stories.

These stories attempted to explain the meaning of life and to explain humankind's relationship to powers and conditions which men and women could neither control nor understand. Eventually the ancient stories were written by men, but only after they had first passed through a long, oral tradition.

The stories of King Gilgamesh, two-thirds God and one-third human, come from twelve, ancient, stone tablets written in the northern-Sumerian dialect of Akkadian. (Shown above is a fragment of the eleventh tablet.)  Gilgamesh was the king of Uruk in southern Mesopotamia, a land that is now part of modern-day Iraq.

The stories were recorded soon after true writing developed in this area.  From these stories we observe relationships between men and women.   One women, the goddess Ishtar,  was rejected by Gilgamesh.  He rejected her because he noticed the destruction that came to many of Ishtar's mortal lovers.   Because of Gilgamesh's rejection, Ishtar openly tries to kill him.

Because life is a cycle, because all things live then die, a mother goddess would be associated with both birth and death. That is certainly true even today as we consider Mother Nature. She is associated with spring birth and winter death, destructive storms and soothing rains and sunshine.

One goddess, typical of so many, is the Lady of the Beasts. This Semite deity from Phoenicia is associated with both the tomb and womb, with both darkness and light, with opposite attributes in the same personage. As an archetypal Mother Nature, she both creates and destroys.

Notice the serpents in her upraised hands perhaps suggesting an Eve-like association with the Serpent. Notice the skulls below her suggesting death. (Certainly we have death associated with our own Mother Eve, death which came through our first parent's transgression). Next, notice the lady's large breasts which suggest fertility and life. The dancing horses show the woman's connection with the raw energy of the physical world.

Women as Destroyers

 

Ulysses and the Sirens by John Waterhouse

From the Greek myths, first recorded around 800 B.C., we learn of the Sirens, three seductresses whose final home becomes the west coast of Italy near Naples. Here the shore was strewn with the bones of sailors who had been lured to their deaths by the delightful siren songs. One adventurer, Ulysses, was able to listen to the siren song and escape destruction because he had all of this men plug their ears with wax, and he was tied securely to the mast. The men rowed past the danger.


Another Greek myth showing the femme fatale motif deals with Hylas and the nymphs. The handsome, young page of Hercules was sent onto the island of Cios to find water. He found water, but oh, what additional dark beauty he found. The nymphs lured Hylas into the pool and pulled him to the cold, silent bottom. Although Hercules made the earth shake as he called for Hylas, the unfortunate page was never seen again.

From the stories collected by Sir Thomas Mallory in Le Morte D'Arthur and popularized by T. H. White in The Once and Future King, we observe the moral rot caused by Lancelot's and Guenevere's adultery. We also see the seductive forces of the half witch, Morgan Le Fay as she lured Arthur to her bed.   With her thick, flowing hair, Morgan Le Fay was an exquisite beauty.  From this unhappy union came the bastard son, Mordrid. Later, Mordrid fought to destroy the Round Table, and he started a civil war which resulted in the deaths of hundreds.

Perhaps the strongest femme fatale warning in the Judeo-Christian culture comes from stories recorded in the Holy Bible. We know of Eve's transgression, and the temptation of Adam. These actions resulted in death for both Adam and Eve and for all humankind.

We know of the consequences of David's love for Bathsheba.   We remember the treatment of Delilah as she deceived Samson.  And we read of Potifer's wife, the great seductress. But perhaps the most powerful words of warning came from Solomon as he told the dangers of inappropriate sexual encounters.  We read in Proverbs 7:10-24:

The clever woman described here is firm in her resolves. She ponders carefully,  plans and dresses for the occasion, uses logical arguments, and kisses unabashedly her man. She knows him, and she knows what he likes.   She uses various perfumes to make the experience more enticing and pleasurable.

This uninhibited woman, with her "fair speech," tells the man exactly what he wants to hear including what they will be doing in the bed.    Well, it is all too much for the man. He yields. Conclusion

We see a wide range of attitudes and behaviors when examining women in literature and art.  On the side of light are angels of mercy like Joan of Arc, Florence Nightingale, Mother Teresa, and Bernadette.  On the side of darkness are destroyers like the Sirens, Morgan La Fey, La Belle Dame sans Merci, and Maila Murmi.   Yet as we examine the femmes fatale theme, we find aspects of the myth which are highly unfair to women.

Born out of man's early roles and duties, born out of the most ancient of myths, and perhaps born out of feelings of inadequacy,  men have created and perpetuated myths which are hostile towards women.   Some men may have felt anger and ambivalence because, on the one hand, they wanted a woman's intimacy, but on the other hand, they resented their own needs and inability to resist a   woman.

Looking again at the Epic of Gilgamesh, this ambivalence towards women is shown as we observe Enkidu, a major character and friend of the king.   An Adam type, Enkidu was deeply connected to nature.  At the same time, a companion was needed for Gilgamesh.  The Gods choose Enkidu for Gilgamesh's companion, but Enkidu was so wild that he first needed to be tamed.  The Gods made a plan.   Their method was to have Shamhat, a temple prostitute, offer herself to Enkidu.  

She did.  She took off her clothes and seduced Enkidu near the watering hole.  The results, as planned by the Gods, were predictable.   Losing his wild nature, Enkidu became more civilized and less able to join with the animals.   Yet he gained something too: he became more like the Gods by gaining wisdom and knowledge.  Nevertheless, feeling a sense of lose, Enkidu resented Shamhat.   He lamented no longer having a deep, carefree connection with the animals which now would have nothing to do with him.

In the story of Gilgamesh we see man's negative attitude toward women not only by what is said but also by what is not said.  A wife is mentioned only as an afterthought, and the only significant women are Shamhat, the prostitute, and Ishtar, the goddess of sexual love.  When Enkidu felt attached to a woman, his life grew increasing complicated.  As he moved toward civilization, he became enmeshed in an ever-increasing, restricting web of lethargy.  He blamed his encounter with Shamhat for many of his newly found hardships.

Ancient myths show that men blamed women not only for death and loss of innocence, but also for many problems in the world.

Consider the Greek myth of Pandora. 

Pandora was given a beautiful, well-crafted box by Hermes while at the same time, she was given the gift of curiosity by Hera.   She was cautioned never, never to open the box.  She resisted for a while, but eventually she opened the box.   Immediately, disease, sorrow, and numerous other problems flowed out of the box into the lives of all mortals.

Although man's contempt for women reaches back to our earliest beginnings, negative attitudes are also found in contemporary cultures.   For example, consider China.  From Pearl Buck's The Good Earth and from Amy Tan's The Joy Luck Club, we learn that  Chinese men treat women as inferiors.  Making all the important decisions, men are the masters; women are the servants.  A new male baby is cherished; a new female baby is despised.  Even today, females are sometimes killed by parents who are disappointed that a newborn is not a boy.
