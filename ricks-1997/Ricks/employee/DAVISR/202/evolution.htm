EVOLUTION: IS IT COMPATIBLE WITH MORMONISM?

By Valerie Willden Geol. 110, 1993

 

For some who may read this, even the heading might cause a gasp of shocked disbelief. Those who consider themselves educated and enlightened are often as adverse to the concept of evolution as the uninformed. Uniformitarianism, the idea that the earth is very old and that life forms evolved slowly, is the opposing view of creationism. Creationists espouse the concept of an earth and universe which are relatively young, that catastrophism explains the physical form of the earth today, and that all living things were created supernaturally (National Academy of Sciences 7). Since Adam lived only about six thousand years ago and is esteemed as the first man by Christians, how do we explain Cro-Magnon and other pre-Adamites? Do Latter Day Saint scriptures and religious tenets clash with evolutionary theory, or do they support each other?

When Charles Darwin, noted scientist and member of good standing in The Church of England, first proposed his theories of natural selection and common ancestry in the mid 1800s, religious fundamentalists were scandalized (Gould 64). These ideas seemed contrary to everything Christians believe.

Darwin, however, disagreed. He felt that evolutionary theory collides with religious dogma only if the Bible is scrutinized in the most literal sense.

Evolutionary theory captured the immediate interest of the scientific community as well as of Christian thinkers and writers. Eventually, the entire argument began to flip flop, with evolution tipping the scale as the most accepted theory.

Controversy among Christians still exists today. As well documented as fossil and faunal succession records are', evolution remains a taboo subject in much of the Christian community. The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-Day Saints is undoubtedly one of the most enlightened and educated groups, encouraging its members seek knowledge “out of the best books” and to recognize that “the glory of God is intelligence, or in other words, light and truth” (Doctrine and Covenants). Yet, among Latter-Day Saints, the subject of evolution is usually avoided. Most Mormons, in fact, are visibly uncomfortable when questioned about evolution or a possible relationship between uniformitarianism and Mormonism. Purists from both camps claim that they are light years apart in philosophy; that there can be no link between the two.

One popular Christian writer called this a naturalistic viewpoint as opposed to a supernaturalistic viewpoint (Lewis 534), with the supernaturalist attributing the wonders and miracles of life to a Higher Power. Many thinking Christians fall into this latter category, including a number of Latter Day-Saints, though they may be loathe to admit it. A. Lester Allen, Dean of Biology and Agriculture at Brigham Young University, claims that “for most members of the LDS Church and for most LDS biologists there is no problem” in accepting the evolution question. He calls the common ground between the groups “divinely directed development.” Allen terms what C.S. Lewis call “naturalists” as “mechanists” and what Lewis called “supernaturalists,” he lists as givitalists.” (Allen 9) The vitalists assume that living things are endowed with a special life principle or spirit; the theistic vitalists believe that God controls the physical manifestations of bodies through their spirits and further assumed that God used natural law to bring about his purposes. This type of theistic vitalist-evolutionist assumes that God used evolutionary-type processes to prepare the earth for the habitation of man.

In 1988 a surprisingly credible attack was launched on both evolutionists and middle grounders. Phillip E. Johnson, respected law professor at University of California at Berkeley, gave a daring lecture, taking to task those who accept evolution as fact rather than theory. Darwinism, said he, is not a' real scientific hypothesis, merely an attestable expression of naturalism (Woodward 33). Johnson claimed that scientists have forced the facts by using ad hoc hypothesis and circular reasoning. According to his lecture, later elaborated upon in a book called Darwin On Trial, if Darwinism were a scientific hypothesis based on a fair assessment of evidence, it would have been abandoned long ago.

Johnson clings to the creationist viewpoint and deplores the complimentary view (science tells what happened and how; theology tells who did it and why). Although Johnson's book has achieved a fair amount of attention and respect for the thoughtful way it is presented, his theory remains unpopular. Overwhelming supporting evidence makes evolution still our best explanation of what happened to form the earth.

Is the theory of a divinely inspired creation by natural law acceptable by Mormon standards? Does it reflect the stated position of the Church?

Respected geologist James E. Talmadge, chemist John A. Widstoe (after a time) and Duane E. Jeffrey, Brigham Young University Zoologist, are among those who have agreed that evolution probably played a large part in the creation (Hess & Matheney).

Joseph Fielding Smith was one of evolutions most adamant opponents, but even he finally concluded: The stated position of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-Day Saints seems to be that while man is literal creation and descendent of God, there really is no stated position about the rest of evolution.

According to a statement by President Smith, published in 1909 and again in 1925: The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-Day Saints, basing its belief on divine revelation, ancient and modern, declares man to be the direct and lineal offspring of Deity. . Man is the child of God, formed in the divine image and endowed with diving attributes.

More recently, in the Encyclopedia of Mormonism, the following treatise is given: “The scriptures tell why man was created, but they do not tell how, though the Lord has promised that he will tell that when he comes again.” The Encyclopedia goes on to state that in 1931, when organic evolution was hotly debated, the First Presidency (Presidents Grant, Ivins and Nibley), addressed the General Authorities and concluded: Upon fundamental doctrines of the Church we are all agreed. Our mission is to bear the message of the restored gospel to the world. Leave geology, biology, archeology, and anthropology, no one of which has to do with the salvation of the souls of mankind, to scientific research . . . Upon one thing we should all be able to agree. “Adam is the primal parent of our race.” (Evenson 478)

The stated claim of the Church, then, regarding evolution, seems to be that there is no stated claim, with the exception of the creation of man. And on this subject, President Spencer W. Kimball, said: “The story of the rib is, of course, figurative,” referring to the creation of Eve. If Eve's creation is figurative, might Adam's creation from the dust of the earth mean symbolically that he was made up of natural elements? We do not know exact details of the creation of man. Suffice it to say, Mormons should be content to realize that Adam was the literal offspring of God, the first true man endowed with a human spirit capable of eternal promise, and that upon the return of the Lord, these things will be revealed.

If religion and science do not necessarily clash on the subject of the creation of the world (assuming that evolution did play a part, under the divine direction of the Godhead), where does that leave the pre-Adamites? Without a doubt, they existed. Would God--for some inscrutable reason, or merely to test our faith--create five species, one after the other . . . to mimic a continuous trend of evolutionary change? (Gould 68)

On this subject, religion is unsure. Following the Origin of Man statement, a surprising editorial appeared in the Era by the First Presidency in 1931. These are the authentic statements of the scriptures, ancient and modern, and it is best to rest with these, until the Lord shall see fit to give more light on the subject. Whether the mortal bodies of man evolved in natural processes to present perfection, through the direction and power of God; whether the first parents of our generation, Adam and Eve, were transplanted from another sphere, with immortal tabernacles, which became corrupted through sin and the partaking of natural foods, in the process of time; whether they were born here in mortality, as other mortals have been, are questions not fully answered in the revealed word of God.

Religion and science do not necessarily have to clash. Members of both worlds of theology and of science admit that they do not have all the answers. Evolution is a theory, the best theory with the most supporting evidence, which man, with his limited knowledge, can provide at this time. Mormon scientists concur that evidence for evolution is overwhelming.

To what degree evolution played in the creation, of its possible effects on the creation of man, we are unsure. We do know that the search for truth is the common link shared by science and Mormonism. Latter Day Saints of all persuasions agree that priesthood, that form of energy eluding our comprehension, is used to do God's work.

“The Lord is not displeased with science or scientists, and it appears that He is using man to help prepare the earth for the millennium,” states A. Lester Allen, Dean of Biology and Agriculture, Brigham Young University.

The Lord permits man to use his discoveries for righteous purposes, each discovery pointing to the undeniable Christian testimony that no evolutionary changes were random happenstance. Rather, they were divinely orchestrated developments with definite purposes and goals.

In a righteous desire for truth, man is allowed, even encouraged, to study and ponder. In doing so, he must bear in mind that neither science nor religion has revealed all of the answers to date. Duane E. Jeffery, Dean of Biology at Brigham Young University, said that these issues should be considered when dealing with the subject of evolution: that we must keep open minds, without compromise of doctrine; that we should not expect a public declaration unless it comes from the First Presidency, and if one does come, we must must be prepared to accept it. We have a right to ask for help in understanding such declarations and scientific theories. The Lord is the author of all truth. And last, we should be cautious in judging other people on the basis of their opinions about such things as evolution: it is not our job to judge them at all.

Jeffery also stated:  The Church . . . has no official position on organic evolution as long as certain doctrinal concepts are not compromised. Adam and Eve were divinely created. After specifically and intentionally considering the above, all facts, theories and speculations concerning the history of the temporal earth, its geology, biology, and anthropology, shall be "fair game" for investigation by the responsible scientist.

In conclusion, science (evolutionary theory) and religion are not necessarily at loggerheads. The responsible Latter-Day Saint who wishes to study evolution may be assured that this is permissible within the bounds of what has been mentioned. Many brilliant Latter-Day Saint scientists believe that evolution played a large part in the creation. Finally, we can look forward to the time when we will be able to know of a surety how this world came to be. Yea, verily I say unto you, in that day when the Lord shall come, He shall reveal all things--Things which have passed, and hidden things which no man knew, things of the earth, by which it was made, and the purpose and the end thereof--Things most precious, things that are above, and things that are beneath, things that are in the earth, and upon the earth and in heaven. (Doctrine and Covenants 101: 32-34)

Surely Latter-Day Saints should not be afraid to seek knowledge about these things within the correct premises. In the future, man will be required to know these things in order to progress to the point of creating other worlds.
