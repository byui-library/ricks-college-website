Revision

The best way to learn how to revise effectively is to do it. Revision will be one of the most important skills you take with you from this class.

Sometimes it helps to understand that revision is a lot like surgery. A heart surgeon doesn't try to fix a faulty valve by curing the patient's acne or other surface flaws. She cuts beneath the skin, wedges open the rib cage, and has at it. It's a messy job, but nothing less will fix the problem.

You should revise in much the same way. Fixing a sentence here or changing a word there won't do the job. Revision often involves getting right to the heart of the essay and doing major surgery. The flow chart included in this section should give you an idea of where to start. Begin with the big concerns: thesis, organization, and transitions. Notice that you don't even get to the grammar and mechanics concerns until the very end of the process. They are like frosting on a cake they're important, but if the cake's no good, no amount of frosting will fix it. (I think I'm mixing metaphors here, but I hope you get the point.) Start with what's most important the cake or the heart the very internal structure of your essay.

A word of caution. Revision is messy. Cracking open the chest of your essay or messing with its recipe is bound to make an impact. Once you've made changes, you have to go back and make sure they fit seamlessly into the surrounding landscape of your paper. Think about Frankenstein. (Sorry, I'm adding yet another analogy to the mix.) What made him such a monster is that he had so many strange parts. The doctor would sew on an arm from one body and a leg from another, never taking time to see how the separate parts of the body would fit and work together. Pretty soon he ended up with a monster.

As you work through the revision process, you might notice a similar trend. Sometimes students complain that their papers actually seem to get worse as they revise. That's to be expected. As you sew on new parts and cut out others, your drafts are bound to become a bit monstrous. Don't panic. That's part of the process. Just make sure you don't stop there. Once you have the body of your essay stitched together, it's your job to go back and smooth out the seams (transitions), to check and make sure all the parts really do belong with the body (thesis and support), and to see that the parts work and function together as you intended.

Revision can be frustrating, brain draining, head banging work, but the important thing to remember is that it also leads to a remarkable payoff: your own, living, breathing creation--a finished piece of work of which you can rightfully be proud. Enjoy!
