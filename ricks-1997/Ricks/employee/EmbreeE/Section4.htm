Effective Interpersonal Communication: Equality

Peter Delisle*

*Peter A. Delisle was an honorarium instructor in communication at the University of Colorado, Colorado Springs, for nine years. He holds a B.A. in communication from the University of Connecticut and an M.S. in education from Connecticut State University. HQ is vice president of human resources for Convex Computer Corporation, Dallas, Texas.

When I think of the characteristic of effective interpersonal communication that has been most important in my understanding of what makes people successful, I think of equality.

In this context, equality means that we recognize each other's significance in the process of communication and you and I see a parity in our roles and mutual respect. This is also, in my estimation, the single most misunderstood and least-regarded characteristic of the many identified.

I've seen some good examples of genuine practice of equality that I think illustrate the vitalness of this behavior. In each case, the circumstances involved my association, however brief, with men most people would deem to be famous and powerful.

As a young Army officer, I used to marvel at the ease with which general officers could converse with soldiers. As a matter of fact, it seemed that privates and generals always seemed to get on best. My first sergeant observed that they both had the same number of people to salute. Essentially, they understood each other's station, had nothing to prove, and yielded a genuine respect for each other. Hence, they talked to each other as human beings. It was the majors and colonels who seemed to wrap themselves in knots.

I experienced this genuineness with a general officer shortly thereafter, upon my assignment as Army liaison officer to General Omar Nelson Bradley I was a brand-new captain enjoying my newfound humanity upon being liberated from the ignominy of my lieutenancy. I got my assignment: "Take care of General Bradley...If it goes perfect, you're doing your job; if anything gets fouled up, it's your fault....Yessir, Colonel!" A protocol packet and vita two inches thick detailed Bradley's career, personal profile, medical needs...right down to diet. I committed it to memory and started to prepare for the visit. Ironies stunned me. Bradley was promoted to five stars the year I was born; was a major the year my dad was born; commanded 12 million soldiers during the Second World War, initially organized and led the Veterans Administration; had twenty croix de guerre decorations-the Soldier's General.

I was stunned, frightened, and awed by the stature of this man. I was near paralysis, but my job was to help his aid-de-camp, provide first-class gopher assistance, and to stay as obscure and unobtrusive as I could.

General Bradley was eighty-four when he visited. I had read how badly he was crippled by arthritic knees, confined to a wheelchair. He wasn't able to leave the chair and needed accommodations that allowed access easily. Standing subjected him to grave physical pain. Well, we got the general safely to his billet. I was scurrying around getting his gear stowed when he aid grabbed me by the shoulder. C'mere, Pete," he said, "I want you to meet the general." I shrugged with, "Gee, Colonel, I need to do my ~ob," wishing to remain comfortably anonymous. He marched me by the elbow to meet the "Ol' Man." (I laughed a year later when my soldiers referred to me as the Ol' Man.) I squirmed, righted myself, and watched and listened as the aid said, "General Bradley, this is Captain Delisle, he's gonna take care of us for the next few days." He shoved me gently toward the general...Bradley stood up to shake my hand.

A few years later, while working for a well-regarded electronics company as a training and organization communication specialist, I had occasion to meet a cofounder of the company as he received an award of national stature from the Air Force for his service to America. David Packard was revered by military people as the civilian who blew the whistle on government contracts that were bid and won before the product was produced. "Fly before you buy" was Packard's plain comment! He brought integrity to a much abused procurement process. Packard was known by his employees as a tough-minded businessman who wandered the halls of each operating division during his visits, talking to people face-to- face, person-to-person. Packard believed in meting and talking to people where they worked--on their turf, so to speak.

Rumor has it that at one meeting of general managers-- as late as 1984, when he had handed the reins to his successor--he chastised the group's infatuation with the glamour of "office automation" and electronic mail, with the comment,"I don't know how you can be out talking to your people with your face stuck in a tube all day."

Packard was most often regarded as a warm, humane person who genuinely cared about his friends, his "gang" of employees. I felt that warm regard, that feeling of acceptance and sense of elevation, when after standing in the reception line waiting to meet him, I finally approached to shake hands (armed with my recollection of General Bradley's gesture). I resolved to greet him cordially, but what came out of my mouth was,"Hi, I work for you." Packard smiled and said, "I know." He immediately turned to his wife, Lucille, and introduced my wife and me. We had a conversation that lasted two minutes, but I'll remember it a lifetime. I left with a feeling of genuine acceptance. No position, no rank, just friends meeting for the first time.

On Being Likeable

Roger Ailes, Communications Consultant

  1. Be considerate. Make listeners comfortable.
  2. Get off to a good start. A strong, warm, responsive beginning puts you in control.
  3. Choose your words. Try to talk so your listeners can understand you.
  4. Persuade. Requests work better than orders.
  5. Relate. Be enthusiastic and react naturally. Don't hide behind a deadpan expression.
  6. Be patient. People think at different speeds.
  7. Read between the lines. Some people have personal problems. If someone doesn't seem to be absorbing what you're saying, don't immediately assume that he isn't buying your story.
  8. Admit your weaknesses, when appropriate. You will gain respect and understanding.
  9. Pay compliments. They express your awareness and make people want to please you.
  10. Express thanks. You'll gain support.
  11. Finally, consider Lord Chesterfield's advice: "Be wiser than other people, if you can, but do not tell them so."
Roger Alies, chairman of Ailes Communications, Inc., is a communications consultant to corporations and their CEO's. His political clients have included Presidents Bush and Reagan.

Subject: Management Responses to Dead Horses

Dakota Indian tribal wisdom says that when you discover you are riding a colloquial dead horse, the best strategy is to dismount. However, in modern organizations, we often try other strategies with dead horses, including the following:

- buying a stronger whip
- changing riders
- saying things like "This is the way we always have ridden this horse"
- appointing a committee to the study the horse
- arranging to visit other sites to see how they ride dead horses
- increasing the standards to ride dead horses
- appointing a tiger team to revive the dead horses
- creating a training session to increase our riding ability
- comparing the state of dead horses in today's environment
- passing a resolution declaring: "This horse is not dead"
- blaming the horses' ancestry
- harnessing several dead horses for increased speed
- declaring that "No horse is too dead to beat"
- providing additional funding to increase the horse's performance

THE TRUTH

Are the people you supervise telling you what they want you to hear instead of what you need to hear? Are they hiding bad news or mistakes? What you don't know can hurt you. FORBES asks eight top executives what they do to keep information flowing from bottom to top.

Charlotte Beers
Chairman
Ogilvy & Mather

It's the women who are catalysts for truth-telling around here. They are the most blunt and bold.
Women have the advantage of not being trained in business talk, while men have been trained to be more careful- even discreet. Men don't use words as emotionally laden or as colorful as women do. Women are more willing to discuss the truth that uncovers people's feelings and ask an uncomfortable question to get at the truth.
A man once explained to me that if a man is too direct he has to consider a fistfight. Women have never had to live with that fear.

Pamela Lopker
President
QAD Inc.

The key number one item that causes alarms in my head is when somebody says it's so complicated I can't explain it to you-when they say, "Trust me, I'll get it done." If someone can't explain to me what they are doing very simply, and show me milestones between now and when they finish it, I just don't believe them.
I've never had a project completed successfully where someone couldn't articulate what they were doing.

Jerry Kalov
President and Chief Executive
Cobra Electronics

If anyone in the company is free to call you, then you'll get more truth. Here at Cobra I have a phone extension that is not screened by anyone, and it's only given to employees. I pick it up every time it rings. I got the idea for this phone line from having been an employee-when I wasn't a big shot. Very often I had things I wanted to say, but my immediate boss didn't want me to do it because he wanted to take the credit. Or maybe he didn't agree with it. So I felt like I had good ideas or things to say but I couldn't get through.
The open-line system is better than management by walking around because people may be intimidated to be seen talking to the chief executive. The supervisor can say, "What were you talking to him for?" What do you say? If you use something as discreet as a telephone line it seems to work pretty well.
More often than not when my phone rings here at Cobra it's somebody down on the assembly line. They might have an idea, a concern.
I remember one dastardly example. People were using drugs on company time. A call came from an employee who wanted to be anonymous. She felt no one else would listen. She trusted me. She had no other way to do it. I started an investigation in the company and it turned out her information was valid.
Who knows where the next brilliant idea is going to come from? Who knows who's the next chief executive officer?

David House
Chairman and Chief Executive
Bay Networks

I worked at Intel for 22 years, where Andy Grove finds 12 for every 3 problems. He constantly challenges you: "What's your problem? What's your plan? What are you going to do about it?" He's always looking for the problems. Pretty soon he uncovers one you haven't found!
I've been out of Intel for two months now and I have noticed that at Bay Networks people didn't have a culture of speaking honestly about problems. They tended to talk more about the good news. But, like Andy, I don't let them off the hook. I've got to make it wrong not to talk about problems.

Charles R. Eitel
President and Chief Executive
Interface, Inc./Floor Coverings Group

One morning I was coming in late and I parked my Mercedes in a handicapped parking spot at one of our plants in La Grange, Ga. It wasn't more than 30 minutes when one of our hourly employees in the order department came up and asked me if I had parked in the handicapped parking space. I said yes. She replied that her mother was handicapped and that she really took offense. When she said that, I was so embarrassed; I was really shocked. But I immediately thanked her for having the courage to say that.
Her complaint reassured me that people who work for me aren't afraid of me.

Martin Edelston
President
Boardroom Inc.

I invented this system called l-Power. The original suggestion for it came from my old friend Peter Drucker. He suggested that I could improve the quality of my meetings if I asked my employees to bring two suggestions to every meeting on how they could work more effectively. Now we have suggestion boxes all over our offices, and they do bring about a level of truth. Every suggestion is reviewed, and considered. And there's an incentive. Ten dollars for an "A" idea. Five dollars for a "B." And $100 for the best suggestion of the month.
One of our most famous suggestions came from one of the low-level people who suggested that we try to get under the 4-pound postal rate. I didn't even know that a 4-pound rate existed. I checked it out, and found out that if we made our books 1/8 of an inch smaller, we could get under that 4-pound rate. We saved more than half a million dollars a year.

Jules Kroll
Chairman
Kroll Associates

Don't go into a question of veracity naked. Be prepared for the question you're posing. I don't believe in going on intuition. There's nothing like the facts, knowledge. You've got to have that to measure the truth of people's responses. And understand the person you're dealing with, what they do for a living, their age, what they like to do and where they come from.

Robert Crawford, Jr.
President and Chief Executive
Brook Furniture Leasing & Sales

To get the truth you've got to do all you've got do to get to the source. When I got the word that people in one of our stores were unhappy with the manager, I dropped by that store. I could feel a raw tone among the 12 or 14 people there. I asked them: "Is there anything I can do to help you?" You know what it was in this particular instance? A picnic. They were supposed to have this family picnic in October, but it got canceled. I said, "Why don't we just have a Christmas party for you instead? We'll pay for it." And it seemed to lift them right up, just like that.
