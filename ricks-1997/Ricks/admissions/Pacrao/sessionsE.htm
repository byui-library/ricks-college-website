Session E1

DIPSCAM Revisited

Facilitator:
Robert Tufts, Portland State University

Recorder Name:
Joseph St. Hilaire, Western Washington University

Presenter:
Allen Ezell, Assistant Vice President for Investigative Audits - First Union Corporation

The history of falsified college credentials and diplomas goes back more than 100 years in the U.S.  The first Congressional hearing into diploma mills were in 1924.  The FBI first got involved in fraud investigations in 1981 when Allan Ezell was assigned full-time on the task of closing down the mill of South-Eastern University. He then discovered, however, that such practices are nationwide, indeed worldwide, and he conducted his investigations until his retirements from the FBI recently.

A diploma mill is any organization that awards degrees or other educational credentials without any educational requirements for the purpose of financial gain.  Such fraudulent activity threatens legitimate educational institutions because it cheapens a college degree, threatens confidence in higher education, and does harm to the prestige of our degrees abroad.  

With the arrival of the Internet and web sites, diploma mills, have proliferated and pursuing them has become more difficult.  Some operations are grossing millions of dollars.

Title 18, Section 2320 and 1028 of the U.S. Code are the federal laws prohibiting fraudulent diplomas and identification.  Colleges and Universities should be vigilant in watching for fraudulent documents and they should be reported to legal authorities.

Session E2

Raising the Bar

Facilitator:
Damon Blue, California Lutheran University

Recorder Name:
Wayne VanEllis, Chapman University

Presenters:
John Reed, Linfield College
Dan Preston, Linfield College

This session addressed the issues that exist between the admissions office and the financial aide office.  Although the work these two offices do have an impact on the other, they do not communicate.  Instead they continue to work independently of each other.

What John Reed proposed at his college was to have a working relationship between the two offices, thus eliminating the miscommunication and hostility that usually exists between these two offices.

He has found that by having a common goal the offices can better serve the students and the institution.

Session E4

Counseling Students in Academic Difficulty

Facilitator:
Tika Esler, Bellevue Community College

Recorder Name:
Marie Wutzke, Pacific Lutheran College

Presenters:
Tim Cook, Clark College
Dan Fortmiller, Portland State University
Adrienne Wolf-Lockett, Reed College

This session focused on presenting profiles of students who encounter academic difficulties and what to do for them.

The presenters noted that in general it is seldom a student's academic ability that is the biggest problem but rather a lack of time management skills and discipline.

Each presenter gave examples of the intervention methods at their institutions: letters, notices, warnings, advising, and so forth.  The difference perhaps is that each school is implementing intervention much sooner because the record proves that with passive intervention (warning letters only) students tend to believe that they can improve without assistance or with simply "try harder next term."  They seldom seek advice, tutoring or counseling without direct invitation or imperative demand.

In addition to quicker intervention each school placed more stringent requirements upon students to be able to continue, for example requiring a written appeal and a contract outlining the nature of the student's difficulty and the strategies needed to overcome the difficulties.

Session E5

Web Services Washington State

Presenters:
Richard Backes, Washington State University
Karl Ibsen, Washington State University

The Registrar's Office at Washington State felt the need to improve the connection between their records and their clients, the faculty and departments.  Their solution was in in-house developed system called RONet that runs on a server in the Registrar's Office.

This session provided a history and overview of the problems of the technical and system details and other development details.  We learned how Washington State uses the web to provide staff and faculty to connect with the information class lists, student schedules and program lists on a 24-7 basics.  The Registrar's Office is also able to find out about administrative enrollment transactions, drops, adds, section changes, etc.  RONet provides two-way information between staff, faculty, and the Registrar's Office - client server with fares.  Washington State is collecting final grades during the season on a 24-hour basis.

The demonstration of RONet was a convincing example of Web Services in action that speeds and simplifies everyday life for faculty and staff at Washington State, which is always good news for students.

Session E8

Orientation for International Students

Facilitator: Annie Goodell, Yavapai College

Recorder Name:  
Molly Munger, Northern Arizona University

Presenter:  
MaryLee Carter, Director, Programs for Foreign Students and Scholars, Thunderbird, The American Graduate School of International Management. 

Orientation often can be the event that determines a student's success and continuation in an academic career, especially the international student.

The purpose of international orientation is three fold:  

  1. To facilitate students to life in a new culture and to help them understand the difference in values and the educational system.  

  2. To meet their immediate needs and to learn about basics (i.e. where to eat, and sleep, how to get around town, where to go for driver's license, etc.)

  3. To help them develop a support group that will replace the group they have left.  How to make friends and how to establish themselves in the community.

It is important to assist the new international students in developing their core group together and then non-international students can be incorporated in the orientation and campus get acquainted activities.

It is also very important to educate the university staff about the foreign students needs, learning styles, and sensitivities.

The orientation program should give guidance, information, assurance, and comfort, and should include the following areas:

  1. A component dealing with immigration matters.  

  1. Academic advising, student life, opportunities for leadership as well as volunteer activities in the community.  

  2. Life in the community and what's unique about the area in which they live.

  3. Any social or cultural concerns they might have.

  4. How to find resources on and off campus.

  5. Break out groups to discuss life scenarios (i.e. plagiarism).  This is more effective than talking to the entire orientation group.
