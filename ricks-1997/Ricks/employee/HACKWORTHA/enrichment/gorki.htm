The Beautiful Naivete of Maxim Gorki

One of Russia's greatest dramas has been the toppling of the Tsarist regime. Showing dynamic character contrasts, cowardly yet brave, poor yet affluent, tyrannical yet devoted to freedom, hateful yet loving, stupid yet alert, obscene yet delicate, repressed yet emotional, the Russian people have written a remarkable, painful, and bloody plot.

Their suffering has been immeasurable, as for almost ninety years the growing unrest and protests of oppressed Tsarist Russian kept jails full, occupied the executioners, and shook old world values. It is estimated that within a 70 year period more than 800,000 people were exiled and forced to trudge into the frozen lands of Siberia.

While freedom fighters fought to break the Tsar's grip of economic and political slavery, additional problems and stresses were caused by the Russian Industrial revolution. This revolution produced intense agony as people moved from their farms to the cities to form spontaneous ghettos, spawned by the poorly-oiled chains of industry.

However, starting like a gentle rain which builds gradually into a black, ominous, violent storm, the powers of discontent and revolt grew in Russia. Eventually, by 1907 the great logy bear had raised its drooping head and a new consciousness was born as Russia began to articulate its sources of pain.

During these bitter times of exploitation, suffering poverty, and the pains inherent in a knowledge of good and evil, noble men spoke of a better life using such words as: brotherhood, justice, peace, love, comradeship, and freedom. These men yearned for the death of poverty, hate, tyranny, and violence.

A story of the early revolution, its ideals, struggles, failures, and dedication is told by Maxim Gorki in the novel, Mother. Pavel Vlasov, the son, totally dedicates his life to the revolution. His mother. Nilovna Vlasov, gradually comes to appreciate and support her son’s actions and beliefs.

Nilovna is a personification of all Russia, innocent, abused, beaten, ignorant and yet innately capable. Time, however, which shares experiences and wisdom with Mother Nilovna, gradually teaches Nilovna a new awareness. Consequently, Nilovna sees a vision of the truth and adopts the role of a revolutionary.

Being moved by the pains of the proletariat, the reader cheers for Nilovna and Pavel and hopes for their success. The revolution is just; the workers are noble. Read their lofty sentiments:

There will come a time, I know, when people will take delight in one another, when each will be like a star to the other, and when each will listen to his fellow as to music. The free men will walk upon the earth, men great in their freedom.

They will walk with open hearts, and the heart of each will be pure of envy and greed, and therefore all mankind will be without malice, and there will be nothing to divorce the heart from reason . . . (185).

Then we shall live in truth and freedom and in beauty, . . . (185).

Those will be the best who will be the freest; for in them is the greatest beauty . . . (185).

. . . We maintain that a society that regards man only as a tool for its enrichment is anti-human .... We want to fight, and will fight, every form of the physical and moral enslavement of man by such a society . . . (451).

The word of my son is the honest word of a workingman, of an unsold soul. You will recognize its incorruptibility by its boldness. It is fearless, and if necessary it goes even against itself to meet the truth. It goes to you, working people, incorruptible, wise, fearless.

Receive it with an open heart, feed on it; it will give you the power to understand everything, to fight against everything for the truth, for the freedom of mankind. Receive it, believe it, go with it toward the happiness of all the people, to a new life with great joy (497).

The desires are beautiful; nevertheless, this writer is struck with the great disparity between the honorable goals, slogans, and statements, showing unfeigned sensitivity and love for one's fellow Russians, and the ugly, brutal conduct and mandates of later communist demagogs.

While the well-deserved revolution of 1907 almost produced a democratic nation, by 1917 the chances for democracy were robbed from the Russian people by machine - gun - slinging, intelligent, hard-core, communist leaders such as Lenin, Trotsky, and Stalin.

These bitter tyrants, with cunning skill, ripped the new, tender, democratic heart from Russia's bosom. The brutal Bolsheviks replaced an indifferent Tsar with a disciplined iron fist that smashed all jaws which did not rhythmically clack with the party's slogans.

Lenin's muscle, the Red Army, was developed and strengthened by Trotsky. Lenin also used the terror tactics of the Cheka secret police to crush all opponents. By 1917 the final civil war, the anti - revolution which wrestled power and leadership away from the provisional government, brought destruction and death to many Russians.

Then under the Bolsheviks in 1922, 33 million Russians starved and five million died because of ineffective socialism and severe drought. Later the rude, strutting destroyer, Joseph Stalin, took control of the Russian empire at the death of Lenin in 1924. This butcher purged the communist party and slaughtered all allegedly disloyal party workers.

Wanting to put the party's Five-Year Plan into effect, Stalin felt resistance from the Kulaks, a class of prosperous peasants. Therefore, Stalin ordered the complete genocidal liquidation of all. the Kulaks. Consequently, thousands more of the Russian people were slaughtered in an effort to more quickly establish a beautiful world of brotherhood. Stalin would save his people by force.

Seventy three-years after the publication of Mother we sorrowfully see that the vision of Maxim Gorki, Pavel, Nilovna, and other noble idealists has been violently betrayed.  Feeling "the means justifies the end," the hard-core communists are as dogs returning  to their vomit. Their behavior has proven to be more evil, tyrannical, and anti-human than that of the aristocratic Tsars.

The great mental illness of the world, force, has shown that Russia, of all countries, is most sick. The communists justify their violations of human rights by reminding the world that when full communism is finally achieved, the need for an oppressive government will dissolve.

Men and women, in all their grandeur, then will be totally alive; poverty and baseness will die. Men and women will treat each other with respect and kindness; the world will "live happily ever after." This state, however, must be hurried along by force and revolution.

In heaven the devil asked God for a chance to force men to be moral and productive but God rejected Satan's proposal and kicked him and his angels out of heaven. The devil has now taught his plan to the communists.

Believing that the goals of brotherhood, peace, wealth and freedom could be achieved by the socialist revolutionaries perhaps was more likely in 1907. But after observing the established patterns and the present directions of the 20th century Russian communists, it seems that Gorki's beautiful optimism is dreadfully naive.
