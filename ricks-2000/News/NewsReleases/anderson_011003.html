October 3, 2001

BYU-Idaho staff member 

braves Mount Kilimanjaro


REXBURG, Idaho – When BYU-Idaho is hiring a new staff member, applicants naturally undergo a series of interviews to determine their ability and attitude.  From now on, maybe a new question should be added.  “Have you ever done something to put you on par with Superman?”

            Maybe not everyone could answer that question in the affirmative, but at least one current member of the BYU-Idaho staff could.  His name is Steve Andersen – and he just climbed Mount Kilimanjaro.

            Steve was born in St. Anthony, on May 13, 1948.  But there was something different about him.  He was born with cerebral palsy.

            Cerebral palsy is not an illness or a disease.  It is the result of an injury to the head sometime before, during or soon after birth.  Because of the injury, or possibly an infection, the blood supply to the newborn’s brain is affected, and the child will grow without complete control of his or her muscles.  There are differing degrees of severity with cerebral palsy, from damage causing a noticeable limb to severe damage restricting the individual to a wheelchair for life.  For many, this can be a challenge that will sharply limit their activities for the rest of their lives.  But not for Steve.

            For starters, Steve graduated from high school.  Then he went on to Ricks College and Brigham Young University to receive his bachelor’s and master’s degrees, in applied sociology and educational psychology, respectively.  For the next several years of his life, Steve worked as a caretaker for many people.  Most of them were severely handicapped, either physically or mentally, most even more than himself.  Then Steve came to Ricks in 1984.

            Steve started the Adaptive Services for Adventure Program.  This program is designed to give experiences to disabled or handicapped students who normally would not have had them.  Some of the regular activities include horseback riding, skiing and canoeing.  He continues to take anywhere from 5 to 35 students with him as often as possible.

            All of this is very impressive, but what in world could put Steve on par with Superman?  How about climbing one of the tallest mountains in the world – with cerebral palsy?

            Around the end of August, friend and avid mountain climber Tom Whittaker invited Steve to join an expedition to climb Mt. Kilimanjaro.  The mountain is located in the northeastern tip of Tanzania, an African country about twice the size of California located below Kenya on the east African coast.  Kilimanjaro is known as one of the Seven Summits.  The Seven Summits are made up of the highest point on each continent, such as Mount Everest and Mount Denali.  There are also seven different ascents to reach the summit of the infamous mountain; Steve’s group, which included a film crew and artist, took the most difficult.

            At first, Steve was animated about the adventure.  “I was very excited to get a chance like this,” he said.

His wife, Shirley, added with a laugh, “But every day closer to the departure date, he was a little more reluctant to leave.  So finally I said, ‘Go on!  Go climb that mountain.  You’ll never get a chance like this again!’”  And so Steve went.

            The group flew out of the United States Sept. 10, the day before the terrorist attacks in New York City and Washington, D.C.  Because of their flight schedule, no one in the group knew anything about the attacks.  But when they arrived in Tanzania, the entire group was forced to purchase protective visas, and then they were put under house arrest.  Whittaker, the team leader, was arrested and questioned until it was safely determined who they were.  Twelve hours later the group was released, still having no idea why they had been arrested.

            “Finally, a little old English lady told me,” Steve recounts.  “I was shocked and very sad.  I wanted to come home.”

            But after talking to Shirley, Steve and the rest of the group decided to stay and accomplish the task they had given themselves.  They began their ascent of the mountain Sept. 14.

            Kilimanjaro stands 19,340 feet above sea level.  At its base, it is surrounded by plains and sunny weather in the 80’s.  Ascending the mountain, the climate changes while passing several different types of terrain.  At the top the wind howls and whips up snowstorms almost on a daily basis.

The ascent that Steve was climbing is the most technically demanding.  This was not a simple walk up a mountain.  It was the type of climb that most people prepare for year after year.  Steve, who had previously climbed such mountains as the Tetons, the tallest of which stands about 13,747 feet above sea level, had two weeks to prepare.

            At first, everything went well.  Steve used specially designed collapsible ski poles to pull himself up the mountain, using his arms as much as his legs.  Where it was possible, two of the team members would hold two stout poles between them, stretcher-like, creating a moving banister for Steve to use as he climbed along with them.  But never, for one step of the way, did Steve ask for anyone to carry his load.

            “He would send me e-mails, and it seemed that every day he was over there, he was more and more determined to make it to the top,” Shirley said.

            “I was so excited to be there.  The Tanzanians are so friendly, loving and beautiful people.  Strangers would come up to me and say, ‘Way to go, Steve.  You’re a good man,’” Steve said.

            And foot by foot, using ropes and harnesses at the most difficult points, Steve and the rest of the group made their way toward the top of great Mount Kilimanjaro.

            Once climbers reach more than 10,000 feet, the oxygen becomes thin enough to make climbing more difficult, and much more dangerous.  As the atmospheric pressure lessens, there are less oxygen particles per breath for the body to absorb.  It is not uncommon for climbers to develop Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS), an illness that can include severe headaches and exhaustion.  For anyone, much less someone with Steve’s medical condition, it would be a difficult challenge to overcome.

A common remedy for AMS is to use a chemical supplement called Diamox that prevents the development of AMS.  Steve’s group began to use Diamox as they climbed, and so did Steve.

“That’s when I got really sick,” said Steve.  “The Diamox didn’t go over well at all with my body.”

            Then, finally, at 17,500 feet, a mere 1,840 feet from the summit, Steve had to stop.  “I finally admitted to myself that I didn’t have the energy to go on.  I was so close!” he said, unconsciously clinching one fist.

            “This was definitely a case where the spirit was willing, but the flesh was just too weak,” Shirley chimed in.

            Accompanied by one of the more experienced climbers in the group, Steve descended the mountain and made preparations to come home.

            “I am very happy to be home, and I feel good,” says Steve.

            “I’m glad that he made it back safe,” Shirley adds.  “And I’m very proud of him.  He’s never backed down from a challenge in his life.”

            Climbing 17,500 feet under these circumstances probably doesn’t qualify as a failure to very many people.

            In what way could Steve Andersen compare to Superman?  Well, the Man of Steel may have bulging biceps and unlimited strength, especially compared to Steve Andersen; but Steve Andersen has an iron will and determination that would make any superhero proud.
