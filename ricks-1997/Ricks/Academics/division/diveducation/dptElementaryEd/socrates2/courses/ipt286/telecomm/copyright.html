Copyright Guidelines

Copyright laws are extremely important in our society and it is important, especially as teachers, to follow those laws. Copyright laws not only apply to print and audiovisual works but they also include computer programs, and other technological devices.

In 1974, Congress developed the National Commission on New Technological Uses of Copyright Works (CONTU). This commission has been charged with dtermining how computer programs should be protected by copyright laws. In 1980, CONTU came up with the following guildelines:

It is not a copyright infringement to make or authorize the making of another copy or adaptation of a computer program provided that:

  1. The new copy or adaptation is created as an essential step in the use of the computer program in conjunction with a machine and that it is used in no other manner, or
  2. The new copy or adaptation is for archival purposes only and that all archival copies are destroyed in the event that continued possession of the computer program should cease to be rightful.

However, in every case, the provisions of what can be done with a piece of software are governed by the license agreement that accompanies the software. It superceeds any and all verbal agreements or other understandings concerning the software.

Software

Software purchased for school uses may only be used at the school. It cannot be taken to a private home for any sort of use, not even the teacher can take it home to figure out how to use it. Also, if a school buys 7 copies of a software program, it may only be loaded to 7 computers at the school. This can be changed, but only by negotiating agreements with the software company. The normal rule is 1 copy of the software for each machine. This is not the case when lab packs or site licenses are purchased.

Computer Programs

Computer programs are considered to be the same as literary works. As you know, you cannot copy any literary work word for word, nor can you copy the idea without citing it. Computer programs are the same. It is important in this aspect of copyright that the "idea" is distinguished from the "expression" of the idea. One example of this was the case of Lewis Galoob toys, inc. v. Nintendo of America, Inc. Lewis Galoob Toys made the "Game Genie," used with Nintendo to alter features of the game. In this case, Nintendo argued that the Game Genie is a derivative work of Nintendo. They lost the case, however, because the Game Genie simply enhanced the audiovisual displays, but it did not recreate the game. This illustrates the importance of distinguishing the "idea" from the "expression" of the idea.

Multimedia Copyright

Multimedia productions usually include much more than just text. They usually combine text, graphics, still images, animation, audio and video and more in a single presentation. Many people use these things to create their own multimedia productions. There has been much concern about guidelines for this sort of copyright, and groups such as Consortium of College and University Media Centers and the Association of American Publishers, and other software publishers and education organizations have been working for several years to agree on fair use guidelines. Electronic Library Reserve

Many college instructors place materials on reserve in libraries so that students can access them at any time. They may place printed copies of book chapters and journal articles and other items of interest to students on library reserve through the Internet. This becomes a problem because now professors can just scan printed material into the computer and the information can be given out over the networks to anyone interested.


Guidelines for electronic reserve now ask that anything such as journal articles, book chapters, and excerpts from longer works be only a small proportion of the assigned readings for a course and must include copyright warnings. Also, the instructor would have to require permission from the person who holds the copyright to use the materials for more than one semester. The last requirement would be that only students of that particular instructor could access these reserve items. They would do this by having a particular password. These rules are not yet in action, but are still being considered by various committees.

Electronic Publishing and Copyright

There is much concern from authors, publishers and users on how they can protect their items with copyright. It is important to remember that even materials on the Internet that are not registered with the Copyright Office, the materials are still protected by copyright. The only way this changes is if the author specifically states differently.

Conclusion

There is bound to be many changes in copyright laws because technology is advancing so quickly. It is important for educators especially to stay on top of these changes. Technological achievement will keep many on their toes for new copyright laws.
