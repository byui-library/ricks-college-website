Copyright Guidelines

Copyright laws are extremely important in our society and it is important, especially as teachers and students, to understand and abide by those laws. Copyright laws not only apply to print and audiovisual works but they also include computer programs, and other technological devices.

In 1974, Congress developed the National Commission on New Technological Uses of Copyright Works (CONTU). This commission has been charged with determining how computer programs should be protected by copyright laws. In 1980, CONTU came up with the following guidelines:

It is not a copyright infringement to make or authorize the making of another copy or adaptation of a computer program provided that:

  1. The new copy or adaptation is created as an essential step in the use of the computer program in conjunction with a machine and that it is used in no other manner, or
  2. The new copy or adaptation is for archival purposes only and that all archival copies are destroyed in the event that continued possession of the computer program should cease to be rightful.

However, in every case, the provisions of what can be done with a piece of software are governed by the license agreement that accompanies the software. It supercedes any and all verbal agreements or other understandings concerning the software. All computer programs have a license agreement, regardless of format (disk, CD or downloadable).

Software

Software purchased for school uses may only be used at the school. It cannot be taken to a private home for any sort of use, not even a teacher can take it home to figure out how to use it. Also, if a school buys 7 copies of a software program, it may only be loaded to 7 computers at the school. This can be changed, but only by negotiating agreements with the software company. The normal rule is 1 copy of the software for each machine. This is not the case when lab packs or site licenses are purchased.

Computer Programs

Computer programs are considered to be the same as literary works when it comes to copyright. A literary work cannot be copied word for word, nor can an individual copy the idea without citing it. Computer programs are the same. It is important in this aspect of copyright that the "idea" is distinguished from the "expression" of the idea. One example of this was the case of Lewis Galoob toys, inc. v. Nintendo of America, Inc. Lewis Galoob Toys made the "Game Genie," used with Nintendo to alter features of the game. In this case, Nintendo argued that the Game Genie is a derivative work of Nintendo. They lost the case, however, because the Game Genie simply enhanced the audiovisual displays, but it did not recreate the game. This illustrates the importance of distinguishing the "idea" from the "expression" of the idea.

Multimedia Copyright

Multimedia productions usually include much more than just text or graphics. They usually combine text, graphics, still images, animation, audio and video and more in a single presentation. Many people use these things to create their own multimedia productions. There has been much concern about guidelines for this sort of copyright, and groups such as Consortium of College and University Media Centers and the Association of American Publishers, and other software publishers and education organizations have been working for several years to agree on fair use guidelines. An overview of their guidelines can be viewed at: "Fair Use guidelines for Educational Media " (This link requires the use of Adobe's PDF Reader).

Electronic Publishing and Copyright

There is much concern from authors, publishers and users on how they can protect their items with copyright. It is important to remember that even materials on the Internet that are not registered with the Copyright Office, the materials are still protected by copyright. The only way this changes is if the author specifically states differently.

Conclusion

There is bound to be many changes in copyright laws because technology is advancing so quickly. This document addresses only electronic resources. Readers must realize that all forms of expression, including print, audio and artwork are also protected.

It is important for everyone, especially educators and students, to stay on top of these changes. Technological achievement will force on-going changes to copyright laws. But for now, the typical user of of any resource: print, audio and electronic, should follow four simple rules:

  1. If the item states that it is not copyrighted and may be distributed freely, then the user may use it without hesitation.
  2. If the item states that it may be used following specific guidelines, then the user must follow those guidelines.
  3. If the resource states that it is copyrighted, then it should only be used within the "Fair Use" guidelines or after obtaining permission from the copyright holder.
  4. If the resource is silent concerning copyright, then the user should assume that it is copyrighted and follow rule number 3 above.
