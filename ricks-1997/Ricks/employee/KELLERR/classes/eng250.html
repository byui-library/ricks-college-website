Contemporary World Literature

R. Keller

 

Most people like to read, but only if they can read what they want when they want. But once someone assigns a reading and a due date, most of the enjoyment leaves. It even gets less enjoyable when readers are forced to find things in their readings that they can't see. The primary purpose of this course is to make reading an enjoyable and valuable experience by concentrating on some great literature. This is not an in-depth, majors-only course, but a survey course to expose often hesitant readers to the relationship literature can have with their lives. Although the ideal way is to let students read whatever they want, whenever they want, it is, however, a chaotic classroom approach, so there will be assigned readings. The readings will hopefully be enjoyable (I realistically know they all won't be), but more importantly, I hope the readings will be a springboard for discussions of how we can improve our lives by what we read. I also hope that as we read the works reflecting some of the world's most significant minds and talents we learn to appreciate and respect these people individually; although, their attitudes often do not reflect our own. I also want students to establish their own intelligent standards to judge whether writing is good, bad, or worth their time. This is possible, and it is enjoyable.

Course Objectives:

-- To become acquainted with contemporary respected writers who have illuminated our human experience.

-- To understand the common forms of literature--fiction, drama, poetry, essay.

-- To learn the critical vocabulary that describes literature.

-- To distinguish between sentiment and sentimentality and between escape and interpretive literature.

-- To examine the ways meaning emerges from a literary work.

-- To become excited about the realm of ideas opened to the person who reads.

-- To enjoy reading.

Writing Assignments

-- Reading journal responses (20). These are approximately 250-word mini essays concerning your personal reactions to readings. The twenty journal entries will consist of a minimum of three responses demonstrating each of four chief writing aims: writing to reflect, writing to compare, writing to explicate, and writing to analyze. Two revised responses will be graded individually.

Your writing responses may be informal--explorations of your ideas. Often the questions have no right or wrong answer, but rather challenge you to read a text carefully and then interpret it against your own experience and understanding.

To keep track of your responses in an orderly way, please enumerate, name (use the author of the work you are analyzing for your title), and date each response in the upper right-hand corner of your paper--for example: Response #1, "Hawthorne," January 17, 2000, Writing to Explicate. (The date should correspond to the class period when the work will be discussed.) Please begin each writing response on a separate sheet of paper. These responses need not be typed, but please write legibly. At the end of the semester I will collect your responses and mark them.

Grades

Grading is based on the percentage of total points earned: one mid-term (100 points); quizzes (daily, 10-15 points each); reading journal responses (20 at 5 points each); four revised journal entries (25 points each); one literary concept final (100 points); one comprehensive final (100 points).

Course Content

AGreat literature is the artistic expression of complex human experience in language, rendered by insightful writers. Our object in Ricks College English courses is to develop minds and spirits through texts that have the potential to enrich and enable. Just as daily life teaches and refines us through experience with opposites, so literature often works by means of conflict. Because great literature treats inherently difficult moral, philosophical, and social issues, it sometimes confronts us with what is tragic and ugly in human experience along with what is hopeful and beautiful. In contrast, poorly written works tend to present human experience in shallow, simplistic, and sentimental ways. Some literature judged by scholars to have merit realistically or figuratively depicts life in language which may, paradoxically, be either beautiful or offensive or both. Nevertheless, literature should not be judged merely by its diction, subject matter, or contents of isolated passages. Ultimately, great writing should teach readers to understand and appreciate the dignified, empathetic, and artistically skilled rendering of complex human experience.@
