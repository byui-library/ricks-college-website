Graham: The woman who came to embody American modern dance

by Patricia Selman
SEL00001@BYUI.EDU
Scroll Staff

Martha Graham, often hailed as the mother of modern dance. AP Photo Archive
Modern dance is one of the most original and recent types of fine art, and Martha Graham is often thought of as the inventor of it.

“Did she invent modern dance? No, but she came to embody it, arrogantly and spectacularly — and permanently,” according to Time Magazine. “When the legend becomes fact, print the legend. The legend of Martha Graham long ago became fact.”

Before any type of theatrical debut, Martha Graham was a young girl who attended a performance of Ruth St. Denis, an American contemporary dance innovator who influenced almost every phase of American dance, according to the Encyclopedia Britannica. Graham went on to dance at St. Denis’ school.

“I went to the theatre, the Mason Opera House ... [and] that night my fate was sealed,” Graham said.

Graham’s fate was to become the innovator of dance techniques that “are part of the air every contemporary dancer breathes,” according to Time.

In 1926 she gave her first recital in New York.

“The first concert was held at the 48th Street Theatre. ... There was an audience. They came because I was such a curiosity — a woman who could do her own work,” Graham said.

Through the years there were high and low points, as in 1936 when she refused the invitation to dance at the Olympic Games in Berlin.

“So many artists whom I respect and admire have been deprived of the right to work for ridiculous and unsatisfactory reasons, that I should consider it impossible to identify myself...with the regime that has made such things possible.” She said in a reply letter to the Olympic committee.

In 1937 she danced at the White House for President and Mrs. Roosevelt. She has since danced for seven other presidents.

In 1970 Graham stopped dancing.

“A dancer, more than any other human being, dies two deaths: the first, the physical when the powerfully trained body will no longer respond as you would wish ... I knew. And it haunted me. I only wanted to dance. Without dancing, I wished to die,” Graham said.

After many years of choreographing such works as Lamentation (1930) and Errand into the Maze (1947) after having her homegrown technique with fierce pelvic contractions and floor work placed into the minds of dancers, Graham died April 1, 1991.

“The center of the stage is where I am,” Graham said.
