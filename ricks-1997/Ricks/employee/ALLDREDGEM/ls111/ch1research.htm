Information

Information includes a variety of data which has been collected. Some information has not been recorded. Directories will help locate sources of unrecorded information. We each collect information but may not record it. Information has many characteristics.

Characteristics of information

  • Factual information is data that is often requested on multiple choice quizzes. Trivia and game show questions show the variety of facts that may be requested. Sources of simple facts include dictionaries, atlases, handbooks, and directories.
  • Analytical information is critical thinking which is required for essay questions. Critical reviews provide analytical information about literature, movies, and current events. Sources of  analytical information include encyclopedias, some handbooks, and overview or context sources.
  • Objective information is the balanced and unbiased answers that we expect from scholarly work. Statistics can gather observations that are part of inductive research.  From case studies the researcher inductively reasons that general principles exist.
  • Subjective information has its place to persuade us. Editorials are usually subjective. Surveys and statistics can be used by two opposing groups based on the interpretation given to the facts. Questions to ask about subjective information include:
      • Is the author's opinion better due to the presentation or information?
      • Opposing Views sources will present both sides of controversies.
      • Does one side rely more on emotion or logic?
      • Is the interpretation of  facts accurate?
  • Original or primary information sources are popular with genealogy and history. Primary, secondary, tertiary, and finding aids are needed for all fields of study. Rumors and gossip show how diluted information can become the farther removed it gets from its source.
    1. The first appearance of information is the original or primary source.
    2. Secondary sources are translations, edited versions, or interpretations.
    3. Tertiary or finding aids are sources that lead to secondary or primary sources.
      • Tertiary sources are based on secondary sources.
      • Indexes, catalogs, and bibliographies are finding aids for secondary or primary sources.
      • Access tools or finding aids may not contain any usable facts only a reference to a source.
  • Information comes in a wide variety of formats.
    1. Unpublished sources are researched by interviews and correspondence.
    2. Internet sources may be subject to frequent changes in format or content.
    3. Nonprint sources include videocassettes, audiocassettes, microform,  and other formats.
    4. Printed sources have built in ways to determine authority.
      • Primary sources or archival records may be microfilmed or digitized.
      • Digital format is an electronic or scanned format. Adobe Acrobatic Reader is software used to view some digital sources like government forms which the author wants it to retain the printed format. 
      • The publication description helps identify reliable collections of information. Seals and embossing are used on legal documents to verify their source.
      • Scholarly work goes through a review and editing process.
      • Quarterly publication have more time for editing and review than weekly periodicals.
      • Government sources have built in authority for legal research.

Research Methods

Many different methods of academic research are used.
    Deductive research moves from general principles to specific recommendations. 
  • Reviews of existing research using library resources are deductive.
  • The English 111 research unit using Background Study Guides are deductive.
    Inductive research moves from specific case studies to general observations. 
  • Laboratory work and weather observations are inductive.
  • Surveys like the Gallup poll and census are used for inductive research.
  • Statistical research is often inductive.

Research for an assignment

Many libraries use the Big Six to help students with research.

1. Task Definition 
1.1 Define the information problem 
1.2 Identify information needed in order to complete the task (to solve the information
problem) 

2. Information Seeking Strategies 
2.1 Determine the range of possible sources (brainstorm) 
2.2 Evaluate the different possible sources to determine priorities (select the best sources) 

3. Location and Access 
3.1 Locate sources (intellectually and physically) 
3.2 Find information within sources 

4. Use of Information 
4.1 Engage (e.g., read, hear, view, touch) the information in a source 
4.2 Extract relevant information from a source 

5. Synthesis 
5.1 Organize information from multiple sources 
5.2 Present the information 

6. Evaluation 
6.1 Judge the product (effectiveness) 
6.2 Judge the information problem-solving process (efficiency) 

Choosing a topic (step 1 in Big 6)

    Choose a topic to fit available information & stay within the guidelines of the assignment. Questions to consider include:
  • Does the library collect information on the topic?
    • Libraries collect material for its purposes. We collect local information and materials to support the curriculum. Ricks College does not have the marine biology information that BYU-Hawaii would.
  • Will this topic take more time or resources than are available? Some topics are too popular to have books available.
    • Procrastination may reduce time too close to the deadline for other options.
    Some topics requires original research.
    Some topics require telephone contacts, mail or Internet.
  • Are credible sources available?
    • Government agencies (Internet addresses with .gov)
    Educational institutions (Internet addresses with .edu)
    Non profit organizations (Internet addresses with .org)
  • Is the research too specialized to be successfully pursued now and here?
    • Tax publications and legal sources are examples of specialized research.

Information Seeking (step 2 in Big 6)

  • Place your topic within a discipline or fields of study.
  • Watch for related or more specific terms.
  • Use overview or analytical sources to identify facets of the topic. (Encyclopedias)
  • Watch for authorities and recommended sources.
  • Use access or finding aids to identify books, periodicals, or Internet sources.
  • Gather source descriptions as you go.
  • Analyze descriptions of sources to determine relevance to your topic.
  • Is the history or recent news only needed?

Selecting sources (step 3 in Big 6)

  • Information for disciplines and fields of study are grouped together in libraries.
  • Browse call numbers assigned to disciplines to identify sources.
  • Use the library catalog and other finding aids to locate sources in related fields.
  • Libraries often collect authoritative Internet links by subject.
  • The library catalog helps locate bibliographies, biographies, books, descriptions of countries, essays, ethnic history, presidential biographies & publications, travel information, and critical reviews.
  • Dictionaries provide information about abbreviations, definitions, geographical names, words, and quotations. General dictionaries for English language are found on stands throughout the library and under the call number PE. Specialized dictionaries are found under call numbers B to Z for various fields of study.
  • Encyclopedias provide background, bibliographies, biographies, country descriptions, definitions, ethnic history, government information, historical documents, statistics, outlines of knowledge, pictures, polictical data, presidents, travel information, time lines or dates, and analysis of why events are significant. General encyclopedias are found under call number AE. Specialized encyclopedias are found under call numbers B to Z for various fields of study.
  • Indexes are finding aids or access tools for biographies, news, essays, ethnic history, statistics, magazine articles, scholarly journal articles, political data, reviews of books or movies, quotations, authorship of literary works, and contents of collections. Many index databases also have the full text not just the reference to information.
  • Directories provide information about Internet and postal addresses, subject lists of organizations, and bibliographies of publications by subjects or organizations. Telephone directories and similar tools may include maps.
  • Handbooks, yearbooks, almanacs, and manuals provide information about addresses, biographies, countries, news, dates, government, statistics, famous people, occupations, political data, travel, and authorship.
  • Biographical sources are located under the call number CT and A to Z for biographies by discipline or field of study.
  • Geographical sources are located under the call number G and in the maps and atlas collections. Many disciplines and fields of study will also have geographical sources. Biological atlases will show where plants or animals or anatomical parts are found. History, travel and description for each state and country are located under call numbers D to F.

Note keeping (step 4 in Big 6)

    Research is more efficient when proper notes are taken.
  • A research log records the researchers efforts to engage a topic. A research log is not published as a finished product. A genealogy research log helps keep track of the sources and what information was being sought. Some researchers use the same sources again and again but for different surnames or events. An award winner scholar who visted our campus based her work on one journal. Her research found patterns that were supported by other primary sources. A research log can include
    • the steps taken (Write down the source, look in the index, look in the table of contents, etc.)
    • search terms (Look for maiden name, married name, name after second marriage, etc.)
    • results (The information that you wanted: birth, marriage, or death facts: when, where, who)
    • choices made to narrow or broaden (Sometimes you may see too many or not enough facts. How did you decide what was important enough to be recorded?)
  • Documentation is the description of the sources. Other researchers will judge your work by the accuracy of your documentation.
      • Books should have the following given:
    1. author,
    2. title,
    3. place of publication,
    4. publisher, and
    5. date.
      Periodicals should have the following given:
    1. author of the article,
    2. title of article,
    3. periodical title,
    4. volume,
    5. issue, and
    6. date.
      Footnotes or endnotes are documentation to specific pages with numbered references.
      Footnotes appear at the foot of the page.
      Endnotes are found at the end of the paper, chapters, or book.

 Synthesis (step 5 of Big 6)

Organize and present the information. Present your research in the format preferred by your audience. The Student Technology Center can help with the electronic tools, PowerPoint or Web Page presentations. The Writing Lab can help with the mechanics of written presentations. Regardless of the format certain standards of scholarship exist. 

Standards of documentation

      1. Can someone else find the same information?
        Web addresses need a date of creation or access. 
        Could your professor verify your access to information?.
      2. Is there a format preference? The most popular are:
        1. The Chicago Style Manual is exhaustive (over 900 pages) Kate Turbian has simplified it for students.
        2. MLA Style Manual or the Modern Language Assocation Manual is popular with English majors.
        3. APA or American Psychological Association style uses parenthetical quotes. Internet APA style guide
      3. Are you creating footnotes, endnotes, or bibliographies?
        Scholarly work shows authority. Credentials may be needed for quotes. Notes can explain or draw attention to scholarly work.
      4. Copyright and privacy issues need to be met for presentations before audiences. Many institutions have web page policies about copyright and permission.

Evaluation (step 6 of the Big 6)

Evaluation should be part of every step. If all evaluation is saved to the end, disappointment may result. If a choice is offered on a topic, do background research. Be sure the research will be successful before clearing a topic with an instructor. Evaluate sources as you use them. During organization and presentation, evaluations will need to be made. A review of the finished product is helpful. Habits of procrastination may have robbed the product of its success. Perhaps you did not do enough background research to be assured that the project would be successful.

  • Are the authors used authoritative?
  • Are the titles of the publications evidence of  scholarship?
  • Are statistics based on objective or subjective data?
  • Was your audience-- experts or peers? 
  • Were the number of sources within the assignment guidelines?
  • Were enough scholarly journals available?
