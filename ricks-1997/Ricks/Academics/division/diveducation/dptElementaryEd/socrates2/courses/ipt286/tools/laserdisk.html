Laser Disks represent a well established example of Optical Technology.

Laser disks were first mass marketed in the mid 1970's. They were primarily used for movies and entertainment, and competed directly with VHS and Beta video cassettes. The laser disks were superior in video imagery and audio clarity; however, they had to be turned over because of the limitation in data that could be held per side. Two sided laser disk players were not available, and, despite its technical superiority, laser disks were abandonded in favor of video cassettes.

In the last several years however, laser disks have seen a resurgence. You can now find LD's for both entertainment as well as training and education. To help you become more familiar with this technology, the following technical and comparison data is provided.

There are two types of laser disks: CAV and CLV. Both have advantages and disadvantages, and are used in different sectors of the audio/video market.

Technical Descriptions

CAV - Constant Analog Velocity

Organization: Chapter & Frame (can be addressed by frame or chapter number at random)
Size: 54,000 frames per side
Chapters: 80
Video Play Time: 30 minutes per side
Slide Equivalent: 1 LD equals a 13 story tall stack of slide projector trays
Control factor: Almost complete control possible

CLV - Constant Linear Velocity

Organization: Minutes and Seconds
Size: 54,000 frames per side
Video Play Time: 60 minutes per side
Equivalent: 1 LD (counting both sides) equals 1 video cassette tape (T-120)
Control factor: Limited control by the user.

Audio Capabilities


Laser disks, regardless of format, contain 2 audio tracks. These tracks can be used to provide stereo sound or can be used to hold two completely separate sound tracks. For example, one could contain music while the second contains narration. Both can be accessed independent of the other, or played simultaneously. Using a CAV disk, the audio track, like the frames and chapters, can be accessed completely at random and controlled by using an address.

Comparisons

CAV - Constant Analog Velocity

Because of the organization of the LD in this format, the LD can be used easily for training and education. Some of the advantages and disadvantages of a CAV disk are:

Pause and hold images indefinitely
Maximum control over images
Less than 5 second access to any frame from any other frame
Expensive ($100+)

CLV - Constant Linear Velocity

Because of the organization of the LD in this format, the LD is used primarily for entertainment purposes. Full length motion pictures can be imprinted on the disk, but again, it takes both sides to hold the movie. Some of the advantages and disadvantages of a CLV disk are:

Twice as much video capabilities as a CAV disk.
Inexpensive ($30 - $70)
Cannot pause and hold picture on screen

Control of a Laser Disk


There are primarily four references to the control level of a laser disk. Each is described below:

Level One


Level one control involves manipulating the laser disk by use of the controls built into the LD player, or by use of a remote control or barcode reader. Random access to the LD is the highest interactivity level possible.

Level Two


Computer program code is built into the LD. This is seldom seen and few level 2 disks exist.

Level Three


The LD player is attached to a computer. The computer is equipped with software that allows the user to control the LD player, and subsequently access and control all aspects of the disk.

Level Four


This level is still not well defined. In general it refers to anything above level 3. It may include a disk being controled via a touchscreen in a kiosk. The LD being used in simulation machines, such as flight simulators, in a completely interactive mode.
