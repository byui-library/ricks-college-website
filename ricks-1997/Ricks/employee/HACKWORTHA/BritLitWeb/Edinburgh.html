Edinburgh
{ed'-in-bur-uh}

Edinburgh is the capital and the second largest city in SCOTLAND, with a population of 438,000.   Edinburgh's site is marked by isolated hills, some of them volcanic, over and around which the city has spread. These include Arthur's Seat (823 ft) and Castle Rock, on which Edinburgh Castle was built. Edinburgh has cool summers; the average July temperature is 59 deg F. (41 deg F), but are sometimes marked by very cold winds off the North Sea.

    Central Edinburgh is dominated by Castle Rock, below which the old city with its narrow streets clustered. North of the rock lie Princes Street and the regularly planned 18th-century new city. Industrial development is primarily concentrated at Leith to the north. Although engineering and metalworking are important, Edinburgh is not primarily an industrial city. Its functions as Scotland's capital are not extensive, but these may grow if Scotland gains some further degree of autonomy in the future. Edinburgh is first and foremost a cultural and educational center. Its university, the largest in Scotland, was founded in 1583 and early became a famous center for medical studies. The city is also noted for its schools, galleries, and museums, and it has an important annual festival of music and drama. Its architectural monuments include the castle, the Gothic St. Giles' church, Holyrood Palace, and many Georgian town houses.
The name Edinburgh derives from the Celtic Dune-eideann, meaning "Eidin's Fort." The town grew up in the 12th century beneath the castle constructed there by Malcolm III (r. 1058- 93). It suffered in the wars with the English but became the Scottish capital in the 15th century. Edinburgh lost importance in 1603, when the Scottish and English crowns were united and the seat of government was moved to London. Its cultural preeminence emerged in the 18th century, when the new city was built, and Edinburgh became one of the intellectual centers of Europe.
