The small pieces of literature are often difficult to find. Reviews, short stories, plays, poems, and quotes are examples of small pieces of literature. Cataloging seldom covers small units of literature. Music and illustrations are other small parts of collections that may be difficult to find without an index.

A variety of indexes to literary and other collections have been developed to make the task easier. The H.W. Wilson Company has published several types of literary indexes as a result of its other indexing efforts. Other companies have created indexes to collections of illustrations and music.

Book Reviews

A review describes the material often without critical evaluation. The review is designed to inform prospective buyers or readers. Periodicals and newspapers often have sections with reviews of books, motion pictures, videos, and a variety of other entertainment opportunities.

H.W. Wilson periodical indexes has lists of book reviews in the back of the issue.

This section in the back of the index is arranged by author unless none is credited. An entry looks similar to other index references with similar abbreviations.
1. Keep track of what source listed the review.
Occasionally indexes make errors.
If you wish to obtain an article on interlibrary loan, you may be asked for your source.

The H.W. Wilson Company also publishes  Book Review Digest.

The main entry is under the author (or title, if no author is credited). A subject and title index in the back will give the author.

Anthologies

The Greeks collected literature into "anthologies". The word "anthology" is their word which means literally "flower gathering."
The word suggests that the best literature has been collected. Literature courses often use a text called "Readings..." which is another term used to describe collections of literature. Twayne's Masterwork studies is an example of an anthology of classic lterature.

Without indexes finding a poem, quote, short story, essay, play or speech would be difficult. Even with indexes a systematic search may take time, if the information is not available in several anthologies.

Concordance

A concordance is an index to all the unique words used in a book. Concordances to the Bible are widely used by ministers of various religions. When the LDS scriptures were printed using computerized type setting, all the words in the scriptures were placed in a computer database.

The Computerized Scriptures is available from the Salt Lake Distribution Center. The software is for IBM-DOS computer systems with 640k of internal memory and 12 megabytes of hard disk memory. At one time we had copies in the library.

Infobase is a commercial version of LDS scriptures with the full text of many Mormon gospel sources.
The CD-ROM program has help screens to explain how to search and mark results for printing.
All machines in the library have access to the 1997 edition of the CD-ROM.
The company has an Internet site with a concordance to the Ensign @ http://infobase.ldsworld.com/.
Fulltext of many of the Internet sites is available only to subscribers or those who own a copy of the CD-ROM.

The Church's official home page provides access to recent general conference speeches.
Internet resources has a link to http://www.lds.org.
General conferences has a link to http://www.lds.org/en/3_General_Conference_en/General_Conference_en.html

Literary classics, such as those by William Shakespeare, also have printed concordances.
A person wanted a poem by Robert Frost which contained the words "silver lizards". Using a concordance to Robert Frost's poems, the poem could then be found.

Short Story Index (Ref Z5917.S5C6)
Play Index (Ref Z5781.W48)
These H.W. Wilson indexes are similar to Essay and General Literature Index.
The main entry is by author.
If only the title of the play or short story is known, look at the title entry for the author which will be listed next to it.

Popular Music (Ref ML120.U5S5) provides song listings with indexes to lyricists, composers, performances, and awards. Knowing more about a song including when it won awards helps locate sheet music. The list, "Song Books Indexed" includes OCLC# which is helpful for locating or obtaining song books at other libraries. "W,M" are abbreviations used to show that both words and music are included.

 Internet

Internet resources has links to other sources for current reviews of MOVIES. The wide variety of resources that are in electronic format helps locate and reproduce small pieces of literature.
A right mouse click on an image may allow you to save the image for use in written presentations. Sending images on Internet or e-mail requires at least acknowledgements, if not permission. Copyright has new laws covering what may be transmitted by electronic means.
Search engines, such as Altavista allow searches of Internet. Altavista allows phrases to be searched by surrounding them with quotation marks: "I love you without knowing how, or when, or from where." A variety of postings from literary groups correctly identify the author. Pablo Neruda is the actual poet.
    Try http://www.altavista.com/
    +neruda +whitman +"i love you without knowing how"
    Use title: to limit the search to sites with Neruda in the title of the page.
    +title:neruda +"i love you without knowing how"
    Edit the search to narrow or enlarge the search. [On May 15, 2000 at 1p.m. the following search retrieved 7 pages of which half were from outside the United States.]
    +title:neruda +whitman
    Look at Internet addresses to evaluate the usefulness of the information.
    http://users.sti.com.br & www.irisz.hu & www.uca.edu.ni & www.sisib.uchile.cl are probably foreign addresses without looking at the content. The last letters of the address before the slash (/) identify the domain. If theses letters are not edu, com, mil, org, or gov, suspect a foreign site. For literary information "edu" and "org" may be more authoritative than "com."
    The address: "URL: 194.52.240.199/~poems/" does not tell us very much. Don't be surprised, if it cannot be retrieved later.
