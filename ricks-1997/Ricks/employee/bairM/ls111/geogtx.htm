Vocabulary

Cartography is the field that draws maps.
Books on map drawing could be found under the subject, CARTOGRAPHY.

Geography is the study of the earth's shape and features.
Classes in geography will provide more information than this brief summary.

A globe is a round representation of the earth's surface.
A map is the representation of the earth's surface, usually flat.
An atlas is a collection of maps.
The name "atlas" came from a picture of the mythological Atlas holding up the earth which was found on many early atlases.
Today atlases may also contain charts, tables, and other descriptions in addition to maps.

Atlases are often published by geographical organizations, newspapers, and government agencies.
National Geographic publishes an atlas and also includes maps in its magazines.
The London newspaper, Times publishes atlases.
The USGS or Geological Survey of the United States publishes many maps and atlases.
Maps stands and shelves near the main reference collection have oversize atlases.

  • National Geographic Atlas of the World (Atlas G1021.N38)
  • Times Atlas of the World (Atlas G1021.T55)
  • National Atlas of the U.S. (Atlas G1200.U57)
Central Intelligence Agency produced until recently the only reliable maps of areas in Communist nations.
An author keyword would show how many maps and atlases have been produced by this agency.
A general keyword search combining the agency and the country will produce efficient results.

Maps may be single sheets stored in the map cases on the first floor east wing.
The Geological Survey of the Department of Interior has produced maps for over a hundred years.
The Geological Survey is responsible for producing reports and maps which show geological features.
Water resources is one of the types of studies produced.
The government from time to time may change the agencies and hierarchy.
Many government maps are folded in envelopes or compiled in atlases in the documents collection of the first floor.

Topographic maps are the most popular maps that the Geological Survey produces.
Topographical maps indicate the elevation, rivers, and other physical features.
Topographical maps are especially useful to hikers and others who spend time in wilderness areas.
Some modern topographical maps are satellite pictures which have been enhanced to show elevation.
Elevation is the term used for the height in relation to sea level.
An index map is an outline of the entire area that shows the layout and numbering or naming of the maps sheets.
Individual maps may have a locator map which shows the relative position of the map in the larger area.
Topographical maps for Idaho and neighboring states are located in map cases on the first floor east wing.
The first drawer for each state should have an index map.
The maps are arranged alphabetically by the name that appears on the map after the state arrangement.
Ask at an information desk, if you need help.

Single sheets of maps produced by National Geographic and
the Central Intelligence Agency are located in map cases by Library of Congress numbers.
Maps have been produced to show a variety of features.

Relief map is another name for a map that gives elevation and the physical profile of the area.
Elevations could be represented by different colors.
Contour lines could provide the relief or profile.
A shaded relief uses three dimensions to represent the terrain with shadows as if light was shining from the northwest.

Political maps give political boundaries and cities.
Highways, railroads, airports, and seaports may be given.
Political maps tend to be less cluttered.

Physical maps give detail on deserts, forests, mountain ranges, altitude, and similar features.
The relationship between society and environment is more important on physical maps.

Thematic maps cover astronomy, environment, climate, society, population, agriculture,
income, geological structure, military alliances, and similar themes.
Collections of thematic maps are called topical atlases.
History, astronomy, and commerce are examples of subjects for topical atlases.

  • Historical Atlas of the U.S. (Atlas G1201.S1N3)
  • Atlas of the Night Sky (Ref QB65.A754)
  • Atlas of the Sky (Ref QB65.C313)
  • Atlas of the Moon (Ref QB595.C313)

Rand McNally Commercial Atlas (Atlas G1019.R22)

  • is very useful to businesses.
  • gives the names of airlines and trains that serve a town,
  • tells whether the town has a hospital or a bank, and
  • gives the zip code.
Charts give projected growth and information that will assist businesses in choosing locations for new branches.
Public libraries often use this source to tell collection agencies where the county seat is located.

Children's atlases often are used to teach map reading skills.
Achievement tests often measure a child's ability to use a map.
Accuracy is often not as important in children's atlases as the educational content.
Accurate maps of actual places such as nations and states should be available for children.
Inexpensive or free maps are often available from the local chamber of commerce or state highway department for teachers.

Accuracy is measure by scale and projection.
The map scale is a ratio of the distance on the map to the corresponding distance on the ground.
Three ways of recording this ratio exist.
Representative fraction or RF is 1:63,360 in which one inch represents one mile or 63,360 inches.
A verbal scale would be "one inch equals 1 mile."
A bar scale is like a ruler with distances like one inch marked on it.
Large scale maps cover a small area with greater detail.
Small scale maps cover larger areas.

Topographic maps that are 7.5 minute series have a scale of 1:24,000 or one inch equals 2,000 feet.
The 15 minute series have a scale of 1:62,500 or one inch equals about a mile.
These series have each map covering an area bounded by meridians of longitude and parallels of latitude.
They are often called quadrangle maps.

The projection is the method used to transfer a curved section of the earth to a flat page.
If the correct shape of the land is shown; its area is distorted.
Ocean navigation uses the rectangular map projection to show true compass directions.
A projection that looks like an orange cut at ocean areas and flattened distorts ocean areas while keeping relative land areas more accurate.

A legend on a map shows what symbols represent.
Atlases may have the legend inside the front or back cover to assist the user.
If you cannot find an explanation of the symbols, the preliminary pages may give clues.

The map may have an index.
In the index after the name and page or map number is a letter and number grid coordinate or latitude and longitude reference.

Longitude measures distance east or west of the prime meridian.
Greenwich England is located on the prime meridian or 0 degrees longitude.
Latitude measure the distance north or south of the equator.
The decimal in latitude and longitude is minutes in which 60 minutes equals a degree.
Longitude and latitude are often listed in gazetteers.

A gazetteer is a list of geographic names with descriptions of the location, historical, statistical, cultural and other facts. Pronunciation may also be given.
Gazetteers often have a comprehensive index in which every name that appears on all the maps for the area are listed. Cross-references to popular, different name spellings, foreign language equivalents and changes in names is found in the best gazetteer indexes.

The 1989 LDS edition of the scriptures has set of maps and gazetteer at the end of the Bible.
New copies of the Doctrine and Covenants have a set of maps and chronology. An insert of the maps can be purchased through the bookstores that carry church publications or using the Church's official home page.
Unabridged dictionaries may have a geographical section with gazetteer type entries.

Gazetteers are also books giving descriptive facts.
Genealogists find that gazetteers are especially useful for places which have had name changes.

Public libraries often have current guides for popular tourist destinations.
