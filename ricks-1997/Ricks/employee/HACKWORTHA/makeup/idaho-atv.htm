ATV's in Idaho
© 2000 Allen Hackworth


It's now late January. Heavy, abundant snows fell in Southeastern Idaho this year, and ubiquitous, pure, heavy snows are still falling out of a silver-grey sky. Since Monday, two days ago, we have had another 12 inches of new snow in Island Park, and the forecast tonight is for another 5 inches in the mountains during the next 24 hours.

I don't know the depth of the snow pack in Island Park, but I suspect five to six feet. Once in the mountains past Ashton, driving north, one gradually slips between two five-foot banks of snow, one on each side of the road. Large, rotary, snow plows cut these perfectly perpendicular banks.

Over the past few months, portions of this road have evolved into a tunnel with no top. One would think that if a car skidded out of control, it would bounce like a billiard ball off the snow walls and remain in the tunnel. But this was not true for one driver. Right after it happened, Kay Perrenoud and I saw a new, shinny, Jeep Cherokee which lost control.  It hit the snow / ice wall, flipped into the sky, and jumped like a giant grasshopper over the wall.

As it flipped, this $26,000 car lost its skis which were fastened on a rack above the car. But, cat like, the car landed in a soft blur, right-side up.  What a cushion! What soft violence disturbed the settled, indifferent snow.  Eventually, the flurry settled; the earth groaned, and a gentle slumber again settled over the land.

I always like to get into the mountains in the winter. The dormant land sleeps peacefully under its kind, ever-stretching blanket. The slumber is real, and it infuses quiet peace into the bosom of a loving observer.

At the Big Springs Road, Kay and I turned right. Driving east for a few miles on the Big Springs Road, we came to a plowed parking lot. This spot, called the Snow Lot, had Teton Peaks of snow, higher than buildings, on its northern edge. The snow which had been piled and rolled by snow-moving equipment, looked like giant bowls of ice cream. To the south stood two sturdy buildings: a bathroom and a warming shed.

This day we tried something new. We drove our ATV's on groomed snowmobile trails. In Island Park throughout the winter, mile after mile of trails are used by hundreds of riders. Although it was Monday, (President's Day), and not what you would expect on a weekday, we still passed about 60 snow machine riders during our three-hour adventure. But here is the reason for my words. I am excited, and I want to share with you a discovery, a thrilling insight: an ATV WORKS PERFECTLY WELL on these trails. Of course, you must stay on the roads; you can not jet along with the powerful freedom of a snow machine.

But you can still experience the beauty. The air is as crisp and clean as for the snowmobilers; the evergreens contrast as vividly against the bold, sculptured, whiteness of the hills as for the snowmobilers. Actually, when traveling, I believe an ATV rider will experience the beauties of the mountains more intimately than will a snowmobile rider. This is because the thrill of the snow machine is the flashy ride, the acceleration, the speed. A snowmobiler must be extremely vigilant in braking, twisting, shifting, and sliding, in order to avoid colliding with trees and other machines. We just poked along at 15 to 20 miles per hour, riding single file. There was plenty of room for the Polaris and Arctic Cats to zip past us, and our moderate speed gave us time to scan the country side. (The legal speed for these trails is 45 mph. Signs are posted just like on a freeway.)

We first drove to Big Springs. The waters, gushing year round, always at a constant flow, appear like magic at the base of the mountain. During the summer, a two-lane road crosses this instant river created by the springs. Parking areas on both sides of the bridge allow visitors to mingle on the bridge walkways. The wooden sidewalks hover above the water, allowing one to watch the abundant, rainbow trout as they fin placidly in the pure, shimmering stream. We just stopped in the middle of the bridge, and I walked to the edge to see the fish.

But now the four-foot guardrail which keeps summer visitors from falling into the water was about a foot beneath me. Snow filled the bridge, packed firm by the trail-groom- ing machines and the numerous riders who passed this way.

Soon, we continued on the Black Canyon Road. About 14 miles from West Yellowstone, Montana, we stopped, turned back, and started our leisurely, careless way back to our trailer and car. On the way back, we following the railroad for a few miles. Slipping and churning a bit, I felt uncomfortable crossing two trestles. On one trestle the snow pack raised us about four feet above the floor of the bridge. The narrow, undulating path was more secure for snowmobiles but less secure for an ATV. I feared that our clawing wheels might break loose the edge of this elevated path, causing us and our machines to flip into the river.

The other trestle was bare because the snow had slipped through the evenly spaced, tie beams. But at each end of this trestle, the elevation difference between the floor of the trestle and the snow-covered bank made a steep entrance and exit from the bridge. Oh, I didn't want to plunge into the frigid, black water.

Because we got off the main road when we followed the railroad, and because we crossed some meadows where a single, packed trail was not defined, we almost got stuck. (A two-wheel drive machine would not have made it across these open fields, but a two-wheel drive would be fine on the main roads.) Additionally, I was surprised to see five trumpeter swans in the meandering river.

After loading our machines and drinking hot chocolate from Kay's thermos, one important task remained. It was certainly as important as anything we had done thus far. We needed to eat at a mountain cafe next to the Henrys Fork near Macks Inn. While eating tender, moist, broiled chicken, I made a second discovery. I asked Debbie Green, our waitress, "Do you have any maps which show the locations of the groomed trails.?"

Debbie said, "Sure, I'll get you one." She hurried away and soon returned with a map which was published by Fremont County. The large, two-sided map, in one-mile grids, showed all of the trails, groomed or otherwise.

"Debbie, how much does this map cost?"

"It's free."

"Great! May I have one more for my friend?"

"Sure, I'll get him one." Debbie then scooted away and returned in a moment with a map.

Kay and I had a great day. Later in the evening, Loni and I went to the Zippy Copy Center to laminated our new map. As I relate these two insights, (1) riding ATV's on snowmobile trails, and (2) the availability of a complete, free map which shows the trails, you may say, "I have known this for years." But I really don't think many people have thought about riding ATV's on groomed snowmobile trails.  Now in Southeastern Idaho, ATV riding never again needs to be only a summertime sport.
