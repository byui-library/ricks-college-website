GREEK EDUCATION vs. OURS

The holistic educational view of the Greeks seems to have lost its place in today's world. Many consider today's standard college diploma elitist, or racist, or chauvinistic. Since too few achieve it, it can no longer be considered a “standard” education. Yet in all this comes a cry to “return to the basics—to a core curriculum.”

 

Are today's schools no more than vocation schools? The '88 report to Congress by the NEH found 45% of today's universities awarded a BA without  a literature class, 37% required no history, and 77% skipped a foreign language.

 

Derek Bok, past president of Harvard, notes that society needs people “with a sufficient breadth of knowledge to provide them with judgment, perspective and taste—people with a sensitivity for the problems of others and a strong sense of ethical principles.” These cannot be pushed aside in favor of “professional training.”

 

The humanities help us think critically. We learn good from evil, excellent from poor, and profound from superficial. Our abilities in these areas grow in relation to our experiences with the arts and our exposure to history, religion and philosophical thought.  The knowledge learned in humanities courses is essential to understand the world around us, and whether we wish to alter or preserve it.

 

E.D.Hirsch argued in Cultural Literacy that we only understand words and ideas when used in context. (What is Hitler without visualizing the Holocaust?) Thus, a certain body of knowledge is necessary in order to communicate, because all understanding lies not in the meaning of the words, but in their cultural context.

 

Alston Chase:  “Why is this nation persistently unable to upgrade the educational system, despite repeated tries? The answer, I think they will find, has to do with the hostile social values in which our schools operate. Students, whose role models are baseball players or rock stars, are unlikely to cherish knowledge. Those reared in the culture of instant gratification have little patience for the often laborious tasks of learning. A land wedded to wealth and entertainment is infertile soil for the flowering of wisdom.”

MUSIC

The Greeks valued and understood music so much that they wouldn't let the average person hear it. You had to be exceptional to be allowed the privilege of music.

Plato, in the Republic, says “musical training is a more potent [teaching] instrument than any other, because rhythm and harmony find their way into the inward places of the soul .  .  . making the soul of him who is rightly educated graceful, or of him who is wrongly-educated ungraceful.”

Lowell Mason, America's first superintendent of public school music, wrote in 1838

“Now, the defect of our present system, admirable as that system is, is this, that it aims to develop the intellectual part of man's nature solely, when for all the true purposes of life, it is of more importance, a hundred fold, to feel rightly than to think profoundly .  .  .  Through vocal music you set in motion a mighty power which silently, but surely, in the end, will humanize, refine, and elevate a whole community.”

TEACHING EVERYTHING - BEING CULTURED

Being cultured doesn't mean you listen to just opera. The truly cultured person is like a sponge, always open to new possibilities, anxious to absorb, yet quick to sift out the best just the best from his surroundings. Brand Blanshard (Great Ideas) says education is “living in the presence of the best till one understands and likes it. Till the ways of feeling of fine minds become one's own. Till one catches their health by contagion.” While that sounds crazy it's true. Some of the best food for you may not taste the best at first—you must “acquire” the taste, while the commonplace appeals at first, but grows more and more unpalatable as time goes on. The more you eat, the more refined your taste becomes, and in the end you can readily distinguish the good from the bad with only a small taste. Remember that not everything which is good will produce instant pleasure. Such is not our goal.

 

Knowledge is of two types: hard and soft.  Hard knowledge is the answer to a problem.  Question: “When did Columbus sail? Answer: “1492.” This type of information is useful only as long as the problems are of that type. But what about “When should a child be spanked?” There is no one answer. Rather, you are supposed to figure it out for yourself. But if your whole life has been answers of the Columbus type you will falter. The decision process comes only from a study of the humanities. It shows us how to study many dissimilar examples and from them arrive at a valid conclusion.

 

But with the Cartesian revolution came the Two Cultures spoken of by C.P.Snow in his 1959 book of the same title where the world was divided into the sciences and the humanities: scientists don't understand Shakespeare; and humanists can't describe the Second Law of Thermodynamics.

 

W.H. Auden's description of the bewildered fifth-century Athenian who visits our society points out the consequences of the neglect in general education:

“Yes,” says the man from Athens, “I can see all the works of a great civilization, but why cannot I meet any civilized persons? I only encounter specialists; artists who know nothing of science, scientists who know nothing of art, philosophers who have no interest in God, priests who are unconcerned with politics, and politicians who only know other politicians.
