GERMAN GRAMMAR OVERVIEW
10/1998 Brooks Haderlie
What to tackle first when unraveling a German sentence:
1. Find the verb.
2. Find the rest of the verb (prefix, past participle, infinitive, idiom).
3. Find the subject.
4. Find the direct object.
5. Translate everything else pretty much word for word.

Common German Sentence Structure:

        Subject Verb Predicate (direct object, indirect object, time/manner/place).

NOUNS

Case: English uses word order and grammatical constructions to indicate the role of nouns in a sentence. German uses four different "cases" to do this; the cases are usually indicated by the article in front of the noun. This makes it possible for nouns to be located in many different places in the sentence in German.
 

CASE         Masc.   Neuter  Fem.   Plural     Use of Case
Nominative der       das          die        die        Subject
Accusative  den      das          die        die        Direct Object
Dative         dem     dem         der       den +n   Indirect Object
Genitive      des      des          der        der        Possession

Contrived example:
           Ich gebe dem Kind das Geschenk des Vaters.
        Subj. Verb I.O.            D.O.          possession
           I'm giving the father's present to the child.

Subject: The subject is the main actor of the sentence. In English, the subject is typically at the beginning of the sentence. In German, it is indicated by the use of the nominative case.
 

Where can the subject be?
    - beginning of sentence or clause (Die Frau, die dort lauft, heißt Ute.)
    - right after conjugated verb (Diesen Brief hat das Kind geschrieben.)
    - after a conjunction or prepositional phrase (..., weil er krank war.)

Example: Die Dame hat das Pferd gesehen.
    Either Dame or Pferd could be the subject here. By convention, in this situation the noun before the verb is considered to
    be the subject.

Note: Here are 6 words that are always the subject if they are in a sentence:
        er; man; ich; wir; du; wer
        ('man' is like the British 'one', as in "One shouldn't do that")

Gender: see handout

Plural forms: no change, Umlaut, -e, -en, -inner, -n, -er -s.

ADJECTIVES VS. ADVERBS
Adjectives describe a noun (tall building). Adverbs describe an adjective (very tall building), or tell how or when something is done (quickly, smoothly, today).

Many words can be used as either adjectives or adverbs. In German, if it has an adjective ending (see below), it should be translated as an adjective. If it has no ending, it should be translated as an adverb, frequently by adding an -ly in English.

FYI: 'diese' and 'jene' in a sentence. If an author discusses two different items and then starts referring to 'diese' and 'jene', translate 'diese' as 'the latter' and 'jene' as 'the former'.

ADJECTIVE ENDINGS

Adjectives take different endings in German, depending on the role of the noun they modify:

                    Masc. Neuter Fem. Plural
Nominative er, e es, e e, e e, en
Accusative en, en es, e e, e e, en
Dative em, en em, en er, en en, en
Genitive es, en es, en er, en er, en

Example: Die nette Dame gibt dem kleinen Mädchen einen hübschen Hund.

Three possibilities: 1) der, adj. ending
                            2) ein, adj. ending*     *adjectives following an ein word with no
                            3) adj. ending               endings take the first adj. ending on the
                                                                chart. (ein guter Mann)

PRONOUNS

Pronouns can be used in place of a noun to avoid repetition. Possessive pronouns are different because they are actually used like adjectives, meaning they are used with a noun, and they use adjective endings.

VERBS

Verbs must be "conjugated" to agree with the subject in number (singular or plural) and person (1st, 2nd or 3rd person). (I see the dog. She sees the dog.)
ich wir              -e -en
du ihr               -st -t
er,sie,es  sie     -t -en
    Sie                 -en

We speak of three "voices", each of which can reflect different times (past, present, future).
• Active voice is the normal condition. (I see the horse.)
• Passive voice is when something is being done to or for a person or thing. (The book was shown to the child.)
• Subjunctive or conditional indicates uncertainty, or that an event is conditional. (If he built it, they would come.)

Modal Verbs
Modal verbs are "helping" verbs that are used with the infinitive (although sometimes the infinitive is implied: Wer kann gehen? Ich kann (gehen)).
        sollen = should, ought to, supposed to
        wollen = want to, intend to
        können = can, to be able to, to know
        mögen = to prefer
        dürfen = may, be permitted or allowed
        müssen = must, have to

        See handout on modal verbs.

PARTICIPIAL PHRASES
This is a construction peculiar to German. Thomas Mann (among others) loves these. Mark Twain despises them (see handout). They typically contain an article, a descriptive phrase, a present or past participle, and a noun.

To understand and translate them, follow 1 4 3 2.

Example: ...die von Krebs entwickelte Methode . . .
                   1         4             3                 2
                ...the method developed by Krebs.

Try this one:
Dr. Berg untersuchte die der Handschrift des Bankraubers sehr ahnliche Schrift auf dem in der Kuche gefundenen Zettel.
