Clauses
Before you learn about clauses, read about conjunctions.

First, understand what a phrase is.
 
Phrase
A phrase is a group of words.  A phrase does not contain a subject and a verb.  The most common phrases are prepositional.
 
A phrase can function as a noun, adjective, adverb, infinitive, gerund, participial, or verb.  (Many of these words will be defined later.  At this point, just remember that a  phrase is  A GROUP OF WORDS.)
 
Clause
A clause is a group of words with a subject and a verb. There are two kinds of clauses:  independent and dependent.
 
 
Independent Clause
An independent clause can stand alone.  It makes sense by itself.
 
Dependent Clause
A dependent clause cannot stand alone.  It needs to be attached to an independent clause in order to make sense.  A dependent clause can function as either a noun, adverb, or adjective.  (This concept will be understood as you learn the meanings of additional terms.)
 
Learn a few more terms.   Earlier you learned that an adjective, adverb, and noun could be a single word, a phrase, or a clause.  You were shown examples of single word adjectives, adverbs, and nouns as well as adjectives, adverbs, and nouns used as phrases.
 
Now consider the noun, adjective, and adverb clause.  Make sure you understand these structures.
These clauses will always be dependent; therefore, their presence will always imply complex structure.
 
Noun Clauses
A noun clause will often start with the word, "that."  A noun clause can function as a subject, direct object, indirect object, or an object of a preposition.  In fact, any place in a sentence that takes a noun can take a noun clause.  Here are two examples.  One uses a noun clause as a subject, the other as a direct object.
 
Example.  That you are well prepared is certain.  "That you are well prepared" is the subject.  The subject is a noun position; therefore, this clause is a noun clause.
 
Example.  Stewart said that you would come later.  "That you would come later" is a direct object.  The direct object is a noun position; therefore, this clause is a noun clause.  (In this example, the word "that" could be left out.  This would actually improve the sentence.) With the examples just given, all are complex sentences.
 
Adjective Clauses
An adjective clause will often start with the words, "who" or "which" or "that.".  When they do, the words "who, which, or that" function as the subjects of the adjective clause.  Here are some examples:
 
Example.  The dancer who is in the spotlight is my sister.  "Who is in the spotlight" is an adjective clause.  The clause modifies a noun, dancer.
 
Example.  The car which is by the curb belongs to Shelly.  "Which is by the curb" is an adjective clause.  The clause modifies a noun, car. Both examples just given are complex sentences.
 
Example.   The truck that is red belongs to Frank.  "That is red" is an adjective clause.  The clause modifies a noun, truck.  This is also a complex sentence.
 
Adverb Clauses
The adverb clause will start with a subordinating conjunction.  These words were listed earlier.  Also, the adverb clause will answer one of the four questions that adverbs answer.  Here are some examples.  Example.  We could eat because we drove to the pizza parlor.  "Because we drove to the pizza parlor" is an adverb clause.  Example.  Although it is raining, we will fish anyway.  "Although it is raining" is an adverb clause.  
 
In all cases, adverb, adjective, and noun, these clauses are dependent.
 
BEN FRANKLIN SAID, "IT SEEMS TO ME THAT THERE IS SCARCE ANY ACCOMPLISHMENT MORE NECESSARY TO A MAN OF SENSE, THAN THAT OF WRITING WELL IN HIS MOTHER TONGUE. BUT AS MOST OTHER POLITE ACQUIREMENTS MAKE A GREATER APPEARANCE IN A MAN'S CHARACTER, THIS HOWEVER USEFUL, IS GENERALLY NEGLECTED OR FORGOTTEN."
