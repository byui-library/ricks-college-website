Guidelines to Writing About Literature

Writing to Respond

i Read the work several times, making marginal notes and writing journal entries to explore your responses.

i Focus on one response that seems particularly strong.

i Explain that response, using examples from your own experience, but also make certain to refer to the work so that the connections between your experience and the work are clear.

i Remember that a response asks for your own ideas and feelings, not simply a summary of ideas and feelings in the work.

 

Writing to Compare

j As you plan the paper by doing preliminary reading, writing, and thinking, remember that listing and outlining are useful strategies for planning a comparison paper.

j Remember that a comparison should be made for a purpose. A comparison should not simply list the similarities and differences discovered during planning sessions.

j Note which similarities or differences seem most significant and decide which you will emphasize.

j Decide how you will organize your paper--for example, the Awhole-subject@ approach or the Apoint-by-point@ approach.

j Open with a paragraph that focuses on the purpose and point of the comparison.

j Develop each subject (or each point) in a separate paragraph (or a series of carefully related and logically linked paragraphs).

j Make certain that transitions between paragraphs and between sections of the paper show the connections--the comparisons and contrasts--you want to make.

j Develop a conclusion that offers an analysis, evaluates the evidence the body of your paper provides, or in some other way shows the significance of the comparison you have made.

 

Writing to Analyze

l An analysis looks at parts (or a part) in relationship to the whole to which they belong.

l A literary analysis often focuses on one or more elements of literature, including--but not limited to--the following: character, plot, irony, setting, language.

l An analysis shows how the element or elements being considered contribute to the whole meaning of the work; each part of the discussion, therefore, must relate to a clear central idea.

l An analysis is not a paraphrase (a restatement of the ideas of the work in your own words); instead, it is an explanation of the way a work communicates.

 

Writing to Explicate

m An explication Aunfolds@ the poem (or section of a short story, novel, play, or essay). That is, it explains in detail how the work communicates to the reader.

m An explication considers significant details and suggestions in the piece, including--but not limited to--these elements: sound, structure, figurative language, irony, definitions of words.

m An explication shows how each part contributes to the whole meaning of the work; each part of the discussion, therefore, must relate to a clear central idea.

m An explication is not a paraphrase (a restatement of the ideas of the work in your own words); instead, it is an explication of the way a work communicates.

m An explication is easy to organize; working chronologically from first to last stanza or a poem (or from beginning to end of a section from a short story, novel, essay, or play) makes the most sense.

m An explication begins with an introduction, indicating the main idea of the work and suggesting the direction of the explication.

m An explication concludes with a paragraph that sums up the meaning that has been unfolded for the reader.

 

Writing to Evaluate

q Identify the beliefs and values expressed in the work, making note of specific details that demonstrate these beliefs and values.

q Think about the criteria you will use to evaluate those beliefs and values.

q Consider what questions might be raised concerning those beliefs and values. (If you share those values, imagine the response of someone who does not.)

q Decide whether your evaluation will support or question (perhaps even refute) the values and beliefs expressed in the work.

q List your reasons for supporting, questioning, or refuting these values and beliefs.

q Remember to reread the work frequently to make certain you are responding to values and beliefs actually expressed there.

q Make certain the opening section of the paper makes clear both the values and beliefs expressed in the work and the approach you are taking toward those values and beliefs.

q Make certain the conclusion sums up the evaluation--the reasons you support and subscribe to (or do not support and subscribe to) the beliefs and values expressed in the work.
