Claim and Support

In most academic writing, you follow a familiar pattern. You make a claim and then do your best to support it. Notice how this pattern is used in the sample magazine analysis. In the first paragraph of each example, the claim is underlined, and the support is in italics. See if you can spot the claim and support in the other sample paragraphs.

Advertising
Refusing limits is the focus of many of the magazine's advertisements and reflects the tone and attitude of the magazine as a whole. An advertisement for Good Year Tires encourages readers to "go where you want to go." And going where you want to go leaves readers open to exotic new locations and products. In their half page ad, Portillo Chile promises "snow to be wild" and "a ski adventure in the Chilean Andes." This advertisement coupled with ads for authentic Russian vodka and a lager that promises "the fiery passion of Mexico. Immortalized in Painting. Song. Six-Packs" capture the international flavor of adventure and no-limit living the magazine promotes. Skiing celebrates excess not only on adrenalin pumping slopes, but in ads for hard liquor and Joe Cool Camel cigarettes. Readers of this magazine not only see themselves as carefree adventurers, but they have the jobs and money to match, as seen in the adds for foreign ski trips and mid-priced to luxury-priced automobiles that make up the bulk of the magazine's advertisements.

Types of Articles
The magazine is divided into four sections. The first appeals to audience members' sense of adventure, profiling different spine tingling skiing destinations. In the March/April 1997 edition, the two destination articles included one entitled "Hanging in Purgatory" about a resort in southwest Colorado promising to explore the slopes and the "hyperactive sport culture." The other, "Dancing on La Grave" celebrates a resort in France with steep glacier skiing on "raw" ungroomed slopes. Both articles capture the devil-may-care, high adventure attitude of the magazine.

The second division, entitled Instruction, includes tips for novice, intermediate, and advanced skiers, but the emphasis is really on the more advanced practitioners. The central article of the section offers advice on how to ski couloirs, huge, high altitude glacial channels between high mountains. The authors travel to France to test their metal against the cliffs. Well over 1,000 feet long in some cases, this tough terrain skiing is only for the elite and the rich.

Writing Style
By now we've established that Skiing is not for the weekend hobbiest. This is a magazine for skiers who laugh at limits and imagine themselves hurdling down a slope, living on the edge. The tone and style of the magazine's articles reflect that attitude. Most articles are written in a very informal, first person style. An evaluation criticizing a Bolivian ski resort describes it in the following terms: "Mostly...the road is a cesspool of mud the width of a large dog, with no guard rail and a drop-off that would allow me to write my last will and testament before we hit bottom."

This type of conversational style peppers most of the magazine's articles and columns, but the language and sentence structure are by no means simple. In fact, writers for Skiing magazine use vivid figures of speech and complex sentences to try and capture the skiing experience they describe. In "Hanging in Purgatory," Sam Walsh describes his experience on the Colorado slopes with a colorful comparison: "The skier ahead of me glided over and around the powder bumps like a porpoise playing in the waves and then disappeared over the crest of the trail, powder hanging in his wake. I bounced and jolted about like a dinghy with no one at the oars." Another writer uses a similar style when describing a steep, cliff descent, "With cliffs soaring above you on both sides, it feels as if the mountain is wrapping its arms around you."

What we often do as we structure a paragraph is move from the broad (general claim) to the specific (concrete support for that claim). Think of it in terms of an upside down triangle. The paragraph starts out with a general assertion, and the paragraph becomes more and more specific as you work to support that assertion until you've clearly made your point.

There are several different kinds of support you might use to back up your claims. I've listed several here; you might think of others.

  • Facts and statistics
  • Expert testimony
  • Personal experience
  • Examples or illustrations

The key is to recognize that when we make claims in our writing, we must be prepared to back them up.
