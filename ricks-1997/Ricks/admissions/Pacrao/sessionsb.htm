Session B1

Washington Online Virtual Campus

Facilitator:
 Judy Richardson, Clover Park Technical College

Recorder Name:
 April Retherford, Tacoma Community College 

Presenters:
 Connie Broughton, Washington Online Consortium
Suanne Carlson, The Virtual Campus Consortium
Candy Lacher, Big Bend Community College

Washington Online (WAOL) is a project of a consortium made up of the 34 community and technical colleges in Washington State.  The initial goal of the project was to develop an online AA degree that could be delivered by all colleges in the consortium.  Through a competitive process, 20 courses were selected to be developed by instructors working at colleges in the consortium.  Once the WAOL courses were developed, colleges put them on their schedules.  Students enroll through the college of their choice, and the college collects the tuition and FTE dollars.  The enrollments from all colleges are collected into sections that are taught by instructors from colleges all over the state.  WAOL also manages college-owned courses.  Courses are delivered using an outside vendor Embanet, of Toronto Canada.  In the first year, WAOL enrollments grew from 350 (Fall 1998) to 1561 (Fall 1999).  WAOL is now beginning a new round of course development with an emphasis on courses for technical and vocational programs.

In January 1999 the Virtual Campus Consortium was launched as an additional approach to augment online learning in Washington, particularly for students who want to take all their classes in an online environment and from a single one-stop entity.  Candy Locher shared her campus experience providing student services at Big Bend Community College, both the challenges of implementation and the satisfaction of students living and working far from campus.

Session B2

Scheduling: Politics, Strategies and Stakeholders

Facilitator:
Tina Toma, UC Irvine

Recorder Name:
Julia Reisinger, Oregon College of Art and Craft

Presenters:
Kathleen Copenhaver, UCLA
Doug Thompson, UCLA

After a brief history of how UCLA used to coordinate room scheduling, Kathleen then reviewed their current policies and procedures that utilize both the school's home grown student information systems and their web site.

Through the use of the web site, the Registrar's Office has increased the communication between departments, and the dispersal of information regarding room assignments.  Department heads can look up current room reports concerning how many classes are scheduled at certain times, or in specific rooms.  These reports may be inclusive, or tailored to fit the needs of the requester (i.e. only undergraduate classes, or a specific department).

This increases information has also led to an increased decentralization of the room scheduling process.  Department heads first enter their own schedules, and then the Registrar's Office has the authority to adjust times and/or locations as needed.  As enrollments increase or decrease, the Registrar's Office may change locations again.  However, since faculty and students are accustomed to utilizing the web on a regular basis, the Registrar's Office no longer needs to post changes or print revised schedules.

There are still some areas needing attention, such as department heads entering non-standard meeting times, and the never-ending desire for all classes to meet between 10 am and 2 pm.  However, the increases communication has helped in other areas such as scheduling special events and extension courses.

Session B3

The Overlooked Community

Facilitator:
 Michael Brubaker, Mt. St. Mary's College

Recorder Name:
 MaryAnn Marwitz, Antioch University

Presenter:
Kate Fitzgerald, Art Center College of Design Los Angeles

The overlooked community is the LGBT (Lesbian, Gay, Bisexual, and Transgender) sector on campuses, including students, faculty, and staff.  Several myths exist regarding this community. 

MYTH: The LGBT community often are not overlooked (e.g. regarding medical insurance, housing, tuition waivers, social events, etc.

REALITY: The LGBT community often is invisible (e.g. regarding medical insurance, housing, tuition waivers, social events, etc.)

      MYTH: College students are old enough to be 
                    comfortable with their sexuality.

REALITY: College students often are just beginning to figure out who they are. 

MYTH: My college is located in a part of the country where LGBT students feel welcome.

REALITY: No part of the country is 100% 

MYTH: My college is a place that welcomes all types.

REALITY: Does your college print a non-discrimination clause in your catalog? Is sexual orientation included?  Does your college offer domestic partner benefits? 

Students who know or think they are LGBT will read your catalog and check your bulletin boards for evidence that they are welcome.  These students represent income to your school, and this affects your bottom line. 

Does your school have openly gay mentors?  Do posted fliers use inclusive language?  Do counselors, staff, acknowledge partners/significant others?  Are LGBT publications available?  The answers to these questions affects the perception, the "climate" of your school.

 Prejudice exists.  People "come out" in safe environments.  "0Coming out" is key to fighting prejudices.  Is your school a safe place to "come out"

MYTH: It costs to much to support the LGBT community

REALITY: No, it doesn't!  Educational institutions that offer domestic partner insurance have seen an average of 1% increase in insurance costs.  Subsidize LGBT groups only if your college subsidizes other groups.  Pay LGBT facilitators only if you pay other group facilitators, and remember COMPASSION COSTS NOTHING!

Staying in the closet if you are staff gets in the way.  Staying in the closet if you are a student gets in the way.  It marginalizes you and perpetuates the false notion, "I don't know any of these people."

Rewrite your non-discrimination and sexual harassment policies.  Create a safe space on your campus.  Organize a student/faculty/staff group.  Connect with off-campus organizations.  Sponsor LGBT centered events for the entire campus.  MAKE IT HAPPEN.

Session B5

Quality Student Service: One Stop Shopping at Cal. State University

Presenter:  Marilyn Radisch,  San Jose State University

San Jose State has an enrollment of 26,000 students.  The administrative staff wanted to improve student service by combining high tech and high touch.  Students indicated the University needed to improve various functions.  Common complaints were customer service issues such as long lines, slow processing, wrong answers, and too much paper work.

The student service staff reviewed student issues and developed a list of important criteria for improvement.

  1. Give the right answer first

  2. Give directions for proper contact first time

  3. Be available evenings and weekends

  4. No longer than a 15 minute wait in line

The solution was to combine high tech with high touch (70% of services could be handled with technology).  This could include transactions such as grade processing, admission, and financial aid awarding.  The remaining 20% were general service questions that the staff could answer and 10% special situations requiring staff intervention.  It is important to train staff on customer service and to review all practices for possible improvements.  Have regular common meetings with all areas so one person is able to answer most questions.

Session B6

Leadership - Part II "Humor Yourself - The Teamwork Factor"

Recorder Name:
Janet Shandley - Seattle University

Presenters:
Roger Swanson and Faith Weese - Publishers of "Becoming a Leader in Enrollment Services"

After Roger's introduction, Faith took over this session.  She did an icebreaker using Oreo cookies in a psycho-personality test

Faith emphasized that need to value our employees as our greatest asset.  She suggested the "dilemma of the decade" is: 1) attracting 2) recruiting 3) developing 4) motivating 5) keeping good people.

To give us "tools for our toolbox" Faith suggested we learn how our team works together based upon the role each plays in the whole.  The sessions attendees were put through an interactive exercise called "The Official Color Kit" to help us determine our "color."  The work styles associated with each color category were discussed and valued.

Above all, Faith suggests we lighten up and keep everything in perspective.  If we understand each other's style and expectations, we can learn to play to everyone's strengths rather than concentrate on the differences.
