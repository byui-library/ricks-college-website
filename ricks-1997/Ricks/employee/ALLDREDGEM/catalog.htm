 Introduction

    Catalogs of library collections have existed as long as libraries. The word, "catalog" originally meant a list. The British spelling is "catalogue." It is a combination of traditional rules and the French influence before their language was standardized. Under Old English a "u" has to appear between "g" and "e" or "i" to retain the sound of "g" in "goat" or "guide." To follow this tradition, I will only use a "u" when an "i" or "e" are part of the word "catalog.*"
    The first catalogs were merely a list of the items in the library collection. Cataloging or cataloguing now describes the item in more detail. Levels of description exist for different types of material in different types of collections.
At our library paperback materials and items on 'Reserve' often have brief descriptions. Older material may not have descriptions that are as complete as new material. In the past fiction was not assigned subjects. Music found on compact laser disks have more complete descriptions including contents.
 

Kinds of Catalogs

 The first catalogs were printed as books.

The Library of Congress printed a series of books called The National Union Catalog or NUC which in 167 volumes described its collection before 1942. College course catalogs and merchandise catalogs may still be printed in book form. Patron demand has resulted in printouts of audiocassettes, music CDs, and videocassettes. Print catalogs do not need software or machines to use them.

 

Card catalog are being replaced by computerized versions.

The most widely used catalog in the past has been on 3 inch by 5 inch cards. Our library no longer has this type of catalog. At other libraries the traditional card catalog may be available for only old material that has not been entered into the computerized catalog. A more accurate question may now be: "Where is a catalog of your library materials?" Look at the section on types of entries: author, title, and subject to see why this type is vanishing.

David O. McKay Library at Ricks College has an online catalog.

    In addition to telling location and call number for materials, online catalogs show the status of materials (checked out). The same database that is used to check out material is the one used by everyone in the library. The status of materials includes when materials are checked out and when they are due. "Make a request" allows users to request materials that are checked out. When the material is returned a message appears prompting library workers to put it on a shelf behind the circulation desk with the name of the person making the request. For several days no other person can check out the material. Faculty have longer check out periods. "Recall" is a procedure that allows circulation supervisors to contact a patron to negotiate an earlier return on materials that have become popular. Library personnel can also make requests for additional copies to be purchased. "Make a request" puts users in a first come first served order. The online catalog does not show which  materials are currently being used in the building. Again talk to library workers. Inventories, shelf reading and reports of missing material are needed to keep materials where they should be.
If you cannot find material, ask!

  Some libraries have used computer output microfiche or COM as a back up system.

When the computer does not work libraries needed backup systems to find materials. Libraries also wanted to share their catalogs. In 1984 a computer output microfiche copy was made of our catalog. Our microfiche catalog is now so old it is no longer available. Historical research may use these COM catalogs.
 
CD-ROM or compact disc read only memory catalogs are used by some libraries.
These CD-ROM catalog have taken the place of COM for public catalogs, backup and ways to share resources. The Monthly Catalog of U.S. Government Publications now appears on CD-ROM with GP3.8/7: as a call number. A search of our catalog will retrieve many other government catalogs to publications, Internet sites, products and services.

Internet catalogs allow users to search a catalog far away.

Our library has an Internet version of the catalog at  http://abish.ricks.edu/webpac/. WebPAC is a software program used with Internet catalogs to remove the local search differences. PAC is the abbreviation for public access catalog. The Monthly Catalog of U.S. Government Publications has an Internet version that has links to many full text publications at http://www.access.gpo.gov/su_docs/dpos/adpos400.html

Using catalogs

Print catalogs

Print catalogs are often divided into a subject, author, or title catalog.
A dictionary catalog has all three types of entries under one alphabet.
Look for signs, table of contents, indexes or notes indicating which type of entries printed catalogs contain.
Many libraries are eliminating  paper shelf lists and other printed catalogs.
 If it looks like a card catalog, inquire about its coverage before relying on it.
 

Computerized catalogs

Computerized catalogs (CD ROM, online, Internet) have different search options for subject, author or title.
  •  An alphabetical or browse search is similar to searches of a printed catalog.
    1. Word order is important.
    2. Rules for alphabetizing may be important.
    3. Spelling needs to start correctly but browsing helps with spelling long complex words.
    4. Controlled vocabulary determines the order of subject words.
      5. See a thesaurus like the Library of Congress Subject Headings.
  • A keyword search uses words that are unique and have meaning without requiring a specific order.
    1. Common words should be avoided on keyword searches.
      • "A, the, an, of, by," are common words (articles, prepositions, etc.)
      • "AND, OR, NOT" should be used only as Boolean operators.
        • Boolean logic is a way to create sets or groups of attributes.
        • "AND" combines sets and designates a group that has both attributes.
        • "OR" expands to make a set of different attributes.
        • "NOT" excludes from a set the members that have a certain attribute.
      • American, United States, and history may slow a search.
        • Add an author or other unique word to a general keyword search
        • Use subject keyword for periods of history. (U.S. -- History -- Civil War)
      • Mormon and history at this library may be too common without author or more specific terms.
    2. Check your spelling, if  few or no titles are retrieved.
    3. Word order is not important. Maiden names may be useful to search for women authors.
    4. General keyword searches scan for words as author, title, subject and content notes.
      • Our default or first search is general keyword to increase success.
      • Author, title, and subject can be combined
  • Advanced searches allow the search to be limited to collections or publication dates (In house searches)
    1. An 'x' is placed in the box next to the advanced search feature
    2. A menu appears to restrict or limit the search.
    3. Patron information is available with advanced search or under the "file" option.
    4. Try to remember to turn limits off when finished.
      • Exiting the program returns to the default or normal search options.

Internet searches of catalogs have similar search to online and CD-ROM catalogs.

  • Try an Internet version of our catalog at  http://abish.ricks.edu/webpac/.
  • Keyword searches include general, title, author, and subject
  • Advanced searches are currently used to restrict the search to a collection
  • Library of Congress http://lcweb.loc.gov/catalog/
  • Choose alphabetical (browse) or (key) word search
  • Choose time period (since 1975 is quickest)
  • Choose type of entry: author, title, subject, or special number
  • Select a title.
  • Select the view:  brief or full display

    • Kinds of entries

        An entry is a single listing.
    • The author card is called the "main entry" in the United States.
      1. Other authors or creators listed on the title page are joint authors.
      2. If more than three authors are involved, the first author gets all the credit with the others listed as "et al,"
        • Et al = the Latin phrase for "and others."
      3. Added entries are often made for other creators.
      4. A government agency, a corporation, an association, a society, or other group may be the author.
      5. An author may write under different names.
        • pseudonym or pen name
        • legal name of an author
        • the name that appears on the book.
        • Birth and death dates may be given.
        • cross references may link these names.
        See the Biography study guide for sources that will help identify pseudonyms and various forms of the name.
      6. An authority file is the term used to describe efforts to group material under a standard form for authors and subjects.
    • If no author entry is used, the title is the main entry.
      • Current cataloguing rules may not give author entries for editors.
      • Added entries are often made for other titles by which the work may be known.
      • Uniform titles are created to gather all copies of the scriptures and literary works.
      • Periodicals often change titles resulting in entries for preceding and succeeding titles.
        1. Preceding titles are those given to earlier copies.
        2. Succeeding titles are later titles for the same material.
        3. Some titles vary a great deal from issue to issue and notes may be made to show these variations.
      • A book may belong to a series of related books.
        1. The series may be recorded as an optional title.
        2. (The work and the glory) is the name of a series of popular fiction books at this library.
    • Subject entries are based on the classification system.
      • For the most widely used list of subjects see the Library of Congress subject headings.
      • For a simpler list used with Dewey Decimal Systems see Sears subjects.
      • As mentioned briefly in the unit on arrangement and classification systems, government groups each use their own. National Institute of Medicine and ERIC have their own subject headings.
    Typing all those cards and entries and filing them was a lot of work. Ordering just a pre-printed card for a shelf list added two weeks to processing library materials. The only card catalog at many libraries is in Technical Services where it is used for inventory. Our library discarded this card catalog in just the last ten years. A shelf list is the name of this inventory list. Technology has allowed libraries to keep electronic versions.

    Government document selection numbers will appear for government documents in MARC format.
    This number and others may not appear on catalog screen seen at our library.
    These numbers are important for librarians but not for every catalog user.

    ISBN or International Standard Book Number is another number of value that may be hidden at PAC terminals.
    This ISBN number identifies publishers and edition of books for commercially produced materials.
    This number is useful for ordering materials for purchase or obtaining them from other libraries.

    ISSN or International Standard Serial Number is similar to ISBN but used for serials (periodicals or magazines).
    A book may have both an ISBN and ISSN number.
    Serials include books that are published every year such as almanacs.

    Others call numbers may be hidden.

    Subjects

  • Different audiences may use different subjects:
      1. Library of Congress subjects are used by large or academic libraries.
      2. Sears subjects are used by many public, school or small libraries.
      3. Education subjects may be called ERIC descriptors.
      4. Medicine subjects use NLM numbers and subjects.

    Subject headings

    The Library of Congress Subject Headings or LSCH contains the subject headings used by academic and large libraries

    The inside cover or first pages of the volume will gives these symbols:
        UF Used for (these are synonyms that are not used for the subject )
        BT Broader topic
        RT Related topic
        SA See also
        NT Narrower topic
        (May Subd Geog) geographic locations may follow the heading

    Call numbers appear for about thirty percent of the headings.
        [Square brackets surround the call number.]
        Several call numbers may be given.

    Scope notes are used to define how the subject is used.
        GUN CONTROL is used for general and nonlegal material.
        FIREARMS -- LAW AND LEGISLATION is used for a legal treatment.
        See also may be used in scope notes to point to words that are used in special ways.

    USE and UF are reciprocal in nature.
    Words that have entries with UF will be displayed after USE if the UF term is looked up.
    Topics preceded by UF are not currently used.
    They may have been used in the past.
    The library may have material catalogued under the older term.

    Broader topics and narrower topics are part of a hierarchal or outline relationship.

  • LIGHTING is Broader Term
  • EXTERIOR LIGHTING is Narrower Term

    • Related topics are associated in ways that are not hierarchal.
  • BIRDS and ORNITHOLOGY are related
  • BIRDS is the common or lay word
  • ORNITHOLOGY is the scholarly word for the field of study

    • Subdivision may be by topic, form, date, or geography.
     -- LAW AND LEGISLATION is a subdivision by topic.
     -- DICTIONARIES is a subdivision by form.
     -- TO 55 B.C. is a subdivision by date.
     -- IDAHO is a geographic subdivision.
    These subdivisions may not appear with every subject in the subject headings books.

    Alphabetical Arrangement Rules

    The card catalog with title, author, and subject cards interfiled is called a dictionary catalog.
    Dictionaries might use an alphabetical arrangement different from the dictionary catalog.
    Some dictionaries use letter by letter rather than the word by word arrangement used by dictionary catalogs.

    Alphabetical arrangement requires some filing rules.
    Rules tell how numbers and abbreviations are treated.
    Each library may have its own interpretation of alphabetical filing rules.
    Generally, the card catalogs are arranged in word by word alphabetical order with the following rules:

    1. Arrangement is by the first word which is not an article (a, an, the or their foreign equivalents).
    MARC records usually have "indicator" numbers that tell the computer how many letters to skip for articles.
    If indicator numbers are incorrect, alphabetical searches do not work properly.

    2. Authors names are written with the last name followed by a comma and the first name and middle or maiden names. Authors with the same last name are then arranged by the first name.

    3. When the author or subject name is identical, file chronologically by the dates.

    Examples are:
    Johnson, Thomas H., 1803-1880
    Johnson, Thomas H., 1958-

    4. Royalty are filed by first names and numerically:

    Elizabeth I, Queen of England
    Elizabeth II, Queen of England

    5. Materials by an author come before  materials for which he is the subject.
    Remember that subjects are in CAPITAL LETTERS or red ink in traditional card catalogs.

    Shakespeare, William 1564-1616  Author
    SHAKESPEARE, WILLIAM, 1564-1616 SUBJECT

    6. Subject cards with dates which are part of the subject are arranged alphabetically and then chronologically. For example:

    U.S. HISTORY--REVOLUTION
    U.S. HISTORY--WAR OF 1812
    U.S. HISTORY--CIVIL WAR

    7. Different editions of the same book are arranged chronologically.
    Some libraries put the newest edition first; others put the oldest first.

    8. Abbreviations and numbers may be filed as if spelled out.
    Mrs. may be filed as 'mistress' or may be filed as spelled.
    "Mr." is usually filed as spelled "m-i-s-t-e-r."
    A thesaurus program may allow a computer to be intuitive but don't rely on it!

    9. Words beginning with Mc may be filed as if they were Mac.
    Names beginning with De, Von, or Van are filed as one word.
    Van Gogh would be the same as Vangogh.

    10. Hyphenated  words are filed as two separate words. Newton-John could be filed after Newton, Isaac if punctuation is ignored. Other rules would place it after Newton, Zacharias if all regular Newtons would precede all hyphenated Newtons.

    11. Punctuation marks and titles of honor in the author's name are disregarded usually.

    12. Books written under a pseudonym might be placed under the real name using filing rules for authority files.
    For example, Mark Twain might be listed under Samuel Langhorne Clemens.

    Computerized catalogs and reference tools may be literal without these efforts to put things together.
    If you cannot find the item, try several applications of the alphabetical filing rules.
    Computers usually allow keyword searches which avoid the whole problem of alphabetical arrangement.
    Many programs also allow the user to sort the results of a search.
    People who work at the library may have special insights.
    At times the indexing of computer files may have problems due to upgrades in software.

    One drawback of card catalogs are that cards can be removed without permission.

    Creating a description

    Whether you create your own catalog or just use one for research, you need to understand what is needed to create a description of research material. Style manual and catalog rules determine the order and contents of a description.
    Basic to all descriptions is the information found on the title page.

    Title Page

    • is used to create catalogs and bibliographies
    • contains the following
      1. Title with subtitle
        • Subtitle is a descriptive phrase explaining scope of title
        • Subtitles are common on scholarly research
      2. Author and other creators
        • Editor,
        • Illustrators and artists,
        • Translator,
        • Composers and arrangers,
      3. Publication information or Imprint
        • Imprint was an embossed seal to authenticate creation and includes:
          1. Place of publication
          2. Name of publisher
          3. Date of publication
            • Edition has the same information each time it is printed

         Verso or back of title page

    • gives the history of the book and its imprint
    • includes copyright information
    • editions are used for revisions
      • Textbook editions may have minor revisions
        • Cookies with 1 teaspoon instead of 1/2 tsp. vanilla is a minor revision
        • Cookies with nuts added could be a major revision, especially if you are allergic to them
      • Editions have different dates and numbers
        • 10th edition (1998)
        • use latest copyright date
    • Printings all have the same material.
      • useful only for collectors
      • Refrigerator cookies have same ingredients but different baking dates.
    • CIP or cataloguing -in - publication
      • provided by Library of Congress as part of copyright process
      • appears as a sample library record
      • gives unique numbers to find books on Interlibrary Loan or for purchase
        • ISBN or International Standard Book Number (0-395-57035-2)
        • Library of Congress Number (93-18836)
      • gives call numbers
        • Dewey Decimal (E for Easy reader in Juvenile books)
          • DC 20 is the edition of cataloging
        • Library of Congress (PZ7.S2744Gr 1993)
        • These call numbers are based on the unfinished work and may change when the Library of Congress sees the completed work.
      • gives subjects assigned to book
        • useful for finding similar books with
          • Grandfather theme
          • Japan or Japanese American theme
     

         Value of creating your own personal catalog.

        A shelf list is valuable for insurance purposes. If you have a major investment, you may wish to create your own personal catalog. Holding on to textbooks is not always as helpful as retaining a list of the title and publication information. If you transfer to another school, you may be able to obtain credit by showing that you already studied a course with a different name or credit and covered by a similar textbook.
        Wordprocessed documents, databases, or spreadsheets make small catalogs or shelf lists easier to create and update.
        Librarians who have survived disasters suggest that a copy of the shelf list be kept in another location or more secure location from the materials. For insurance purposes an inventory in a fire proof box is a minimum option. For your personal photographs, you may want to be very generous with copies to friends and family.

    The size of the collection will help you determine how to create a list.
    Using a word processing program a shelf list and a finding list for a home or classroom library is possible.
        Sorting by first word in a line or column will put the list in shelf order.
        Sorting by the second word or column will put the list in title order.
        Sorting by the last column could put the list in order by picture book or story book format.
        At the end of the line an abbreviation was added to describe the material.
        Sorting by this lastword or abbreviation grouped the list into material into other groups.
        A printout could then be used by your children when looking for a book.
    Other collection lists have been created by using a paragraph for each author and sorting by paragraphs.
    The "find" or "search" key in the computer program will allow "keyword" searches.
