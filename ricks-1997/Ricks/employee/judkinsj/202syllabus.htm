Course Objective

Through communication we discover and rediscover who we are. Each person is born with distinctive qualities and unlimited potential. Each has a "voice" to contribute. Through communication, we relay our unique "voices" or identities to others. We long to be heard and valued. Unfortunately, many people who yearn to communicate, struggle and often fail to do so. Speech pathologists and audiologists are people who, with unique perspective and understanding, devote themselves to serving those individuals with "unheard voices."

The goal of this course is for you to begin to think like a speech pathologist / audiologist.

In order to accomplish this goal, you will be responsible for the learning and sacrifice that necessarily accompany new ways of thinking. To support you, the course will be divided into three units. In each unit, we will explore one of the following questions:

Unit I What exactly is a speech pathologist and an audiologist? Most of you are in this class because you are considering careers in communicative disorders, or related disciplines. Discovering more about these fields can help you make future educational and vocational choices.

Unit II What is normal communication? Acquiring a strong sense of the bases, development, and value of "normal" communication is a vital foundation for all who desire to understand communicative disorders.

Unit III Who is the "communicatively disordered" individual? We cannot provide service of any kind until we know and understand the people we are serving. Emphasis will be on this final question.

By answering these questions and exploring the issues related to them, we will build a foundation upon which you, as future communication disorders students and professionals, can build your knowledge. But whether you choose to continue in this major or not, thinking like a speech pathologist / audiologist can help you to understand, value, and serve all people as communicators.

Textbook & Materials

You must purchase the following textbook: Speech Correction, 9th edition, by Charles Van Riper & Robert L. Erickson. The late Charles Van Riper is a "founding father" in the field of communicative disorders, and was also a self-declared and self-treated "stutterer." His perspective and depth are obvious, and add invaluable insight to the text. It is a well-written and well-organized introductory text. I hope you’ll find it enjoyable and challenging. You’ll receive the most benefit from it if you read it actively, asking yourself how the information applies to the lecture notes, to the course objective, and to you personally. Forcing your minds to come out and "meet the information halfway" is not easy, but will serve you well in reaching the objective of this course. Because this is an introductory course, diagnostic and therapeutic aspects of the text will not be covered or required. See the schedule that follows for more detailed reading assignments.

You may also want to purchase the lecture notes outline, available at Quick Ship-n-Copy. The purpose of this outline is to aid you in taking notes in class. Although it is not required, students in the past have found it extremely helpful. I believe it costs just under $2.00. An unformatted version can be found on my website.

Everything You Need to Know about Your Grade

Unless you choose to have your projects graded (see "Everything You Need to Know about Projects), your grade will be based on your performance on three types of evaluations described below. Your attendance may also impact your course grade.

Prep. Quizzes

In order to benefit maximally from Comm 202, you must come to class prepared. In order to encourage you to complete assignments and reading, 10-15 Preparation Quizzes worth anywhere from 1 to 10 points each will be given throughout the semester. These will be unscheduled/unannounced, but will be embarrassingly simple if reading and homework assignments were completed, class notes were reviewed, and preparation was thorough. If you miss a quiz because you missed or arrived late to class, you forfeit those points. If you petition, I will consider dropping up to 2 low or zero scores given you have a valid reason for missing or doing poorly on them. Be forewarned that days when the class tends to be particularly empty (like early Monday morning, the day before Spring break, & the last day of class, etc.,) are favorite days for prep. quizzes. Also note that consistently missing these points can eventually have an impact on your grade.

Chapter & Unit Tests

There will be 5 Chapter Tests covering 10 ½ chapters. Each will be worth approximately 25-30 points. They will include mostly True/False, Multiple Choice, and Matching question formats, and will cover only material found in the text. These will be taken in the testing center and will always open on a Monday, Wednesday, or Friday, and close the following Wednesday, Friday, or Monday (see schedule that follows).

There will be three Unit Tests worth approximately 40 - 60 points each (because the bulk of the material will be covered in Unit III, this test will be worth more than the Unit 1 & 2 tests). Test questions will likely include True/False, Multiple Choice, Fill in the Blank, and Short Essay. Unit Tests will focus on lecture material.

As an incentive to do well, if your course percentage is 94% or higher, and you score well on a reeview prep.. quiz at the end of the semester, you will not be required to take the final Unit III test. You will be required to come to class and help your classmates review for the test.

Assignments

There will be a minimal number of assignments given which do not appear on the schedule. Like Projects, these will not be graded, per se. However, completing assignments will have a positive impact on daily preparation quiz scores.

Everything You Need to Know about Projects

You must complete three projects to earn a passing grade. Projects are checked off as being accomplished, but are not graded for points and made part of your percentage UNLESS YOU SO REQUEST. This is not to minimize the effects of the projects on your grade--it is simply an attempt to be honest and practical about the purpose of these projects. Each of the three projects has a specific deadline, and must be ready to be turned in at the start of class on the day it is due. Project write-ups will be accepted after the deadline, however, late projects automatically will be graded–and can earn no more than 35 out of 50 points. Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. Why is it optional to have projects graded?

A1. Some students struggle to show all they gain from class through tests alone. Having their projects graded will allow these students to show achievement in a non-test format. Also, some students have told me they would learn more from the project if they knew it was graded. I’ll do anything I can to encourage the most learning possible.

Q2. Can choosing to have my project graded hurt my grade?

A2. Sure it can. If the percentage you earn on the project is less than your overall class percentage, project grades can bring your overall grade down.

Q3. Should I have my project graded?

A3. If you don’t feel your test scores adequately reflect your learning in this class, AND you believe you can more fully express the depth and/or breadth of your learning through an open-ended project & write-up, you should have your project graded.

Q4. What makes a good project?

A4. Basically, I look for solid coverage of the topic (thorough), and learning/life application (insight). See the guidelines for grading criteria. Fancy graphics, paper, photos, binders, etc., that look nice but do not add depth or breadth to your writing, do little to help your grade.
