Helen Mae Hillman Andrus (43)

Tearing down the Spori building is like tearing out some of my most precious memories, even though the building has been remodeled so much that there is little resemblance to its original form.

In my mind, I walk through rock pillars at the entrance of the campus on the north, up a long cement sidewalk with beautiful big lawns and some trees that have been replaced by modern buildings. We played football and made snowmen and had archery classes on those lawns.

I enter the main door and see a wide stairway that goes up to a large landing where the steps divide and go up from the east and and the west ends of the landing to the second floor. The two sets of stairs leading from the platform to the second floor were open, and to the north was a hall where you could look down on the open stairways. From the hall were entrances to the faculty offices, the registrar’s office, and the bookstore, and there was still room for more classrooms. In one, on the southeast, I had a religion class; and on the southwest, education classes and philosophy.

There were large windows with deep window sills where I used to sit and eat my lunch. There was a hallway (on the main floor) west of the men’s locker room, leading to an outside entrance to the heating plant and to a very small parking area.

The southwest room on the main floor was the Student Union Room. There were chairs, divans, tables, a phonograph with several records used for dances after M.I.A. and other times. It was a place to relax and to have meetings and club parties.

There were large classrooms on the main floor. I think the chemistry department was in the northwest corner room. To the north was a classroom with windows so low to the ground, that, on a dare, I climbed out one of them before class began, and came back in through the front door. I had a religion class and a class in Interpretation in the room in the southeast area.

The college’s president, Hyrum Manwaring, taught philosophy. I recall one day before he arrived in the room a large black and white St. Bernard dog had wandered in, (he occasionally visited campus) and settled down to rest in the back of the room. But when President Manwaring began his lecture, the dog got up and left.

The library and four classrooms covered the third floor, as I recall. Stairs went up on the east and on the west to the two entrances to the library. English from an excellent teacher, Edna Ricks, was (taught) in the northwest room. I wrote my first research paper on Rudyard Kipling for her class. She would emphasize a principle of composition by holding up one of her fingers, shaking it at us and saying, ‘repetition with variation to achieve an effect.’

In the library long tables with chairs that would seat about eight students filled the study area. Book stacks were to the north, I think, and resource material seemed quite adequate for us.

I well remember December 8, 1941, when the entire student body assembled in the library to hear President Franklin D. Roosevelt ask Congress to declare war on the axis powers of Japan and Germany. It was a somber, quiet group.”

Helen Mae Hillman Andrus (43)
Alpine, Utah
