Ricks College News Release
January 26, 2001

Dr. William D. Tidwell, right, and Ricks faculty member Dave Stricklan move a part of a Tidwell's private fossil collection that has been donated to Ricks College.

Prestigous fossil collection to be housed at Ricks

REXBURG, Idaho — A prestigious fossil collection has been donated to Ricks College by a renowned scientist who recently retired from Brigham Young University in Provo, Utah.

Dr. William D. Tidwell of Provo recently donated a portion of his private fossil collection that he acquired during his distinguished career.

The collection of plant fossils, collectively known as a "flora," is the most diverse and complete known from the Carboniferous Period of North America. The flora dates from approximately 285-345 million years ago and contains representative species from forests that predated the dinosaurs.

Tidwell has discovered and named more than 100 fossil plant species from around the world.

Upon retirement as a professor of botany at BYU, a number of museums throughout the world, including the Smithsonian, were interested in housing and studying Tidwell’s collection, says Ricks biology faculty member Dave Stricklan.

However, Stricklan, who is a first-year member of the biology faculty, contacted Tidwell about donating the collection to Ricks and he agreed.

He says Tidwell’s donation was based on several factors, including the change of Ricks to a four-year institution. Tidwell also has a close friendship with Roger Hoggan, dean of the Physical and Mathematical Sciences Division at Ricks. Tidwell also is a native of Idaho, having grown up in Homedale in the southwestern part of the state.

The collection is important because of its completeness and exceptional preservation, Stricklan says. It will help demonstrate to students how plants have changed over the ages.

Much of the collection came from the Manning Canyon Shale Formation in central Utah while other pieces came from throughout the United States.

After the more than 150 pieces have been catalogued, part of the collection will be displayed at the Natural Sciences Museum in the Romney Building. The museum is generally open from 8 a.m. to 5 p.m. on school days.

Stricklan expects that scientists and students will come to Ricks to study the collection.

Tidwell published more than 200 books, articles and abstracts in scientific journals on various fossil plants during his career. These publications contain the descriptions of 24 new genera and more than 100 new species from sites in the United States, Australia and India. They include an ancestor of the Joshua tree, two of the earliest known grasses, the first reported gymnospermous wood of the Lower Permian Age from North America, two new tree fern genera and some animals.

His book, "Common Fossil Plants of Western North America," has been widely used by both amateur collectors and many colleges and universities through the world.

He has been president of the paleobotany section of the Botanical Society of America and nominated for the Savitri Sahni Silver Sammon Award as an outstanding international paleobotanist.
