Texts:  Understanding Social Problems.  3rd Edition.  Mooney, Knox, and Schacht.

            Social Problems of the Modern World: A Reader.  Moulder.

 

Course Description: This course is designed to acquaint the student with current social problems and suggests possible means of prevention and/or solution. Examples of possible study areas are population, poverty, crime, family breakup, inequality, environment, racial problems, etc.

 

Course Objectives:  Upon successful completion of this course the student can be expected to:

 

1.      Define sociology and explain the three major theoretical paradigms:  structural-functionalist, conflict, and symbolic interactionist.

2.      Define social problems by social theories.

3.      Identify relevant issues to specific social problems.

4.      Analyze social problems and provide possible solutions.

Final Grades: Final grades will be calculated on a straight percentage scale as indicated.  Total points earned will be summed and divided by 350.  Extra credit is not offered in this class.

 

Attendance:  Attendance is necessary for the successful completion of this course.  There are 10 participation points that are directly related to attendance.  The quizzes and short essays are all in-class activities.  There is something due almost every class period.  You are responsible for all materials and information that is given in class.  Tardiness or non-attendance does not excuse you from these responsibilities or from any assignments or activities.  This means that no matter the reason, if you do not do the work in class at the appropriate time you will have to make it up according to the make up policy.

 

Make Ups:  You can make up anything in this class or do any of the work late with certain conditions.  Any work done before the due date will not be assessed a late penalty.  Exams that are not completed at the assigned time are subject to change without notice (I will give you a different exam than the scheduled exam.) Any missed assignments are to be made up as soon as possible and will automatically be assessed a 10% penalty and an additional 10% penalty per week.  The final deadline for make up work is Friday of the week before finals (Dec. 12.) 

 

Assignments:

 

Reading assignments.  For most topics there will be readings assigned from the Moulder reader (I will assign them as we go.)   Quizzes, essays, and exams may assess your understanding of these readings.

 

Quizzes.  Quizzes are short and will test your knowledge of content or give you the opportunity to apply your knowledge.  The majority will be traditional quizzes that are five question of varying formats (multi-choice, True/False, fill-in-the-blank, short answer, etc.).  They will cover the chapter or reading assigned for that class period. 

 

Essays.  The essays are short in-class responses to questions that are relevant to the topic discussed that class period.

 

Applications.  There are four application assignments that are due over the course of the semester.  For each application you need to bring to class (with your paper) some evidence of a social problem from the chapters we have covered in that section of the course (there are four section delineated by exams).  This can include a movie clip, music, newspaper clipping, a photograph, etc.  Along with your evidence you need to submit a paper that: 1) identifies the problem, 2) explains which theoretical perspective you are basing your application on, 3) gives a possible solution to the problem, and 4) provides a moral perspective of the issue.  Each paper must be a minimum of one page and no more than three pages in length (one inch margins, black ink, Single Spaced, 12 point - Times New Roman font, NO coversheet).

 

Plagiarism policy:  Plagiarism is not tolerated in this class.  Any evidence of plagiarism will be grounds for failure of the assignment (zero points).  Plagiarism is copying someone else’s work.  This includes your peers, any written sources and the internet.  Any information that is not your own original thoughts should be properly documented.  For example, if you write about the relationship between religion and people, and include complete sentences or ideas from another author you need to acknowledge them.  It is ok to include something that Thomas Jefferson wrote, just make sure that it is clear that you are not presenting it as your own work.  It is appropriate to write:   Thomas Jefferson said, “yada yada yada”.  It is inappropriate to say that religion is the opium of the people without acknowledging that Marx said this, not you.
