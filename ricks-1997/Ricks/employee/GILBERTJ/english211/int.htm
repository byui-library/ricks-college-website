Integrating Source Material

Making the correct introductions is an important part of integrating outside information into your essay. Once you know how all the information or ideas you've collected are related, it's time for your readers to become better acquainted with them. Make sure you make the proper introductions.

  • Strong Verbs - Don't be content with your sources "saying" everything. Let them speak with some gusto and enthusiasm. Remember, always use present tense. Here are some sample verbs you can use when introducing source material: states, argues, contends, offers, claims, supports, strengthens, agrees, disagrees, believes (the list goes on, but let your sources actively do something).

  • Name Names - Use full names the first time you introduce a source; use the last name thereafter. John Smith, President of Source Introduction Incorporated, claims that the only way to adequately introduce a source is to give his/her name and credentials. Smith goes on to say that the most effective sources are those we introduce effectively (345).

  • Fit the Material to Your Essay, Not Vice Versa - There's nothing more jarring than running into a quote that hasn't been properly introduced. "If they don't do something about it, we'll all be very angry." It just sits there by itself, leaving the readers to try and figure out why their reading has been so rudely interrupted. Make sure you properly introduce each source. Try following this pattern when introducing source material.

  1. Introduce the topic
  2. Provide the source's name and credentials
  3. Paraphrase or quote the source passage
  4. Cite the source
  5. Explain why the passage is significant to your discussion

Here's an example using a fictitious source. See if you can trace each of the above steps in this paragraph.

Many professors argue that refusing to properly introduce sources will soon lead to a break in source/author relations. Dr. Lucy Long, National Secretary for Source/Author Relations, goes so far as to claim such breeches of research etiquette may soon halt communication altogether. In a recent speech to the Association for College Composition Teachers, she argues that "the problem has reached epidemic proportions. Soon communication, the very essence of our existence as human beings, will break down. It will end, and we will be left alone in the cold, cruel, world" (345). If, as Long suggests, introducing source material is vital for continued communication, we must take steps to teach students the proper way to treat source material in their writing.
