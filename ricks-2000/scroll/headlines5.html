Behind the scenes of the Mormon Tabernacle Choir

by Ashley Andrus
and01049@byui.edu
Scroll Staff

Singing has always been a large part of Latter-day Saint culture. Whether it be singing at church, firesides, devotionals, as a family, or pioneers crossing the plains, singing has been a welcome part of the gospel, and for many, a way to invite the Spirit.

As a result of this interest in song, the Church has spent years investing in a choir to share a holy message with people from around the world.

The Mormon Tabernacle Choir is now in its 154th year as the world’s oldest organized choir, according to the www.lds.org. The 320-member group has traveled around the globe to more than 30 countries, including Russia, Europe, Australia, Mexico, Brazil, Japan, Canada, New Zealand, and throughout the U.S.

It has received numerous awards in past years, including a Grammy Award, an Emmy Award, a Peabody Award and a Freedom Foundation Award.

The choir has performed in front of several political figures such as U.S. Presidents Nixon, Reagan, Clinton and Bush, and international leaders who often accompany Utah Senator Hatch as his special guest to Music and the Spoken Word each Sunday. In addition to many performances every year, the choir has released more than 150 albums, including five gold and two platinum albums and has recorded with orchestras such as the Philadelphia Orchestra, the New York Philharmonic and the Royal Philharmonic Orchestra of London.

However, behind the world-wide traveling and awards received lie the sacrifices of church members, willing to give of their time and talent to help share their uplifting music.

The choir members receive no pay for their time spent on tour, at practice or during weekly performances. Brent Peterson, choir business manager and a previous choir member, said there are no retired members in the choir, so all members must find a way to juggle their jobs with their commitment to the choir.

“Because this choir is 154 years of age, it has been somewhat built into our culture here in Salt Lake City,” Peterson said. “A lot of employers are understanding, but for many it becomes a problem sometimes. Schoolteachers, lawyers — all of these people have commitments to their jobs.”

In addition to occupations, many members have their families and young children to be concerned about. Many female members must ask spouses, baby-sitters or other family members to watch over their children. The choir is also on tour for about two weeks out of every year, although spouses are allowed to accompany the members if they are able to pay their own individual costs.

For many members, commuting to and from practices and performances proves to be a significant sacrifice. One member reportedly travels 210 miles round-trip to each rehearsal and performance, while still maintaining at least an 80 percent required attendance.

For choir members, being a part of the group is usually their main church calling. Peterson said they are encouraged to keep their other church responsibilities to a minimum and only serve in other capacities if they are able to do so.

“Mostly they are asked to limit their time to visiting teaching and home teaching,” Peterson said.

In addition to singing in the choir, members are required to assist with wardrobes (one for men, one for women), serve as librarians, luggage handlers and tour guides, rather than having the church hire extra people to handle those things.

In order for someone to join the choir, one must first make an audition tape and send it to the choir office during the first week of January or the first week of July.

“If we like what we hear, then we call them in for a more in-depth audition and interview,” Peterson said. “We see how high and low the voice can go, if they can do vibrato as soon as they sing a note, and we interview them about their commitment to the choir, and their family and work situations.”

The minimum age requirement for choir members has been lowered from 30 to 25 years old and members are allowed to remain in the choir for as many as 20 years, or until the age of 60, depending on which comes first.

After qualifying as a member of the choir, a 12-week training session called the Temple Square Chorale follows. Members are also required to take a theory exam before and after the training to test their prior knowledge and to see what they have learned from the session.

While on tour, the choir will sometimes accommodate the program depending on the country or culture it is in. Members usually learn a lullaby, hymn, folk song or favorite tune native to that area, sometimes singing it in their language.

This past year, the choir took a tour around the United States, traveling to eight different cities and performing to sold-out audiences, which touched many of the people in attendance.

"It made the hair on the back of the neck stand straight up and clearly announced that the sell-out crowd of just over 2,000 was in the presence of greatness.” the Fort Worth Star in Texas wrote on June 17 about the choir. “Nobody hallelujahs like these folks hallelujah!"

"I must admit to more than just a few tears; I was moved beyond description,” James C. Gilbert, minister of music from the Cathedral to the Holy Spirit, wrote to a choir member after the choir’s performance in Atlanta this year, “It is my personal belief that everyone in the auditorium felt the presence of the Almighty, at that point. I do not know if you even noticed, but it took a moment for the audience to react after the music had ended. That, I feel, was the work of the Holy Ghost. The evening will remain one of my most cherished memories."
