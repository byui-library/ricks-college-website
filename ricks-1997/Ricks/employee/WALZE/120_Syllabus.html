The course objectives are to:

1. Help students gain an understanding of the basic facts and general themes in American history.
2. Allow students to practice effective writing skills essential in a literate world.
3. Expose students to the historical process of analytical research and writing.
4. Do the above in an environment that builds testimonies of the restored gospel and encourages living its principles.

Introduction:

History 120 is a semester-long survey of American History to 1877. The Course serves as a general introduction to the scholarly study of the American experience-and of history in general. I hope it will fill some gaps in your knowledge, clarify your perceptions of the past, make you aware of important interpretive debates, and, most importantly, demonstrate what it means to analyze a people historically.

Because there are so few class meetings for so much material, the course moves along quickly. It is essential to keep up with the reading and to attend class regularly.

The broad scope of History 120 also means that I will not be able to do full justice to the numerous topics in the history of America. You will find, unfortunately, that many subjects in the past will not be covered (or covered to the extent that you might wish). I encourage you to consider more specialized American history courses as you continue your academic career if you want to examine in greater depth some of the issues that are omitted or only briefly examined in class.

Be sure that you read and understand this syllabus. It defines your responsibilities as well as the course arrangements that I am obligated to maintain. If you do not think you can meet the stated requirements, drop the class-because I adhere strictly to the conditions outlined in the syllabus.

 

Quizzes:

There will be 6 unannounced quizzes given during the semester. Each will be worth 10 points and will be designed to help you grasp the basic facts in American history. Quizzes may take any of the following formats: multiple choice, short identification, or half-page response papers. The questions in the study guide will help you prepare for these quizzes on a chapter by chapter basis as you read the textbook. I do not give make-up quizzes but I will drop your lowest score.

 

Tests:

Tests consist of two mid-term exams (possibly given in the Testing Center) and a final. Each test will contain 100 multiple choice questions worth 1 point each. Questions will be drawn from your text, the outside readers assigned for the class, class lectures, and videos shown during class. The last class period prior to the test will be devoted to reviewing material for the exam.

Class Papers:

Each student will write two papers during the semester. The first paper, based on Cronon's Changes in the Land, will be due in class on 29 September, and will answer the question: "What is Cronon's thesis in this book and how does he support it?" The second question, based on Northup's Twelve Years a Slave, will be due in class on 15 November, and will answer the question "What does Solomon Northup's experience teach us about the ability of slaves to resist the evils of slavery?" Both papers must be 2 pages in length, be type written using acceptable margins and fonts, be double spaced, and be analytical in nature (meaning you will need to state a thesis and support it). Papers will be worth 50 points toward the final grade and will be graded on how clearly the thesis is stated and how well the thesis is supported with evidence from the book. Attention should also be given to proper spelling, grammar, and organization. (I encourage you to take advantage of the writing center.)

 

Final Project:

Early in the semester, each student will identify a historical question from the period covered by this class upon which to write a 3-5 page paper. (Example: What was the major controversy surrounding Alexander Hamilton's creation of a national bank?) Papers must be type written using acceptable margins, be double-spaced, be based on at least 2 primary and three secondary sources, and be analytical in nature (this means you need to state and defend an argument). No more than one internet source will be accepted! Papers will be worth 100 points toward the final grade and will be graded on historical content, spelling, grammar, and organization. (Take advantage of the writing center.) The thesis statement in your introductory paragraph should answer the question clearly. The best papers will use secondary sources to describe how others have answered this question and the primary sources to provide evidence for your answer. The paper will be due in class 6 December. During the last week of class, each student will be asked to make a presentation describing their research experience. In the presentation you should tell the class what your thesis is, how your primary sources support your thesis, and what significance the project had in helping you understand American history.

 

Plagiarism and Cheating:

I feel it necessary to remind all students of the honor code at Ricks and emphasize that cheating in all forms is prohibited thereby. Obviously, copying from someone else's exam or paper is cheating, and is therefore unacceptable behavior. You should also know that in today's world of information access through internet that copying or downloading information from the internet is also cheating. And when you copy someone else's paper or article from the internet and call it your own, you are engaging in plagiarism. When you include information in your class papers or final project that you have gleaned from other sources, it is imperative that you cite those sources in your work. The library has several guides that can help you with these citations and I will be more than happy to help you understand how to identify sources that should be cited. You should know that if you can down load information from the internet, your professor can easily use the internet to verify the source of that information.

 

In Class:

Attendance is not taken in class. I encourage you, however, to recognize the importance of regular attendance:

--In class you hear the particular points of a lecture as well as the general context in which those points are raised. One without the other usually leads to problems on the exams.
--In class you can ask for a clarification of confusing issues (rather than stumbling on a problem just before a test).
--By attending regularly, you are not dependent on someone else's notes-notes that might turn out to be awful.
--In class you can work on a skill that the non-attender never develops: note-taking abilities. You cannot sharpen this talent just by copying notes but only by doing the work yourself; being a good listener, becoming familiar with material in its full form, and learning to discern, organize, and relate the critical components on an argument. I cannot emphasize enough the importance of these skills inside and outside the university.
--In very practical terms, there are many announcements and note sheets in class that you will miss by not being present.
--If you miss class, I do not provide my notes to you.
--I specifically design both my lectures and exams so that the student who fails to attend regularly will not know the answers to all of the exam questions.

In class, I expect you to focus your attention on lecture and discussion material. Chatting with neighbors, reading newspapers, and nodding off to dreamland are all pleasant activities-but during class they are rude and distracting. Worse still, they breakdown the mutual respect between students and teacher that I wish to maintain. If you must talk, read, or sleep, do it outside the classroom. Inside, such behavior has no place-and it will result in a grade penalty.

Feel free to raise questions and comments on lecture material during the class. You can figure out for yourself the importance of clearing up any possible confusion over lecture material. But be aware that your comments and questions also benefit fellow students who might be hesitant to raise the very same issues. And those questions benefit me as well by pinpointing weak spots in a lecture and by raising alternative points of view in the classroom. I encourage you to contribute to the course in this way.
