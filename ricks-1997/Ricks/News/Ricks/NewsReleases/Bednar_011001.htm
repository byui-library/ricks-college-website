Ricks College News Release
January 10, 2001

Devotional: Elder David A. and Susan Bednar

REXBURG, Idaho — The principle of repentance was addressed by Ricks College President David A. Bednar during Tuesday’s opening devotional of the winter semester at the Rexburg College.

"I believe this fundamental principle of repentance is one about which we talk frequently and little understand," said Elder Bednar, who is also a member of the LDS Church Fifth Quorum of the Seventy.

He explained two fundamental realities about the principle of repentance: (1) repentance requires the Redeemer, Jesus Christ, and (2) repentance requires an honest heart and real intent.

"Recognizing and forsaking sin, feeling remorse and making restitution for sin, and confessing sins to God and, when needed, to our priesthood leaders, are all necessary and important elements in the repentance process," Elder Bednar said. "If we do these things and fail to recognize and depend upon the Redeemer and His atoning sacrifice, then even our best efforts are in vain ."

He went on to say, "From the atonement of the Savior flows the soothing salve that can heal our spiritual wounds and remove guilt. . . . The results of sincere repentance are peace, comfort, and spiritual healing and renewing."

Elder Bednar said repentance requires an honest heart and real intent. "As we repent and turn to the Lord, we must have real intent and be honest with Him whose forgiveness we seek . . .While our Heavenly Father obviously knows of our sinful actions, it is vital that we discuss our feelings extensively with Him."

He continued, "Some people who lack understanding about the nature of repentance believe they can commit sin in a calculated and planned way, expecting that at the right time they can quickly and conveniently confess to the bishop. . . . I raise a voice of warning and plead with you . . . please do not be influenced by such false thinking. How can you or I repent and seek forgiveness with real intent when in fact our real intent was to sin? Such scheming and dishonesty mock the atonement of Christ and constitute, in part, what is referred to in the scripture as ‘trampling under their feet the Holy One’ . . . Forgiveness from such sin is possible, but it is not obtained easily."

Prior to Elder Bednar’s remarks, his wife, Susan Bednar, gave some remarks concerning hearts. Ricks’ first lady compared the various kinds of hearts that a child might cut out from construction paper to the parable of the sower found in the Bible’s New Testament.

"Though it is the parable of the sower, it’s really a parable about hearts," she said. "Seeds, which symbolize the word of God, are sown into four different places – by the way side, on stony ground, in with thorns, and on good soil. The places where the seeds fall represent the hearts of men."

"We, too, can follow this pattern," she said. "All hearts can be changed. A heart that turns away from the gospel need only exercise a particle of faith for the Savior’s word to start growing again."

The last heart she discussed was a heart that was cut perfectly on the outside but accidently cut on the folded edge, leaving two halves. "This particular heart is a broken heart. We are commanded to offer for our sacrifice unto the Lord in righteousness a broken heart and a contrite spirit. A broken heart . . . is humble and easily led . . . sets aside pride and selfish ambition to follow Jesus. It recognizes a dependence upon and need for the Savior’s atonement. He stands at the door and knocks. All we have to do is open our hearts and let him enter," she said.
