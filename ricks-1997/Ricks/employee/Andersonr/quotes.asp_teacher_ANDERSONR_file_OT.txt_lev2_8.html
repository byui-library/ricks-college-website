The Endowment of Israel- Jeffrey R. Chadwick
When the Prophet Moses wrote the Book of Genesis he seems to have constructed certain passages in such a way that onlythe spirtually enlightened would completely understand them. This appears to be the case with Genesis 28, the story of Jacob's vision at Bethel. When fully understood, this account is relating the endowment of Jacob, whom we also know by the name of Israel.
As Jacob travelled north from Beersheba to Haran, he stopped to spend the night at a "certain place"-- a high, rocky hilltop just east of the Canaanite settlement of Luz. This "certain place" was the same hill, east of Luz and west of the ruin called Hai or Ai, where Jacob's grandfather Abraham had built an alter and received from the Lord an assurance of the Abrahamic Covenant concerning the growth and mission of his posterity and their connection to the land of Israel (see Genesis 12:8, 13:3-17).
Before leaving Beersheba, Jacob had been given those same blessings, the blessings of the Abrahamic Covenant, by his father Isaac (see Genesis 28:1-4). Now, at the top of that rocky hill-- that "certain place"-- the Lord repeated the blessings that covenant to Jacob as a part of a transcending spiritual experience during the night (see Genesis 28:11-15).
As a part of at great experience, which most English Bible translations indicate was a dream (more properly, a vision, cf. 1 Nephi 8:2), Jacob saw a ladder reaching from earth to heaven, with angels of God ascending and descendingon it (see Genesis 28:12). Of this event, President Marion G. Romney, teaching the general membership of the Church, said " . . . Jacob realized that the covenants he made with the Lord there were rungs on the ladder that he himself would have to climb in order to obtain the promised blessings-- blessings that would entitle him to enter heaven and associate with the Lord." President Romney concluded that" . . . "Temples are to us all what Bethel was to Jcob" ("Temples-- The Gates to Heaven," Ensign, March 1971, p.16.)
President Romney's conclusion may have a more literal meaning than generally supposed. For those members of the Church who have been to the Temple and received the holy endowment, verse 12 of Genesis 28 is highly significant when translated literally from the original Hebrew. The Hebrew word sulam, rendered as ladder, would be more properly understood as a stairway, and the Hebrew term malakay elohim, usually translated as "angels of God," literally means messengers of God. To the endowed Latter-day Saint, the scene of messengers going up and down a stairway from heaven to earth and back should be quite familiar.
In addition to these insights, further understanding of Jacob's experience is gained from the Prophet Joseph Smith, who taught that ". . . the three principle rounds of Jacob's ladder (are) the telestial, the terrestrial, and the celestial glories or kingdoms" (Teachings of the Prophet Joseph Smith, p.305).
Knowing all this, what regular Temple visitor could fail to recognize what happened to father Jacob on that rocky hilltop, as he 1) made and received covenants and 2) learned of the three degrees of glory, while 3) messengers came down from and returned to heaven!
As a part of the covenant process, Jacob made promises to the Lord which included his oath to pay tithing (see Genesis 28:20-22). But other promises made and received at that "certain place" were not recorded by the prophetic writer for his mosaic-dispensation readers. When morning arrived, Jacob called the site "Bethel", which in Hebrew (beyt-el) means house of God, a direct reference to the Temple experience he had there (see Genesis 28:17-19).
Of course, one is cautioned in the Temple to treat the things given there with the utmost reverance and respect, and it is improper to discuss certain parts of the ceremony outside those sacred walls. The comments here are necessarily limited to a retranslation of Genesis 28:12, and to a statement of the Prophet Joseph Smith. To suggest even more detail to Jacob's marvelous experience at Bethel might violate some most significant vows. It is important, in this connection, to remember the words of President Harold B. Lee: "When I meet with our missionaries and they ask questions about things pertaining to the Temple, I say to them, as I close the discussion, 'I don't dare answer any of your questions unless I can find an answer in the standard works or in the authentic declarations of the presidents of the Church'" (Ensign, December 1972, p.3). Still, from a Hebrew reading of Genesis 28, President Romney's statement carries a most significant and literal meaning-- "Temles are tos us all what Bethel was to Jacob."
Notes: 1. Only part of what Latter-day Saints call the "endowment" occurred in the Genesis 28 account. As in the days of Joseph Smith, when the ordinances of the Temple came forth over time, the final portion and transcendent climax of Jacob's endowment seems to have occurred in Genesis 32:24-32, which reports Jacob's interview at Peniel, his new and symbolic name "Israel," and his exclamation that he had seen God face to face.
2. The translation of the Hebrew word malak as "messenger" may be found in any lexicon of Biblical Hebrew-- malakay ("messengers") being the plural construct form-- and the rendering of the Hebrew word sulam (root sll) as "stairway" is suggested by E. A. Speiser in The Anchor Bible-- Genesis, gen. eds. W. F. Albright and David Noel Freedman (Garden City, New York: Doubleday & Co. Inc., 1982), pp.217-218.
3. The Canaanite settlement named Luz, west of the "certain place," was also later called "Bethel," but not until several centuries after Jacob, when Israelites captured the city (see Joshua 12:9 and Judges 1:22-23). It is clear from a careful reading of the Genesis account that the spiritual experiences of both Abraham and Jacob took place not in the city, but on the uninhabited, rocky hilltop to the east (see Genesis 28:19).

Faith Precedes the Miracel- Pres. Spencer W. Kimball
What is love? Many people think of it as mere physical attraction and they casually speak of "falling in love" and "love at first sight." This may be Hollywood's version and the interpretation of those who write love songs and love fiction. True love is not wrapped in such flimsy material. One might become immediately attracted to another individual, but love is far more than physical attraction. It is deep, inclusive, and comprehensive. Physical attraction is only one of the many elements; there must be faith and confidence and understanding and partnership. There must be common ideals and standards. There must be great devotion and companionship. Love is cleanliness and progress and sacrifice and selflessness. This kind of love never tires or wanes, but lives through sickness and sorrow, poverty and privation, accomplishment and disappointment, time and eternity. (Pres. Kimball, Faith Precedes the Miracle, pp.157-58.)
Eternal Love- Boyd K. Packer
While I am sure some young couples have some special guidance in getting together, I do not believe in predestined love. If you desire the inspiration of the Lord in this crucial decision, you must live the standards of the Church, and you must pray constantly for the wisdom to recognize those qualities upon which a successful union may be based. You must do the choosing, rather than to seek for some one-and-only so-called soul mate, chosen for you by someone else and waiting for you. You are to do the choosing. you must be wise beyond your years and humbly prayerful unless you choose amiss. (Boyd K. Packer, Eternal Love, p.11.)
Agency or Inspiration- Bruce R. McConkie
How do you choose a wife? I've heard a lot of young people from Brigham Young University and elsewhere say, "I've got to get a feeling of inspiration. I've got to get some revelation. I've got to fast and pray and get the Lord to manifest to me whom I should marry." Well, maybe it will be a little shock to you, but never did in my life did I ever ask the Lord whom I ought to marry. It never occurred to me to ask him. I went out and found the girl I wanted; she suited me; I evaluated and weighed the proposition, and it just seemed a hundred percent to me as though this ought to be. Now, if I'd done things perfectly, I'd have done some counseling with the Lord, which I didn't do; but all I did was pray to the Lord and ask for some guidance and direction in connection with the decision that I'd reached. A more perfect thing to have done would have been to counsel with him relative to the decision and get a spiritual confirmation that the conclusion, which I by my agency and faculties had arrived at, was the right one. (Bruce R. McConkie, "Agency or Inspiration," The New Era, Jan. 1975, p.40.)
