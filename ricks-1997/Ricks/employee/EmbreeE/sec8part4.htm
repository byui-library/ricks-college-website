Comprehending Chaos

Margaret J. Wheatley

February 1993

Brigham Young Magazine

COMPREHENDING CHAOS

Why is chaos such a common experience in our personal and organizational lives of late? Why do so many of us feel as though we're barely holding it together? Though we constantly hear about "managing change," who among us feels any genuine sense of control over change? Most of us aren't managing anything. We're hanging on, overwhelmed, submerged in the turbulence that swirls around us at ever-increasing levels. But our growing recognitions of chaos doesn't need to signal that we're moving into a hopeless world. The increasing presence of chaos in our lives is challenging us to come to a new understanding of the role chaos plays in life. Life is becoming more and more turbulent, I believe, as a means of prodding us into a new relationship with issues of order, control, and flexibility. It's not that we're losing control; rather, we're being asked to rethink the very question of what control means.

My own life and work have been profoundly altered by a new understanding of chaos and the purposes it serves in the cycles of life. I have come to appreciate chaos not as a disruptive force, but as a player in the inherently well-ordered processes of the universe. I have found insights into chaos' positive role in several places--the ancient Greeks, modern science, and Joseph Smith's prophetic visions about how to govern people.

Several thousand years ago, when primal forces haunted human imagination, great gods arose in myths to explain the creation of all beings. At the very beginning, according to the Greeks, were two gods: Chaos, the endless, yawning chasm devoid of form or fullness; and Gaia, the mother of the earth who brought forth form and stability. These two gods were partners, primordial powers engaged in a duet of opposition and resonance, creating everything we know.

The Greeks understood that Chaos' great yawning chasm yielded life, that from darkness came the support and opposition necessary for creative expression. In the intertwining dance between Chaos and Gaia came worlds of creation, dispelling the darkness. Yet life always carried within it the roots its origin, the dark of chaos.

These mythic figures of Chaos and Gaia again inhabit our imaginations and our science. They are taking on new vitality as we explore more deeply the workings of our universe. In the past several years, scientists have been able to glimpse into the heart of chaos with high-speed computers, and they too have observed the dance of which the Greeks spoke thousands of years ago.

Watching chaos emerge on a computer screen is a mesmerizing experience. The computer tracks the behavior of a chaotic system, recording a moment in the system's history as a point of light on the screen. With the speed typical of computers, millions of moment in the system's history can be observed within a short period. The system careens back and forth, never landing twice on the same spot. This is true chaos --a state of total unpredictability. But as the system continues its wild ramblings, it soon becomes evident that the lines of motion are weaving their strands in a pattern. There is order to this disorder. The chaotic movements of the system have a shape. The order is a "strange attractor" and what has appeared on the screen is the shape of chaos.

Chaos has always had a shape, but until it could be observed through the eyes of modern computers, scientists only saw turbulence, energy without form or direction. Chaos is the final state in a system's movement away from predictability. Not all systems move into chaos, but if a system is dislodged from its stable state, it moves first into a period of oscillation, swinging back and forth between different states. If it then moves from this oscillation, the next state is full chaos, a period of totally random and unpredictable behavior. Of the strange attractor, David Ruelle, one of the scientists who named these objects, says, The name is beautiful and well suited to these astonishing objects, of which we understand so little (in Conveney and Highfield, 1991, 204).

Chaos is now understood in an intriguingly paradoxical way as order without predictability. We cannot predict individual moments in the life of a system, but the end result of its seemingly random wanderings is a lovely and discernible order. This is the ancient paradox of chaos depicted in its mythic pairing with order. In every system lurks the potential for chaos, "a creature slumbering deep inside the perfectly ordered system" (Briggs and Peat, 1989, 62). But chaos, when it erupts, does not exceed the bounds of its strange attractor. When we step back to observe the shape of things over time, we see the patterns of movement from chaos to order and from order to chaos. "Here," recalls chaos scientist Doyne Farmer, "was one coin with two sides. Here was order, with randomness emerging, and then one step further away was randomness with its own underlying order" (in Gleick 1987, 252).

Chaos of this nature (known as deterministic chaos) is created by iterations or repetitions in a non-linear system, information feeding back on itself and changing in the process. A value is put into a mathematical process, or function. The function computes for the next value, which is then fed back into the process. Iterations can be repeated infinitely, creating new values with each computation. If the equation is non-linear, strange things can occur very quickly. Very slight variances in the conditions of the equation, variances so small they may not even be discernible, amplify into unpredictable results as they are fed back on themselves. The straw that broke the camel's back is one familiar example of non-linearity: A very small change had an impact far beyond what could have been predicted. All living systems are non-linear, so it is important to note these effects of iteration. In any non-linear system, iterations can take the system in any direction, away from anything we might expect.

Until recently, scientists discounted the effects of non-linearity, even though it abounds in life. Most of us have been trained to believe that small differences average out, the slight variances converge toward a point, and that approximations give us a fairly accurate picture of what will happen. But chaos theory has ended all that. In dynamic, changing systems -in life- the slightest variation can have explosive results. Hypothetically, were we to create a difference in two values as incomprehensively small as rounding them off to the 31st decimal place (calculation numbers this large would require a computer of gigantic proportions), after only 100 iterations the whole calculation would go askew. The paths of the two systems would have diverged in unpredictable ways. Even infinitesimal differences are far from inconsequential. "Chaos takes theme physicist James Crutchfield said, "and blows them up in your face" (in Briggs and Peat 1989, 73).

Scientists now emphasize the very small differences at the beginning of a system's evolution that make prediction impossible, calling this "sensitive dependence on initial conditions." Edward Lorenz, a meteorologist, first drew modern-day attention to this with his "butterfly effect." Does the flap of a butterfly wing in Tokyo, Lorenz queried, affect a tornado in Texas (or a thunderstorm in New York)? Unfortunately for the future of accurate weather prediction, his answer was "yes."

Science has been profoundly affected by this new relationship with the non-linear character of our world. Many of the prevailing assumptions of scientific thought have had to be recanted. As scientist Arthur Winfree expressed it:

The basic idea of Western science is that you don't have to take into account the falling of a leaf on some planet in another galaxy when you're trying to account for the motion of a billiard ball on a pool table on earth. Very small influences can be neglected. There's a convergence in the way things work, and arbitrarily small influences don't blow up to have arbitrarily large effects (in Gleick, 1987, 15).

But chaos theory has proved these assumptions false. The world is far more sensitive than we had ever thought. We may harbor the hope that we will regain predictability as soon as we can learn how to account for all variables, but in fact no level of detail can ever satisfy this desire. Iteration creates powerful and unpredictable effects in non-linear systems. In complex ways that no model will ever capture, the system feeds back on itself, infolding all that has happened, magnifying slight variances, encoding it in the system's memory-and prohibiting prediction, ever.

But what does this mean? Is life so fragile that we need to live in fear of chaos constantly? Is stability so ephemeral that we can't trust it? Looking deeper into chaos answers these questions with both a yes and a no. Any non-linear system can surprise us with a sudden shift into chaos, but on the other side of chaos lies order. Some scientists now call chaos "the science of wholeness." They speculate that chaos arises because the wholeness of the universe resists being studied in pieces. Briggs and Peat, in an intriguing exploration of the mirror world of chaos and order, suggest that wholeness is What rushes in under the guise of chaos whenever scientists try to separate and measure dynamical systems as if they were composed of parts. . . . The whole shape of things depends upon the minutes" part. The part is the whole in this respect for through the action of any part, the whole in the form of chaos or transformative change may manifest" (1989, 74-75). The strange attractors that form on our screens, they suggest, are not the shape of chaos. They are the shape of wholeness.

Lessons about order and wholeness are also revealed in one of the other shapes of chaos-fractals. Less than 20 years ago, the world was introduced to the geometry of fractals, exquisite shapes created by simple mathematical formulae that have repeated back on themselves millions of times, plotting the result of each calculation as a point on the computer screen. The equations change as they are fed back on themselves. After countless iterations, their tracks materialize' into form, creating detailed shapes. These shapes are jagged, curved, rough-edged, and of great complexity. They mimic the complexity and irregularity we see everywhere around us. The straight lines of Euclid have disappeared, giving way to curves and rolls and dimensions that much more closely resemble the shapes of natural objects. This new geometry is allowing scientists to look into nature with new eyes. As Benoit Mandelbrot, the discoverer of this new math, says: "Shapes which are not fractal are the exception. I love euclidean geometry, but it is quite clear that it does not give a reasonable presentation of the world. Mountains are not cones, clouds are not spheres, trees are not cylinders, neither does lightning travel in a straight line. Almost everything around us is noneuclidean"' (in Garcia, 1991, frontis piece).

Fractals are astonishing objects in many ways. Working with them has delighted many a mathematician and amateur computer player because of their sheer beauty. But these shapes beguile not only with their sheer beauty. But these shapes beguile not only with their beauty, but with what they reveal about how complexity is created in natural systems, how chaotic behavior manifests into forms. Fractals are Simultaneously well-ordered and wildly chaotic," (in Garcia, 1991, 37). Like strange attractors, they provide a deeper glimpse into a chaos that displays great

order.

We don't need the phase space of computers to look at fractal objects. They are everywhere around us, in the patterns of nature found in clouds, mountains, rivers, trees, plants. We observe fractals daily. The discovery of fractals has allowed scientists to begin to extrapolate the rules of a geometry that mimic natural shapes.

One of the astonishing characteristics of fractals is their property of self-similarity. The shapes that appear at one level of scale will be repeated everywhere in the fractal's area. What we see at on magnification we will see at all others. No matter how deeply we look at a computer-generated fractal, the same forms are evident as we peer down through very great magnifications (of well over a trillion). There is pattern within pattern within pattern. There is no end to them, no scale small enough that these intricate shapes cease to form. Because the formations go on forever, there is no way to ever gain a finite measurement of them. We could follow the outline of forms forever, and at ever smaller levels there would always be something more to measure. Looking deep into a fractal is an experience of looking into infinity. Yet this infinity is bounded, contained within the area of the fractal.

Fractals reveal many important principles and paradoxes. First, they are objects whose precise measurement is unknowable. Just as chaos is order without predictability, fractals reveal infinite length within a bounded space. Mandelbrot's seminal fractal exercise was a simple question posed to colleagues and students. "How long is the coast of Britain?" As his colleagues soon learned, there is never a final answer to this question. It all depends on what you choose to measure it with: the closer you zoom in on the coastline, the more there is to measure . An ant, climbing over each clump of sand and pebbles, experiences a very different length of beach than a human jogging down the shore, or a satellite taking pictures.

Since there can be no definitive measurement, no final quantitative result, fractals focus our attention on the quality of the system --its complexity, distinguishing shapes, and how it differs from other fractals. If we try to focus on quantitative measures, we are always frustrated by the incomplete and never ending information we receive. Fractals, in stressing qualitative measurement, force us to take a system's or holistic perspective, reminding us that what we can know, and what is important to know, is the shape of the whole, how it moves and changes, or compares to another system. This lesson has important implications for our organizational lives.

In organizations, we are very good at measuring activity; In fact, that is primarily what we do. Fractals suggest the futility of searching for ever finer measures of discrete parts of the system. There is never a satisfying end to this reductionist search, never an end point where we finally know everything about even one part of the system. When we study the individual parts or try to understand the system through its quantities, we get lost in a world we can never fully measure nor appreciate. Scientists of chaos are learning to study shapes in motion. If we were to approach organizations in a similar way, what would constitute the shape and motion of an organization?

We have started edging toward this in the growing focus on studying organizations as whole systems rather than as discrete tasks and functions. Some organizations now use complex system modeling processes that reveal the complexity of interactions between seemingly separate departments or problems. They attempt to discern the overall shape of the organization or issue under study, and the dynamics or its movements. In other organizations, the analytic tools introduced in corporate quality programs serve a similar function. Although they rely initially on diverse and minute mathematical information, their real effectiveness comes when employees are able to stand back and appreciate the complex and ever-changing shape of the organization, of how multiple forces work together.

Even without building complex models, each of us can look for the dynamic shape of an issue by changing how we approach the problem. Can we step back from the problem and gain enough perspective so that its shape emerges out of the myriad variables that influence it? Can we look for themes, patterns, and trends rather than isolated causes? For so long, we have been conditioned and trained to analyze problems into parts. But the science of fractals (and many other areas of science) are pushing us to a new level of analysis, one that accounts for the system as a system, where it is impossible to know what will happen to a part without a vision of the whole.

A second important lesson to be leamed from fractals focuses on how nature creates its complex forms and predictable shapes. Mountains, coastlines, trees, lungs, circulatory systems, broccoli--all of these (and millions more) are fractal, replicating a dominant pattern at ever-smaller levels. The-scientist Michael Bamsley was intrigued to see if he could recreate the shapes of naturals objects by deducing the initial equations that described their forms. He invented the "Chaos Game," decoding objects to derive the equations that expressed certain global information about their shapes (his first attempt was with a fern). These equations captured only essential information about the shape. They were surprisingly simple, devoid of the levels of precise prescriptive information we might expect. Barnsley then interjected randomness, setting the formulae loose to feed back on themselves. They were allowed to follow their own iterative wanderings, working at whatever scale they chose. With this approach, he successfully reproduced an entire computer garden of plant shapes.

His work with Random "fractals" and the chaos game are extremely instructive. First, Barnsley shows that predictability still exists. The shapes that he created are not random in any way. They are built into the formula. But indeterminism (randomness) also plays a key role. It is randomness that leads to the creation of the pattern at all different levels of the scale. The same shapes appear predictably everywhere with only simple levels of instruction and large amounts of freedom. It seems that with a few simple guidelines, left to repeat on themselves randomly, nature creates the complexity and harmony of form we see everywhere.

I have asked groups of managers how they would go about creating a production facility that manufactured standardized ferns. If the task was for each worker to create a fern, how would they structure the work? The responses are predictable. Managers suggest giving workers a template or pattern to copy; or they advise making a mold of the fern; or they would give them detailed, step-by-step directions for carving out each curve and frond. Each of these suggestions reflect our traditional ways of assuring reliability and consistent product design. We believe that only through clear models, elaborate guidelines, and multiple policies that reinforce the correct behavior will we succeed in achieving predictable, quality results.

But this is not what the chaos game teaches us. The chaos game, with its glimpse into nature's complexibility and reliability, says something quite different. It says that given the basic form or set of instructions, and large amounts of freedom, complex, beautiful, and predictable shapes will be created that meet specifications.

This idea has direct implications for the leadership of organizations (and people everywhere). The very best organizations have a fractal quality to them. An observer of such an organization can tell what the organization's values and ways of doing business are by watching anyone, whether it be a production floor employee or a senior manager. There is a consistency and predictability to the quality of behavior. No matter where we look in these organizations, self-similarity is found in its people, in spite of the complex range of roles and levels.

How is this quality achieved? The potent force that shapes behavior in these fractal organizations, as in all natural systems, are simply expressed expectations of acceptable behavior that are free to assert themselves in non-deterministic ways. Fractal organizations, though they may never have heard the word fractal, have reamed to trust in natural organizing phenomena. They trust in the power of guiding principles or values, knowing that they are strong enough influencers of behavior to shape every employee into a desired representative of the organization. These organizations expect to see similar behaviors show up at every level in the organization because those behaviors were patterned into the organizing principles at the very start.

Fractals and the science of chaos speak to a deep principle found in other areas of science as well. Ecosystems are able to maintain a stable structure overall because they tolerate great levels of change at local levels throughout the system. Whether we are studying a biological system or a mathematical rendering of a chaotic system, the structure is capable of maintaining its overall shape and a large degree of independence from environmental fluctuations because each part of the system is free to express itself and adapt as needed. Fluctuations, randomness, and unpredictability at the local level cohere over time into definite and predictable form at the global level. This odd combination of predictability and randomness, of determinism and free will, has become a dominant theme in chaos science. One scientist describes his early attraction to chaos theory because it resolved the eternal questions of how free will could be expressed and have value in an orderly universe: "The system is deterministic, but you can't say what it's going to do next." (Gleick, 1987, 251).

These ideas speak with a simple clarity to the issues of effective leadership. They bring us back to the importance of simple governing principles: guiding visions, strong values, organizational beliefs --the few formulae individuals can use to shape their own behavior. Our task is to communicate, keep them ever-present, and clear, and then to allow the system its random, sometimes chaotic meanderings. In fact, these ideas bring us back to Joseph Smith's powerful and prophetic vision for leaders: "I teach them correct principles and they govern themselves."

Anyone who has exercised leadership in an organization, church group, or family, knows how hard it is to practice the restraint and faith implicitly in Joseph Smiths principle. We are afraid of randomness and autonomy, afraid that open self-expression and personal freedom will drag us deeper into a chaos without form or meaning. We have become conditioned to believe that the only way we can create order is through the exercise of clear controls--telling people what to do and doing this at ever greater levels of detail. If we sense that chaos is increasing, our conditioned response is to charge in, issue new policies, exert more control, take over the task --anything to avoid the descent into darkness that we fear as chaos.

But I believe that Joseph Smith truly understood the workings of chaos, and how real order is created. He understood that the dominant shape of an organization (or a person) can be maintained if we are clear about the purpose and direction of the organization. His simple statement directs us to maintain focus and direction, rather than hands-on control. As leaders, we are called upon to shape our organizations through concepts, not through elaborate rules or structures. We need to learn that chaos -- in the guise of independent actions and free will -- can be the path to the creation of a harmonious pattern that we have set in motion by the clear articulation of guiding principles and values.

There is an important distinction to make here. For too long we have confused control with order; we have believed that the only way to maintain order was through careful and deliberate controls. Natural systems have a different relationship with order. They are part of a universe that is ordered by subtle processes that use chaos to create order, and free will to reach predetermined outcomes. Whether we look to the teachings of our first prophet, or to the work of modern chaos scientists; we need to begin contemplating the fact that order can be achieved without elaborate controls.

Ever since my imagination was captured by chaos, its fractals, and strange attractors, I have wondered if we could identify any strange attractors in organizations. Is there any magnetic force, any basin for activity that is so attractive that it pulls all behavior toward it and creates coherence? My current belief is that we do have such attractors at work in organizations, and that one of the most potent ones is meaning, a force powerful enough to cohere seemingly random behaviors into a pattern.

I have observed companies make deliberate use of meaning to move through times of chaotic change. Leaders made great effort to speak forthrightly and frequently to employees about current struggles, the tough times that lay ahead, and what they dreamed for the future. These conversations filled . a turbulent period with new direction, giving reasons for the current need to sacrifice and hold on. In most cases, given this kind of meaningful information, workers responded with allegiance and energy, doing what they thought would contribute to the overall goal of the organization. Out of chaotic times, organization emerged, shaped by the individual contributions of employees, shaped by the attractor of meaning that had been created. As long as purpose is kept in focus in both our organizational and private lives, we wander through the realms of chaos, make decisions about what actions will be consistent with our purpose, and emerge with a discernible pattern or shape to our lives.

When a meaning attractor is in place in an organization, employees can be trusted to move freely, drawn in many directions by their energy and creativity. There is no need to insist, through regimentation or supervision, that any two individuals act in precisely the same way. We know they will be affected and shaped, their freedom never going out of bounds. We trust that they will heed the call of the attractor and stay within its basin. We come to understand that little else is required except the cohering presence of a purpose that gives people a point- of reference for assessing their own behavior.

In chaos theory, no one can ever tell where the system is headed until it has been observed over time. This element to time is also true for organizations, and it is what makes trusting something as ethereal as a strange atttractor difficult. It takes time to see if a meaning-rich, values-driven organization really works. A few of them are already out there, bright beacons to the future. But even if these types of organizations have not been part of our own experience, we have other evidence close at hand, because our own lives evolve in just such a fashion. By the end of our lifetime, we are able to discern the deep order that has been present all along. Seen over many years, a discernible shape has emerged. Seemingly random events and choices now appear. "Chance" meetings now appear part of the intentional design of our lives.

We discover that our chaotic and confused wanderings have been guided by an attractor, one that is wholly and uniquely our own. We experience a deeper knowledge of the purpose that structured all of our activities, many times invisibly and without our awareness. We become aware of the order that is imbedded in the design of the universe. At the end, we understand the great power of purpose, of how it dispels the illusion of chaos. We have been like ancient Gala, who boldly embraced the void, knowing that from his dark depths she would always pull forth order.
