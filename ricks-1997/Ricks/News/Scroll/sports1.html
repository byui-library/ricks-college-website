He doesn't yell; he teaches

 

Dixon: the man behind the D
By Aaron Barrow, Scroll Staff




by Brian Passey Scroll Staff

 


A friend of Ricks College defensive coordinator, Rick Dixon, once joked that if Dixon had a beard and sat in the mall around Christmas time, children would confuse him for Santa Claus and try to sit on his lap.
For those who know coach Dixon, this scenario does not seem inconceivable. He fits the physical description of Saint Nicholas perfectly. But if it were to ever come to a show down, Santa would be on the short end of the stick. Sure Santa can fit his immense figure down a chimney, but did he ever bench press close to 500 pounds or hit a man during a football game so hard that he flew out of bounds, past the bench area and onto the track? Dixon has.
Dixon gained his foundation in sports in Cincinnati. His father, a World War II veteran, shared with Dixon and his brothers the importance of hard work and a strong enthusiasm for athletics. It did not take long for young Dixon to develop a desire to follow in his father's footsteps - both as a person and as an athlete. In fact, Dixon's father would become his lifetime role model.
"My father would always have time for me. When he would come home from work, I knew he was tired, but he always had time to hit me fly balls or pitch to me until I had had enough or it got too dark to see," Dixon said.
When Dixon was in seventh grade, his father introduced him to weightlifting, an activity that would become a passion and aid him in becoming a top high school prospect in both baseball and football. Dixon's father always believed his son could become a professional baseball player, but when the opportunity to play football at BYU arose after high school it was too tempting to pass up.
While playing linebacker at BYU, Dixon described himself as "just an average player," but his teammates still remember his physical strength and strong work ethic. Dixon worked so hard in the weight room and became so large that he was given the nickname "the human coke machine."
"He was very dedicated in the weight room and loved to be involved in everything the team did," Dave Allphin, a former BYU teammate, said.
After his senior year at BYU, Dixon had planned to go on a mission but instead decided to get married, a decision that influences his advice to his own players here.
"The Lord said to go on a mission. I tell my players to go, and if I did not speak that way, I feel as if the Lord would be upset with me," Dixon said.
After a brief coaching stint at BYU immediately following graduation, Dixon became an assistant football coach for Oklahoma State University during the 1970s.
After several years in Oklahoma, Dixon took a job coaching at North Texas University. By that time, Dixon and his wife had three sons and a daughter. As the children got older, Dixon realized he needed to spend more time with his family and less time recruiting. Dixon decided to quit coaching and worked in various places as a broker, steel manufacturer and then as an independent oil distributer.
But in 1991, Dixon's heart returned to football. He landed a job at Ricks College along side his former coaches, Ron Haun and Wayne Startin.
"I have done a lot of different things in my life, and I do not regret any of it. I've made a lot of mistakes, but it has been a good education for me," Dixon said.
On the field, Dixon applies many of his father's philosophies to teach his players. He does not yell at his players; rather he teaches a principle and lets the players perform on their own. He shows the players respect, and in return, his players show him even more respect.
"He shows sincere interest for each of the kids," Ricks linebacker Russell Kay said. "If he needs to get on a player, he will, but he lets you know that he doesn't take your mistakes personally. He genuinely likes everyone. He takes the time to learn everyone's name. He's a really good guy."
"I just hope that when I die, my kids and those that knew me will say that I was a good guy," Dixon said with a smile. "and those that I don't like, I'll ask them to carry my coffin because it will be plenty heavy."
