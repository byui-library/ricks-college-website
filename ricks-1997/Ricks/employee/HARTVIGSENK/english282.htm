Purpose of this course
The purpose of English 282 is to probe Shakespeare’s genius to come to a greater appreciation of literature and the power of language to communicate human experience. We will try not to take a "text-bookish" approach to the pieces we read; rather, we will explore them to see what they tell us about our own humanity: how we can be happier people, better Latter-day Saints, and heroes of our own lives.

Class philosophy
Perhaps you have come to believe that a story or poem has only one "right" meaning and that your job is to draw it out, no matter the difficulty. You may even wait for others to tell you what a text means because you don't trust your instincts. But part of the fun of reading–especially Shakespeare, whose plays are open to various interpretations–is arriving at and defending your own ideas. It’s also pleasurable to relate these discoveries to your experience, thus making reading a source of introspection and self-awareness. Please, then, be prepared to share what these stories and poems make you think about and how they relate to your experiences and feelings.

Requirements
Please 1) read all assignments and be prepared to respond to a short quiz at the beginning of most class periods;
2) faithfully keep a reading journal in response to questions and problems I pose before each reading assignment;
3) memorize a total of 80 lines from assigned plays (I will ask you to recite or write for memory 20 consecutive or scattered lines on calendar days designated "Retrospect"); 4) take a midterm and final exam; and 5) share your ideas in class.

Texts you will need
1) The Complete Works of Shakespeare, updated fourth edition, David Bevington, editor (Please purchase this edition–much of our class reading is taken from the editorial sections of this text), and 2) a 8" x 10" composition book.

Plays we will read
1) The Taming of the Shrew, 2) Much Ado About Nothing, 3) Macbeth, 4) As You Like It, 5) Henry V, 6) Twelfth Night, and 7) The Tempest.

The A range is 93 to 100% of all possible points; A-, 90-92%; B+, 87-89%; B, 83-86%; B-,80-82%; and so on.

 The Taming of the Shrew, Act 1. As you read the first two plays, please pace yourself through the background material entitled "Shakespeare’s Life and Works," beginning in Bevington on p. liii (about 2 to 3 pages per class). Is there evidence in The Taming of the Shrew to suggest that Shakespeare intended a topsy-turvy interpretation: that Kate is no shrew at all except in the most superficial sense and that Bianca, on the other hand, is just what her sister is supposed to be?

And perhaps even more provocative, is there evidence to suggest that as the play ends, Kate is going to be more nearly the tamer than the tamed–and Petruchio more nearly the tamed than the tamer (though his wife naturally will keep the true situation undercover)? As you read, log passages that argue for or against this interpretation–or a variation of it–commenting as you go.

Macbeth is about the human potential for evil. The play is haunting because it documents step by step the triumph of evil in a man with many good qualities. As you read the play, log scenes that 1) demonstrate Macbeth’s capacity for good, and 2) show Macbeth’s willful choice of evil. Henry V

Nov 2

 Henry V, Act 1. As you read Henry V, please study the background material entitled "The Drama Before Shakespeare," beginning in Bevington on page xxx (about 2 pages per class). Henry V is one of Shakespeare’s most popular history plays. A 1940's film version helped to rally the British when Hitler was bombing London. More recently, however, Kenneth Branaugh’s Henry was a bloody and horrific depiction of war. These varied interpretations remind us of this play’s central ambiguity. It may be seen in two very different ways: "as an epic patriotic drama or as a satirical exposure of vicious hypocrisy, depending on one’s interpretation of its protagonist" (Charles Boyce).

As you read the play, log passages that support either of these interpretations. Is Henry essentially "a hero whose exuberant leadership carries England to triumph over a traditional foe" or a coldly Machiavellian politician who is indifferent to the human costs of war"?

The Tempest

Dec 2

 The Tempest, Act 1.   Some commentators find The Tempest to be the greatest accomplishment of Shakespeare’s career–"a moral allegory of great beauty and emotional power" (Charles Boyce). Much of the pleasure of a well-conceived allegory is in its power to communicate different ideas to different readers, depending on their own life experiences. Note, for example, the several insights a religious parable like the "Prodigal Son" suggests about family, repentance, love, forgiveness, and so forth.

As you read The Tempest, consider the allegorical richness of this play. Jot down your ideas about how characters, settings, and actions take on symbolic meanings that combine to form a unified theme. When you finish Act V, come to class prepared to share your interpretation of this allegory–the meaning that you see beyond the literal.
