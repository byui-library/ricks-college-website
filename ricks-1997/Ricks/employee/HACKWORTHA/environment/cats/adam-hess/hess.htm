Getting Acquainted with Cougars
by Adam Hess
 
What is it about mountain lions that inspires admiration and respect? Maybe it’s their ability to slide through their environment as though they were shadows or ghosts.  Maybe it’s their almost human ability to adapt and live in diverse conditions.  Or maybe it’s their fear inspired by centuries of conflict with humans.

Robert Redford wrote, in the preface of Kevin Hansen’s book Cougar: the American Lion, "The cougar works a powerful magic on the human imagination" (Mountain Lion Foundation). Whatever the cause this marvel may be, these animals deserve our respect. To respect them we must first gain fundamental knowledge.  To better understand cougars, I like compare and contrast them with the  domestic, house cat.   I then discuss the unique characteristics of the mountain lion.

The domestic cat, known by the scientific name Felis catus, is a small, mainly carnivorous animal weighing 6-12 pounds. The Egyptians worshipped them as gods.  During Medievil times, they were reguarded as evil creatures, due largely to an order from one Pope to destroy all cats because they were "evil creatures of the night" (Farheen).  The early colonizers of America brought cats along on their journey to control pests.

The domestication of this species is thought to have originated when the African wildcat, Felis libyca, was domesticated by the Egyptians around 2500 BC. These domesticated wildcats were then transported to Europe where they interbred with the smaller indigeounous wildcats. Whatever their true origin, one thing remains the same- their instinct to be solitary hunters.  One article from the Online Encarta Encyclopedia says, "Felis catus…is popular as a household pet, and valuable for killing mice and rats" (Encarta). This instilled instinct to hunt is part of any cat, and especially the mountain lion.

Features that qualify the common house cat to be considered a deadly hunter include their keen hearing and sense of smell, their remarkable night vision, and their compact yet impressively muscular body. Their athletic ability can be attributed to the flexible and muscular body, and also to their tail, which helps them keep their balance while jumping or falling.

The cat’s claws are designed for catching and holding prey, and their 30 needle-like teeth for biting, not chewing. Cats have excellent night vision, peripheral vision, and binocular vision, but their vision by day is not as good as humans. In the daytime they see movement more than detail. Their hearing is very sensitive, and they can hear even ultrasonic frequencies. The ability to turn its ears without turning its head is an obvious advantage for the skilled hunters to sense things in a 360-degree area with minimal movement, which might spook their prey.

Their sense of smell aides in finding prey and also in the survival of the species itself by providing a way for mates to find each other during breading season, since in nature, they are solitary creatures. The toungue of a cat is like a rasp, and can easily lick flesh from the bones of its prey. A cat’s whiskers are like three-inch radar devices, which feel their way through the night, and provide environmental feedback. Also they posses an impressive memory and an ability to learn by observation and by experience. Cats have always been solitary animals and have never been extremely dependent on humans.

The domestic cat reaches maturity at nine to ten months of age. They may have litters of kittens after a gestation period of around 65 days. Average litter size is 4 kittens. The kittens are weaned about 6 weeks after birth. The original color of the descedents of the domestic cat was gray-brown with darker stripes, which provided camouflage in many different environments. This camaflauge is not needed now, and has been lost by genetic mutation and intentional breeding for specialized breeds.

The leading death among house cats is Feline Leukemia, a cancer of the blood cells resulting in fatal tumors and an impaired immune system which allows infection by other diseases (Faheen).

So we may see from observations made of the domestic cat that the creatures are designed to be deadly hunters.  In our study of the mountain lion, we may think back to the known and commonly observed behavior of the domestic cat. This comparison, however, must not be taken to the extreme.

We must keep in mind that they have been domesticated for 4,500 years where new behaviors and genetic variations may be introduced.  The environment of the domestic cat varies greatly from that of the mountain lion. Cats, although they may live otherwise, are fed by humans, and thus need not hunt to live (however, I have had personal experience with wild "domestic" cats that do hunt to live, and they are quite intimidating for their size).

Mountain lions, commonly known as cougar, puma, panther, live throughout the Rocky Mountains, from the arid desserts of Arizona to the frigid forests of Canada. There is also a species known as the Florida Panther that inhabits the Everglade wetlands.

The scientific name of the cougar is Felis concolor. The males grow from 110-180 pounds and the females from 80-130 pounds, considerably larger than their domestic counterpart. It may be observed from their large geographic habitat that they are creatures that can adapt to life in almost any climate. This fact alone gives them an advantage in their struggle to survive.

Cougars, like domestic cats, hunt at night, or when ever their prey is active, usually in the hours around dusk and dawn. The body of a cougar is much like that of a domestic cat, only much larger and they have a much thicker and longer tail used for balance during the pursuit of their prey. They have extraordinary vision and athletic ability. From a standing position they can jump a vertical distance of 15 feet and a horizontal distance of 40 feet.

A cougar's prey consists mainly of deer, but they may also eat smaller animals such as rabbits, wild hogs, birds, and other such creatures. Cougars depend on their habitat to ensure success in hunting. They can hide themselves and take advantage of many natural obstacles and thus stalk their prey.

Since most cougars are territorial they know the area well and use it to their advantage. The transient or traveling cougars have a more difficult time finding and catching food because they are not familiar with the terrain. These cougars are traveling in search of a home range of their own.

The cougar has a much longer reproduction cycle than does the cat. Fewer kittens survive to maturity due to the natural predators of all wild offspring. The main causes of death for cougars are humans, natural causes and road deaths. Cougar populations in recent years are on the rise due to bans on hunting and abundant supply of deer, elk, and other animals. This raises controversy.

On one hand, isn’t it good they are not going extinct? Yet, on the other hand, is it possible to co-exist with these giant predators? Only 13 deaths and 59 non-fatal attacks have been reported between 1890 and 1995.  During that same time 15,000 people were killed by lightening; 4,000 by bees; 10,000 by deer; and 1,300 by rattlesnakes (Predator Defense Institute). It is suggested, however, that while traveling through cougar territory one should travel with a companion or even with a dog.

Even though the numbers of human attacks is relatively small, the damage done by cougars to livestock is much greater.  One example of this is the Colorado sheep industry. The number of adult sheep killed by cougars between 1984 and 1993 averaged about 60 a year. This number rose in 1996 to 1,200 sheep (Rembert).  As a result, the number of hunting permits rose dramatically in Colorado and in some surrounding states.

Cougars are closer to you than you may think. With an increase in the expansion of housing projects into rural areas comes the natural invasion into the cougars’ homeland. The creatures are not seen as often as one might expect, but this is because they usually intentionally avoid humans.

Cougar sightings have taken place in almost all of the western states. I have heard of sightings several miles east of Rexburg, Idaho.  For example,  when a student, Marcy Jones, was traveling in the back of a pickup truck several miles east of Rexburg, she spotted a cougar running through an alfalfa field. I have also heard of several other sightings in the area.

Closer to my former home in Brigham City, Utah, there was an incident several years ago involving a cougar being struck and killed by a car traveling eastbound about three miles through Sardine Canyon. That is not a wilderness area where one might expect to find lions, but farmland and mountains between two cities that are only 20 miles apart.

The comparision between small cats and cougars helps us understand both species.  Most prominently, we note they are both lethal hunters, and they both use the common tactics of speed, ambush, and experience to find and capturte  prey.  Hopefully, our world will continue to find a place for both animals.
