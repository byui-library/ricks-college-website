Rulon Gardner inducted into 
Ricks Athletic Hall of Fame

REXBURG, Idaho — Gold-medal Olympic wrestler Rulon Gardner was inducted into the Ricks College Athletic Hall of Fame Jan. 25 during a visit to his alma mater.

While inducting Gardner into the Hall of Fame, Ricks College President David Bednar said the athlete holds the distinction of garnering the highest honor ever achieved by a former Ricks athlete – an Olympic gold medal. He added that Gardner was inducted into the Hall of Fame not only for his achievements, but the values that he holds.

While a student wrestler at Ricks from 1989-91, he became the national junior college heavyweight champion during his sophomore year.

Gardner made appearances at several college activities during his stay, including a wrestling match, student assembly, reception and Special Olympics opening ceremony. He also was given the symbolic key to the city of Rexburg and a gift from the Rexburg Chamber of Commerce.

Gardner said it was as a wrestler at Ricks that he revealed his dream of sometime winning the gold medal at the Olympics to his mother.

"This place is what helped me become what I am," he said of the college. "It’s good to know you have people and supporters who believe in you."

Gardner, the youngest of nine children in his family, was raised on a dairy farm in Star Valley, Wyo., where he wrestled as a freestyle heavyweight state champion for the local high school. He then came to Ricks College in 1989 to wrestle under coaches Bob Christensen and Fenton Broadhead.

He went on to the University of Nebraska where he earned a bachelor’s degree and also placed fourth nationally in the NCAA finals in his weight class.

Since 1995 after winning his first Greco-Roman national title, he has competed internationally with great success, qualifying for the U.S. Olympic National Team in his weight class and defeating three-time gold medalist Aleksandr Karelin of Russia for the gold in what many believe was the greatest upset in last year’s Olympic games.

In speaking to students Thursday afternoon, Gardner said, "I had a desire to do my best, and knew I did it even without the medal to prove. If we look past the worldly things (like the gold medal), then we’ll find out what really matters in life."

Throughout his talk, Gardner spoke about his struggles with working on the family farm, education and athletics, and credited them for his success.

Because of reading and learning disabilities, he was forced to work even harder and it took him 6½ years to get a four-year degree. Hearing people say he would never make it through college made him even more determined.

"Never give up, never stop trying, that’s what I live by," he said. "If I can touch someone by helping them understand what I have been through and experienced, then that’s what I want to do."

He also talked about the circumstances of his matches in getting to the 2000 Olympics, as well as the finals bout with Karelin.

"I knew I wanted this opportunity and I had four things going in," Gardner said. "I am an American. I work hard. I have a lot of heart. And I know the Lord is with me. Even if I didn’t win, I knew I would do my best. I’d make him earn it, because I was prepared."

He added, "Karelin wasn’t as prepared, and that’s why he lost. One mistake in his life cost him everything.  That one mistake [by Karelin] has changed my life immensely. No matter what we’re doing, be prepared for life."

He and his wife, Stacy, live in Colorado Springs, Colo., working with an eye toward the 2004 Olympic games.
