Ancient homes — they’re not your average apartment

by Sarah Lunt
LUN99013@BYUI.EDU
Scroll Staff

In the day of varied and complex architecture, as well as the common “box” suburbanite house, it is interesting to see how homes got to be the way they are.

In Norbert Shoenauer’s book, 6,000 Years of Housing, he begins from the beginning of known time and the start of communal civilization.

As is taught in most history classes, most early civilizations were nomadic, Shoenauer said.

That means that their dwellings were extremely temporary, ones that could be constructed very quickly from the natural materials on hand. Most often, these hunters and gatherers had to move with the herds of animals they hunted.

These dwellings would only be used for a few days. Thus, they were simple and small.

Usually, Shoenauer said, they were domed structures without a door — usually just a frontal opening.

African Bushmen and Pygmies, certain peoples in Southern India and the Australian Aborigines live the closest to this ancient standard, Shoenauer said.

As ancient people became more skilled in obtaining food, their dwellings became more seasonal. The igloos of Alaska and the teepees of the North American Indians are examples of these, Shoenauer said.

Many of these people often lived in communal dwellings; these buildings were often constructed of wood and were long so many families could live together.

There were cultures that would take their dwellings with them, such as tents and others with semi-formed structures, Shoenauer said.

As dwellings became even more permanent, people like the Hopi Indians (the pueblo types) built up city-type dwellings where they lived for longer periods of time. Although these were not lifetime dwellings, they lasted for much longer.

The most advanced farmers were able to settle down and live in their homes for a lifetime or even keep the dwellings within their families for generations, Shoenauer said.

It is also very interesting to learn about the types of dwellings that existed in ancient cities of religious and historical importance.

Shoenauer described first the type of dwelling that existed in Mesopotamia — namely Ur, the hometown of Abraham.

Those that lived in the city of Ur lived in homes that contained several rooms around a central court. A staircase near the entrance led to the roof or upper floor, Shoenauer said. These dwellings also had a reception room, a kitchen and other rooms for household functioning.

Babylon and Assyria, two other nations of biblical importance, also utilized the central courtyard, Shoenauer said. On the north side, there was a washroom, a kitchen and other household rooms. The family suite was placed on the south in order to protect them from the heat of the Mideast sun.

Another ancient civilization in Egypt had a different focus than other nations of its time. They devoted more time to building permanent shelters for the dead, rather than for the living. This was as a result of the great importance they placed on the afterlife and the fact that life was short, Shoenauer said.

In accordance with this, the homes were made of weaker materials, such as sun-dried bricks. Also, Shoenauer said, entrances never faced each other on the street, emphasizing the need for privacy.

Ancient China had several interesting home-building characteristics, Shoenauer said. They also had central courtyards, with several buildings surrounding it.

An interesting Chinese custom was that the main gate of their home and an area immediately after would be a “screen or spirit wall,” which, Shoenauer, prevented evil spirits from entering the home.

They believed that evil spirits only travelled in straight lines, so the screen would prevent them from entering. If they did, the people believed that even the smallest spirit would expand until it forced the occupants out.

Another interesting belief that reflected on Chinese homebuilding was that the main entrance could not be perfectly centered in the wall. This would have been an offense against their gods, because no human-made thing should or could be perfect, Shoenauer said.

At the time of Christ, Rome was in power in much of the known world. In Pompeii, a city destroyed around 70 A.D. by a volcanic eruption, most homes were made with sandstone and mud. They had shops in the front of their homes and a small garden in the rear, Shoenauer said.

As the Dark Ages began, many cultures gave up the traditional courtyard house, Shoenauer said. Many wanted more protected housing, and thus the trend toward the modern house began, with the boxier and stronger structure.
