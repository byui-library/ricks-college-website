BYU-Idaho instructors let students decide about religion and evolution

by Kelly Smurthwaite and Alex Shafer
Scroll Staff

Evolution has been the topic of controversy and debate among many groups, scholars and scientists for years.

At BYU-Idaho the teaching of the theories of evolution has to be approached carefully, as there is a fine line between teaching a theory of man and sustaining the scriptural account of the Creation found in the bible.

Although The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints has never released a statement specifically focusing on evolution, General Authorities and priesthood leaders have occasionally addressed the subject.

Tate Carter, a science instructor at BYU-I, approaches evolution by “provid[ing] some ‘what ifs’ for the students.” He challenges his students to move beyond the mind-set that evolution is evil.

There is evidence to support evolution, to the point that species adapt over time. There are even genetic mutations.

“Maybe evolution existed to a certain point short of man, or God is the power behind the process,” Carter said.

Lynn R. Firestone, a biology instructor at BYU-I, is going to teach a class on evolution next semester. She said she sees no need for the great conflict between evolution and the gospel.

Biology, she said, is a body of knowledge and is a way of thinking about the world based on things we can support or deprove through scientific experimentation. She also said that science cannot collect physical evidence for the existence of God, so it does not attempt to; it makes observations on things of the physical world.

“A theory is well-supported, but it is not accepted as absolute truth. It is subject to change when new evidence comes along,” Firestone said.

In her class, Firestone presents the gospel perspective as well as the theory of evolution and the evidence of evolution in all of its forms.

She said she will not resolve the conflict between religion and evolution for her students but instead gives them the tools necessary to resolve the issue in their own way.

“We will study evolution, and I will teach the body of knowledge that is there, but I will facilitate discussion and debate and always encourage the pursuit of spiritual truth as well,” she said.

Robert Marrott, a religion professor at BYU-I, has been a strong proponent of reminding those who teach evolution of the gospel perspective.

His challenge is aimed mainly toward those teaching organic evolution, which theorizes that all life came from a single cell.

In his opinion, professors should teach evolution so their students can understand its theory and function in the world, but not proclaim it as truth.

“The theory of evolution— and it is a theory—-will have an entirely different dimension when the workings of God in creation are fully revealed,” Elder Boyd K. Packer said in an address from the 1984 Ensign.

“Theories of science can no more overthrow the facts of religion than the facts of science.… One cannot build a faith upon the theory of evolution, for this theory is of no higher order than any other inference, and is therefore in a state of constant change,” Elder John A. Widtsoe said.

Elder George R. Hill, former member of the Quorum of the Seventy, expressed his feelings about evolution by saying the theory conflicts with the fundamental principles of science, addressed in a 1993 Ensign article.

“This theory conflicts with a basic law of chemistry, the second law of thermodynamics, which states in part that it is not possible for a spontaneous process to produce a system of higher order than the system processed at the beginning of the change,” he said.

In Church universities and institutions, science professors have had numerous challenges to teach evolution in the classroom in a manner that will not offend any Church doctrine, but that will also be educationally stimulating for students.

Evolution began as a theory of Charles Darwin. His early observations on how populations change have been the basis for an expanse in the field of biology and have lead to advances in genetics and medicine.

Yet at the same time, his theory has led to conflict and theories excluding God as the Creator.

Many scientists have come to accept evolution as fact, in part by the coercion of the scientific community to conform to the current mind-set.

Marrott said that people do not know exactly how the Earth was created; evolution is merely an explanation of life.

The Pearl of Great Price teaches that all things were first created spiritually. Joseph F. Smith taught, “Now when Adam was in the Garden of Eden, he was not subject to death. There was no blood in his body and he could have remained there forever. This is true of all the other creations.”

“If I were to teach evolution, I would teach it like my brother in Provo. I would teach the theory, but I would say that I did not believe it.” Marrott said.
