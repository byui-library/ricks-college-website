Former Ricks student returns after years of family life, politics

by Sarah Lunt
LUN99013@BYUI.EDU
Scroll Staff

Caryn Esplin was pregnant with her youngest daughter, Zoe, when she introduced the “Prevention of Minors’ Access to Tobacco” bill to the Idaho State Legislature. photo courtesy Caryn Esplin
A valedictorian of Highland High School in Pocatello, Idaho, Caryn Jones’ plans to attend BYU on scholarship changed when she went on a pioneer trek that changed her life.

On a whim, within two weeks of going to BYU, Caryn [now Caryn Esplin] surprised family and friends by choosing Ricks College instead.

As a freshman at Ricks College in 1978-1979, Esplin was a standout in many school activities. Besides being a top student, she also participated in student government, was the AWS freshman representative, the president of the honor program and the outside hitter on the 11th nationally ranked Ricks College volleyball team.

Esplin’s plans to finish at Ricks College and then go on to earn a law degree changed when she met and married her husband, Keith Esplin of Firth, Idaho, her freshman year at school.

Esplin was also the runner-up for Woman of the Year and graduated with her associate’s degree in Interpersonal Communications in April 1980. As a Spori Scholar, she spoke at graduation just a few days before delivering her first child.

With her marriage and subsequent children, she gave up her career plans, because “I realized what was important, and [my] priority was family,” she said.

A few years after her marriage, Esplin took the opportunity to invest in a new and growing business — tanning salons. She opened a salon and also sold tanning equipment. Her business eventually expanded regionally.

However, when President Benson exhorted mothers to stay at home, she sold the business.

Esplin also found ways to use her talents in the community. She has been vice chairperson on the school board for the Snake River School District and has also been a volunteer lobbyist for the Idaho PTA.

As a lobbyist, Esplin went to Boise as a child advocate. She wrote a bill called “Prevention of Minors’ Access to Tobacco,” which was adopted by the Idaho State Legislature.

Many warned Esplin that the bill would never be passed because of all the professional lobbyists the tobacco industry hired to fight her proposal.

However, Esplin’s oratory skills and pregnant state helped her to win her battle. Idaho went from having the weakest tobacco law in the nation to the strictest. Studies done by the state show that minors’ use of tobacco has dropped 50 percent since the bill passed. As a result of her success in getting the bill passed, leaders of the Idaho Legislature offered to support Esplin in running for a legislative seat.

Though this was a wonderful and prestigious compliment, Esplin passed up her chance to run for the office. She felt that opportunities would come later when children were reared, she said.

Now, 22 years after first attending Ricks College, Esplin, 41, commutes from Blackfoot, Idaho, to attend school at BYU-Idaho where she hopes to earn her a bachelor’s degree.

Esplin does not regret the delay or the opportunities she has passed up.

“The success story for a woman is her family. That’s the best career she can have,” she said.
