Interior design students offer furniture arranging tips

by Patricia Selman
SEL00001@BYUI.EDU
Scroll Staff

When it comes to furniture arrangements, college students do not have much of a choice.

They are fortunate if they move in and have a decent couch to sit on, let alone a coffee table, two arm chairs and a centerpiece to look at.

They can usually trust a roommate to provide an old, overstuffed bean bag chair that is leaking whatever it is stuffed with and only cost $3.50 at the Deseret Industries.

Even though college students are able to make do with anything, there are a few guidelines to go by when rearranging a comfortable living space for everyone to enjoy.

“College housing furniture is usually too large and bulky when it is compared to the space it is put in,” T.L. McRae, BYU-Idaho interior design department chair, said. “Because of this, college students will arrange the furniture so the traffic flow will travel through the conversation space.”

Before arranging furniture, a good thing to do is to think about the ways a front room is used in a college apartment. Not only is it a place where guests are entertained, but it is a space to watch television and where students do homework together.

When all of these things are brought into consideration, it is easy to determine that a “intimate conversation area” is needed, McRae said.

A good guideline when trying to achieve this is to have no longer than an 8-foot distance between seats and couches. Make sure the walkway does not go between the chairs and sofas.

Another step before actually arranging the room is to “find a dramatic element that draws [people’s] immediate attention,” according to furniture arrangement information at Lowe’s Home Improvement Warehouse online.

Some apartments have a fireplace or a picture window. But for those apartments that do not have a permanent visual element to center their furniture arrangement on, students can create one. A unique piece of art work, an unusual furnishing or something as off-the-wall as a lava lamp can be something to focus on.

Once the furniture is in place, a common goal for apartment decorators is to make the room look bigger than it actually is.

One way to add a new dimension to the room is to find better lighting than the common center-of-the-room bug lights, McRae said.

Students can also increase lighting by simply adding extra lamps around the living room, Larissa Bybee, a junior and interior design major from Pocatello, Idaho, said.

Inexpensive clip-on lamps can be bought at almost any local store. The lamps can be faced up onto the ceiling to become “up lights.” This opens up the room and adds a more intimate conversation space.

“The best thing you can do [to open up a room] is stick a mirror on the wall,” Bybee said.

Opening and closing blinds is also a way to make a room appear larger and brighter with all-natural lighting.

The last thing to think about when finishing with the arrangement of furniture is the inexpensive ways to make the furniture look better. Simple things such as sheets covering unattractive upholstery and throw pillows can make almost any room more pleasing to the eye.
