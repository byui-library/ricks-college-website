ARCHAEOASTRONOMY LESSENS GAP BETWEEN RELIGION AND ASTRONOMY

by John Walker
scroll staff

Ancient people who studied the stars may have studied more than they knew.

"The people of ancient Middle America were avid observers of the heavens, made intricate calendars and predicted astronomical phenomena," according to William J. Hamblin and Daniel C. Peterson , writers for Foundation for Ancient Research and Mormon Studies.

Reseachers have discovered that these symbols written and painted on the walls of ancient Middle American temples share similarities to the role of leadership within The Church of Jesus Chirst of Latter-day Saints today, according to the Jan. 31 FARMS article.

These ancient astronomers did not see these celestial patterns merely as mechanistic motions in the sky. Rather, they thought these phenomena revealed the will and the plan of their Maker, Hamblin and Peterson said.

Discoveries of temples, ceremonial centers and religious art all over the globe have lessened the gap between religion and astronomy, according to Hamblin and Peterson. One example includes the Anasazi Indian solstice markers at Fajada Butte in Chaco Canyon, N.M.

The main theme of ancient astronomy is that the order on earth should reflect the order of the heavens - their God's order. Ancient civilizations built their religious structures, ceremonial centers and even entire cities to tell the world that its king was in precise harmony with celestial order, according to the article.

This alignment could be very simple, such as building temples that faced each toward the rising sun. Some were more complex, such as in the case of a tomb in New Grange, Ireland, which was aligned so that the light of the sun would enter the inner burial chamber on the winter solstice.

Ancient Americans believed that the heavens were conceived as a hierarchy of spheres, consisting of higher zones closer to God; therefore, more important and holy, the article said.

Society was patterned to show this same celestial order, with the king's chamber at the center of the City and a hierarchy of priests and ministers surrounding him.

The motifs of ancient sites often depicted powerful priests with colorful robes. Priests with less power wore uncolored robes, Hamblin and Peterson said.

Celestial symbols were often included on ancient Egyptian temples ceilings, creating maps of the heavens for the ascent of the pharaohs into the realm of the gods in the afterlife.

Archaeoastronomy, with its roots in the Stonehenge discoveries of the 1960s, it has pressed forward into active fields that are providing new perspectives for the history of human interaction with the cosmos, according to Center for Archaeoastronomy.

"Archaeoastronomical fields have made technological leaps due to advances in new computer programs that can create precise maps of the stars as observed from any point on the earth at any date in history," Hamblin and Peterson said.

Researchers have concluded that technologies improve our understanding of religion and science, Hamblin and Peterson said.

"Since virtually all ancient astronomy was intimately linked with religion-and sometimes practically indistinguishable from it-these new discoveries have illuminated understanding of the intimate relationship between astronomy and religion," Hamblin and Peterson said.
