Student wards are unique from home

by Katie Mcmurray
mcm00003@byui.edu
Scroll Staff

Screaming babies, soft mumblings and rustling papers all make up the typical sacrament meeting in a home ward. There are distractions all around.

This is not usually the case at BYU-Idaho. The university wards are different than many home wards.

Sacrament meeting, Relief Society, callings and teaching lessons are all aspects of Church life that are different between the two atmospheres.

“I like my home ward better because I feel more strongly involved in church service. But I like student wards because I feel more socially involved with the entire ward,” Nick Disney, a sophomore from Carlsbad, Calif., said.

Sacrament meeting is one of the many differences. People leave their home wards and become members of student wards. During sacrament meeting, at a student ward, the drop of a pin could be heard by everyone in attendance.

“I like Sacrament [meeting] better in student wards because it is less noisy,” Neil Aboshamaa, a sophomore from Columbus, Ohio, said.

During the sacrament service, returned missionaries replace deacons in the passing and blessing of the sacrament. There are no screaming babies or wandering children distracting students from the Spirit.

Another difference is in the callings students receive. They are no longer underneath authority. Relief Society presidents, elders quorum presidents and Sunday School presidents are all callings given to BYU-I students. The students run the entire ward under the bishopric.

In relief society, BYU-I women are not disturbed by crawling babies. Peers teach lessons which are geared towards young adults rather than the life of a frantic mother.

“I love my Relief Society back home because of its traditional structure, but I also like the experience I can get here. Being around women my own age doesn’t make it hard to go to Relief Society,” Britnee Walker, a sophomore from Farmington, Utah, said.

“The lessons feel more on your level,” Emily Andersen, a freshman from Salt Lake City, said.

The teachers for every class are about the same age as the rest of the class. Lessons are taught in a somewhat different manner, rather than by the typical 40-year-old Gospel Doctrine teacher.

Sometimes, 18-year-old women are in charge of teaching a class full of students, including returned missionaries, which could prove to be somewhat intimidating.

“I like my home ward because I think the teachers have more experience. Here, everybody is just learning how to do everything,” Dora Requena, a freshman from West Jordan, Utah, said.

While growing up, students have watched as their mothers and fathers leave the house to do their teaching, and now students are all responsible for home teaching and visiting teaching.

Students are forced to create new families as Home Evening groups, made up of a few student apartments. There are no more parents to enforce activity.

Another benefit for BYU-I students is that religion classes are taken in place of an institute course.

Although there are many changes and differences between the home and school atmospheres, the same gospel is taught, the same format is used and the same Spirit is present.
