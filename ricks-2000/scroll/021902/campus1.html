Psychology students learn to train chicks

by Carl Neil Ekman III
EKM98001@BYUI.EDU
Scroll Staff

Freshman Kelly Kipphunt, from Pittsford, N.Y., and freshman Beth Borgia, from Mesa, Ariz., perform an experiment on a small chick for their psychology class. Mike Johnson/ Scroll
Studying the contents of a cardboard box, three BYU-Idaho students watch a baby chick peeping and pecking. The students will be training the chick to peck a red dot with only their ingenuity, a few instructions and a lot of chicken feed.

Sitting in the hallway of the John L. Clarke Building, freshmen Julie Bowen of Shelby, Mich., Kelly Kipphut of Pittsford, N.Y., and Beth Borgia of Mesa, Ariz., chose tasks for the care of the chick. Borgia would feed, Kipphut would attract the bird by tapping the box and Pittsford would supervise.

The chick followed the sound of Kipphut’s tapping, and by doing so, neared the dot and triggered a feeding by Borgia.

After about half an hour, the girls taught the chick to go to that corner.

By offering the chick rewards for certain behavior, the three students of Professor Kim Anderson’s Psychology 111 class will be realistically applying concepts of classical conditioning.

Then came the task of teaching the chick to peck on the red dot.

“Don’t scratch. Peck,” Bowen said, because the chick only received food when it touched the red dot.
“You could say, ‘It’s right there,’ but [the chick] doesn’t know,” Borgia said.

The chick, realizing he did not receive food from standing in the corner, wandered the box. With a tap, Bowen pushed him back to his corner with hopes that he would peck the dot.

Having not been fed in a day and a half, the chicks become frustrated, Anderson said.

Soon the class period was over, but the chick had undertaken an experiment in classical conditioning that helped the girls get hands-on experience of what they study in class, Bowen said.

Classical conditioning is the repeating of a reward or punishment treatment for certain behavior. Here, the reward enforces the good behavior so that the behavior is conditioned; in other words, the behavior is repeated to get the reward.

In the case of the chick, tapping is the conditioned response stimulated by receiving of food.

“This is the best training you’ll ever have for parenting, ever,” Anderson told the frustrated girls.

“Be patient and all good things come in good time,” Borgia said.
