Rescuing a Cougar
by Adam Hess
 
The following article tells about a first-hand experience with a cougar.  This narrative was told to Adam by his  father, a medical doctor in Utah.   The events take place near Spanish Fork Canyon, Utah.

Dad said, "I found out one day what it means to look into the eyes of an angry mountain lion. A few years ago, I was riding with a friend up in the Spanish Fork Canyon.  We had the intent of riding all day on the horses, and had his hound dogs along. Many times he had tracked mountain lions in the area.

"Each time he would obtain wonderful photos, then leave the animals unharmed. We had been riding since before dawn, and been following a good set of tracks for several hours. Eventually, the dogs were hot on the trail, and then started to howl, as though they had a lion in sight. We rounded a curve in the trail and dropped into a small bowl on the south side of the mountain. The cats always like to lounge on the sunny side, in the rocks and cliffs.

"Suddenly my friend was full of anger, rather than the excitement of the anticipation of treeing a cougar. In front of us was a big male cougar, stretched out in the bottom of the bowl, his front paw in one steel trap and his back paw in another. The dogs were called and obediently came to our side; they knew from past experience that we intended the cat no harm.

"My friend explained in angry words that one particular rancher often set steel traps, claiming to be after the bobcats, but setting larger traps than necessary knowing he was likely to catch a cougar which, incidentally, was illegal.  We discussed the sad fact that the beautiful animal would surely die if left in the trap and we wondered if we should shoot it and take it to a ranger, then he could discuss the matter with the rancher.

"I, being new to the adventure and extremely naïve, said, ‘Why don’t we just go over and let him loose from the traps?’

"My wiser and older friend grinned and said, ‘Forget it, silly idea,’ and a few other things I don't want to repeat. We moved a little closer as the big cat glared and snarled at us.  I suggested that his front paw was barely trapped, and his rear foot wasn’t hurt badly either.  Maybe a fracture at the ankle, but no open injury.

"My friend had a good time asking me how many times I had used my medical background in diagnosing a wild animal caught in a steel trap! We talked at length, and decided that the cat, if released, would be able to survive in the wild.  Eventually, I pronounced I wanted to try to release him. We made a plan to roll a large log over the cougar and hold him down while I spread the jaws of the traps.

"My friend held one end of the log and had his Colt .45 drawn and cocked in the other hand. I slowly wiggled the log over the cat’s body over shoulders, starring the whole time into his angry yellow eyes. He seemed to soften a little bit as he may have realized that if we wanted to hurt him, we would have done so by now. Nonetheless, he was not happy, and it showed in his glares and snarls.

"Keeping constant eye contact, I reached out and loosened the traps as he jerked his paw loose. On the count of three, my friend and I let go of the log and jumped back. The big cougar sat in place for a few seconds, looked around, then snarled one last time before he leapt off through the rocks. He appeared to be entirely unharmed.

"Since that warm winter day I have often wondered what ever happened to the cougar.   I’ve also wondered, ‘What in the world did I think I was doing?’ The memory of that cougar starring into my eyes will always be vivid."
