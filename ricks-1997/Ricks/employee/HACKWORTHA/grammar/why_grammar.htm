Developing a skill includes two parts: (a) what one KNOWS and (b) what one CAN DO. Every skill has a knowledge base associated with it. Consider music as an analogy. Although a student filled with raw talent might sing and compose clever songs, ideally we want the young musician to KNOW and NAME what he or she is doing.
Ideally, we want the student to understand some music theory, a tool for analysis for his and for other's creations.  James Baldwin said the root function of language is to control the universe by describing it. The process of naming things began with Adam and continues to this moment.  
Once we can name a process, we gain intellectual control of that process. As we study anatomy and learn numerous new words, we gain intellectual control over a small part of our universe.  We need to learn the vocabulary associated with any particular skill in order to gain intellectual control of that skill. Therefore, we learn music theory, terms for literary analysis, and English grammar, etc.
The learned conventions of formal writing can be applied to one's own text as well as to the texts of others.  For example, principles of capitalization, punctuation, subordination, parallelism, misplaced modification, and organization can be learned and applied during revision and pre-writing.
Although writers are sometimes inspired, and although certain writing techniques can help writers tap unconscious, creative powers, part of the writing process is INTENTIONAL. Consequently, during revision, the more one knows and the more one can NAME what is happening in one's own text, the more one gains control over his or her own writing.
Learning terms and agreeing upon meaning also give the teacher and student a common vocabulary, thus making communication possible. What if you were a car mechanic, specializing in carburetors, and when you talked to other mechanics in the shop, you called everything a "what-ch-ma-call-it" or a "thing-a-mabob"?  What if you called every tool a "deely"?  Quickly, you would feel frustration as you tried to communicate with others.  Also, the people listening to you would feel frustration.  In fact, after a while, your listeners would try to teach you to talk more precisely.  They would say, "No, Frank.  This is not a deely.  This is a 5/16 inch socket wrench."
In all fields of study, as we gain competence in that area, we must learn the vocabulary associated with that field of study.
