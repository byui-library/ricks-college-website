PCOS often undetected though harmful

by Melissa Merrill
MER00006@BYUI.EDU
Religion/People editor

Like many young women, Amanda Davies had irregular periods all throughout her teen years.

And, like many young women, she did not think anything of it. In fact, she was relieved.

Until January, when she was 21 years old and married, Davies, a junior from Rancho Cucamonga, Calif., had no idea that she had Polyscistic Ovarian Syndrome.

PCOS, also known as Stein-Leventhal Syndrome or Polycystic Ovary Disease, affects an estimated 6 to 10 percent of all women, most of whom do not know they have it, according to www.pcosupport.org. It was first recognized in 1935, according to www.womens-health.co/uk/pcos.htm.

PCOS can affect girls as young as 14 or 15 or sometimes even younger, Davies said.

“I didn’t think that missing a period was a big deal,” Davies said. “I was glad that I missed it. But I wish I would have gone to the doctor. That could have taken care of and prevented some other problems.”

Symptoms include absent or irregular menstruation, numerous cysts on the ovaries, high blood pressure, acne, elevated insulin levels, insulin resistance, diabetes, infertility, excess hair on the face and body, thinning of hair on the head and obesity.

“I’ve gained close to 50 pounds, and I haven’t changed my eating habits at all,” Davies said. “That’s really frustrating. A lot of girls struggle with weight and I found out why I did.”

While there is no cure for PCOS, it is treatable with medication as well as diet and exercise changes, according to www.pcosupport.org. In addition to taking a drug called Glucophage, Davies eats healthily and exercises regularly.

“When I eat something, my pancreas secretes a double amount of insulin,” Davies said. “Glucophage counteracts that and acts as if it’s producing a normal amount.”

Additionally, she takes Provera when she needs to have a period. She takes it approximately every two months to train her body to ovulate on its own.

“I used to go six months without having a period. If you don’t have a period within three months, you run a high risk of having uterine cancer,” Davies said.

“It’s affected me so much because I’m faced with the fact that maybe I can’t have children,” Davies said. “I’m trying to do what I can so that I can prevent later problems.”

Davies is also a member of several online support groups for PCOS.

“I’m just a normal college student,” Davies said. “This could happen to anyone. If you have irregular periods, go get it checked out.”
