How to write a lede and a top:

Here are a list of facts -- in no particular order.

· Brian Choi is a Korean grocer in a black neighborhood, West End, near the Olympic track and field stadium in Atlanta.

· In January, the United States Labor Department fined him $1,500 and asked him to provide $5,000 in back wages for employing underage workers and not paying minimum wage.

· "I am not a hero," Choi says. He paid the fine in March.

· In the early 1990s, he and other merchants were boycotted for allegedly overcharging blacks and not giving enough back to the community.

· In 1992, a robber shot him at close range. He spent several days in the hospital and still carries a bullet in his chest.

· The Labor Department has no comment on the case because of a policy that forbids it to talk about the specifics of individual cases. A department spokesman said, "when a complaint involves child labor, it goes to the very top of the list."

· A Freedom of Information Act request revealed the Labor Department never talked with the boys involved in the case.

· About three years ago, Choi allowed Willie Matthews, then 11, and Maurice Matthews, then 12, to hang out at the store and to deliver groceries to cars for customers to get a tip. Later, the boys were sent on errands. On some days, the boys would make $15 each.

· At first, Choi discouraged the boys from working, then he changed his mind because the pair seemed to be in need and were eager to please. "I thought my store could be a kind of sanctuary for them," he said.

· With their parents approval, the boys came regularly. Choi bought them clothes and, occasionally, gave them cash.

· Choi says the experience was helping him open up to the black community.

· Choi pays most of his workers only slightly above minimum wage and gives none of them benefits, including two of his managers. He has never visited the home of a black person nor read any books about the African-American experience nor visited a black church or group.

· His store is a supermarket called Hardy's Supermarket. He keeps a gun in the office and an armed guard, wearing a bullet-proof vest, patrols the aisles armed with a pistol, mace, a night stick and a knife. It is open, by necessity, 18 hours a day.

· Choi finds it difficult to befriend most blacks and adds, "I can't deny that there's been resentment about racial differences."

· After paying the fines, he let the boys go and has lost track of them.

· Choi says, "I've really never changed my mind about helping other youngsters, but I don't know any other way."

· Dan Yager, an attorney for the Labor Policy Association, an employer group in Washington, says, "This case shows a lack of recognition of humanity by either the law or the people enforcing it."

· "These informal arrangements are widespread and something the children and their parents have insisted on," says Aminifu Woodrow Smith, community director of the Shrine of the Black Madonna, a church near Mr. Choi's store. She thinks the Labor Department should work out special arrangements allowing small enterprises like Choi's to keep children off the street.

· No one knows who filed the complaint against Mr. Choi, something the Labor Department doesn't divulge to encourage whistle blowers. Employees told Choi that someone, perhaps a relative of the boys, came by the store once, when Choi was away, and demanded that the boys give their tip money to her. They refused, and she began to make accusations the boys were there illegally.

· In December, following the complaint, a Labor Department investigator came and demanded to see employee records. He complied. Later, he told investigators he was trying to help the boys. Records from the Labor Department say the admission was used against him. "He admitted to the involvement of Maurice and Willie in his store." The good intentions, the government said, were not a defense.

· The boys told Mr. Choi he never abused them.

· The boys and their parents refused to be interviewed for this story.

· A cashier said, "Those boys, they loved Brian."

· Choi appealed and had his child-labor fine cut from an original fine of $2,875.

· Following an early finding that he had violated child labor laws, Choi went to a higher official at Labor, who didn't think he had a good argument and it was this higher official that set the original fine.

 

 

The standard news story lede.

 

Step one:

 

Ask yourself, what is the news here?

 

 

 

Think about it, and write a short, summary answer.

 

 

 

 

 

An example lede:

 

ATLANTA - The Department of Labor has fined a Korean grocer $6,500 for a violation of child labor and minimum wage laws because of his employment of two underage boys.

 

Now that you have reviewed. Answer the main journalistic questions:

 

Who?

The Labor Department, a Korean grocer

 

What?

A fine for labor violations

 

When?

We have not answered that . . . Better get to that in the next paragraph.

 

Where?

This is answered by the Dateline, but more detail on this is needed soon.

 

Why?

For violation of child labor laws, but we can expand on this.

 

How?

This will be answered soon, too.

 

Let's go to the second paragraph now.

 

In March, following a four-month investigation, Labor Department officials fined Brian Choi, owner of Hardy's Supermarket in West End near Olympic Stadium, $5,000 in back wages and $1,500 for violations of child labor laws. The case involved the hiring of Willie and Maurice Mathews, whom he allowed to collect tips from customers and paid them for running small errands.

 

This paragraph answers the questions in more detail . . . Including the how, when and where questions. Now, we need to expand on the why. I instinctively might give both sides here.

 

Choi, for his part, says he was simply trying to make a difference in the lives of two young boys who begged him for work in this impoverished section of Atlanta. He says the boys' parents were supportive of the arrangement.

 

The Labor Department refused to comment on the specifics of the case, citing department policy, but a department spokesman said, "when a complaint involves child labor, it goes to the very top of the list."

 

Now we have the major questions answered in basic detail. Now it is time for the cosmic relevance or the nut paragraph(s) that answer the question "why does this matter" or "why should I care?"

 

This case has raised questions among employee groups about the role of child-labor laws in poor communities. In West End, as in many other American neighborhoods, tensions have run high between Korean-American merchants and African-American patrons and many families struggle to make ends meet. Such relationships between children and merchants are a common way to build bridges and help families pay their bills.

 

"This informal work arrangements are widespread," says Aminifu Woodrow Smith, community director of the Shrine of the Black Madonna, a church near Choi's store, "and are something the children and their parents have insisted on."

 

Now we have what I call the "Top" of the story. It is the first four to six paragraphs of the story that introduce the topic and give a good outline for those readers who choose not to read on. The last paragraph or so of the top is the cosmic relevance, sometimes called the nut paragraph, telling readers why they should care: For the next section, find underlines with numbers in parentheses.

 

ATLANTA - The Department of Labor has fined a Korean grocer $6,500 for a violation of child labor and minimum wage laws because of his employment of two underage boys.

 

In March, following a four-month investigation, Labor Department officials fined Brian Choi, owner of Hardy's Supermarket in West End near Olympic Stadium,(1) $5,000 in back wages and $1,500 for violations of child labor laws. The case involved the hiring of Willie and Maurice Mathews, whom he allowed to collect tips from customers and paid them for running small errands.(2)

 

Choi, for his part, says he was simply trying to make a difference in the lives of two young boys who begged him for work in this impoverished section of Atlanta. He says the boys' parents were supportive of the arrangement.(3)

 

The Labor Department refused to comment on the specifics of the case, citing department policy, but a department spokesman said, "when a complaint involves child labor, it goes to the very top of the list."(4)

 

This case has raised questions among employee groups about the role of child-labor laws in poor communities. (5) In West End, as in many other American neighborhoods, tensions have run high between Korean-American merchants and African-American patrons and many families struggle to make ends meet. Such relationships between children and merchants are a common way to build bridges and help families pay their bills.

 

"This informal work arrangements are widespread," says Aminifu Woodrow Smith, community director of the Shrine of the Black Madonna, a church near Choi's store, "and are something the children and their parents have insisted on."

 

A good approach to basic new structure at this point is to take blocks of text relating to points in the top of the story in the order such issues were raised in the top of the story. The points worth expanding on are up to you. Of course, overlap can occur among the different points as you explain them. Here's point one as I saw it, Brian Choi, the owner of the supermarket.

What I would do here is describe a little bit more about Mr. Choi and his shop to understand the situation better. Here is an attempt:

The environment about Hardy's Supermarket has been tense for many years. In the early 1990s, he and other merchants were boycotted for allegedly overcharging blacks and not giving enough back to the community. Then, in 1992, a robber shot him at close range. He spent several days in the hospital and still carries a bullet in his chest.

 

Today, the market is open 18 hours a day. Choi keeps a gun in his office and, after 9 p.m., an armed guard patrols the aisles wearing a bullet-proof vest, carrying mace, a knife, a pistol and a nightstick.

 

(Let me break in here for a brief aside. This previous paragraph is excellent reporting because of the detail and a great example of showing, not telling, of following the writing principle of writing in a detailed enough way so readers could draw something of a picture . . . If you merely told, you would say, "The market is open for many hours each day and is dangerous at night." You show here by giving the detail of armed guards patrolling a supermarket. Your readers learn it's dangerous through this excellent detail.)

 

Choi employs 20 blacks, but pays them just above minimum wage. He provides no benefits. He says he has never been in the home of a black person nor read anything on the African-American experience. He finds it difficult to befriend most blacks and adds, "I can't deny that there's been resentment about racial differences."

 

Now, let's go on to the second point, that is that case itself.

 

Still, about three years ago, Choi decided to let two young boys, Willie, then 11, and Maurice Matthews, then 12, work at his store after the pair would hang out there and asked for work.

 

At first, Choi discouraged the boys from working, then he changed his mind because the pair seemed to be in need and were eager to please. "I thought my store could be a kind of sanctuary for them," he said. Choi let the pair deliver groceries to cars for customers for a tip. Later, the boys were sent on errands. On some days, the boys would make $15 each.

 

With their parents approval, the boys came regularly. Choi bought them clothes and, occasionally, gave them cash, Choi said. (The parents declined to be interviewed for this story, nor would they allow the boys to be interviewed.)

 

Choi says the experience was helping to open him to the black community.

 

Let's continue the second point by expanding about the investigation. Notice how the story flows naturally in a chronological way, from beginning to end.

 

In December, following a complaint, a Labor Department investigator came and demanded to see employee records. He complied. In a subsequent visit, he told investigators he was trying to help the boys.

 

Records from the Labor Department say this admission was used against him, "He admitted to the involvement of Maurice and Willie in his store," the records say. The good intentions, the government said, were not a defense. According to the records, obtained through a Freedom of Information Act, government investigators never tried to question the boys.

 

In January, the Department decided to fine Choi and told him so. Choi decided to appeal to a higher official at the Labor Department. However, the official didn't accept his arguments and, about two weeks later, sent him a notice to pay $5,386 in back wages, but told him they would forego any fines. Then, in May, the department changed its mind and hit him with a fine of $2,875. Choi appealed, but the fine was cut to only $1,500.

 

The Labor Department won't say who filed the complaint against Choi, but he thinks it might be a relative of the boys who came by demanding the children give her their money and, later, began complaining the boys were working illegally. After the complaint, he said he repeatedly asked the boys if he had abused them. The boys and other employees assured him he had not.

 

Points 3 and 4 would be expanded next. I have not really proved too much on this in the fact list, so we'll make it relatively short.

 

"I'm not a hero," Choi says. He was simply, he and friends say, trying, for once, to do the right thing by the neighborhood. He says, further, he did not know anything about child labor laws nor did he willfully disobey any law.

 

The Labor Department and its investigators, despite repeated requests for interviews, would not discuss the specific case because of a policy that forbids comment on individual violations. However, a spokesman said the law specifically forbids children under 14 from working in commercial enterprises and it doesn't matter whether children or their parents supported or benefitted from any arrangement, as Choi claims happened in this case.

 

Now, we move to point 5. The fallout:

 

Many believe the Department of Labor may have gone too far.

 

Dan Yager, an attorney for the Labor Policy Association, an employer group in Washington, says, "This case shows a lack of recognition of humanity by either the law or the people enforcing it."

 

Aminifu Woodrow Smith, the community director at the church near Choi's store, thinks the Labor Department should work out special arrangements allowing small enterprises like Choi's to keep children off the street.

 

We have covered all the points we wanted to in the top. Of course, we could add further commentary through additional interviews or longer quotes, but you get the general idea. What remains is how to end it.

 

You could simply drop it here. This is acceptable, particularly in the inverted pyramid approach, but the writing isn't quite as nice. You could also go on to some additional facts, such as the history of child labor law or other similar cases. This is a way of diminishing returns and not as clean writing. If your are going to add additional blocks of details, often it is best to reference them in the top of the story.

 

An important approach for an ending is to consider the impact. Some like to return to a main theme established in the beginning. Others try to use a "twanger" that shows the emotional impact or importance of the story. Here is a suggestion:

 

After the decision, the boys returned to the store several times, but Choi sent them home. Ultimately, he lost track of them. "I've really never changed my mind about helping other youngsters, but I don't know any other way," he said.

 

The Feature news story lede.

 

Step one:

 

What about this story makes it unusual?

 

After an answer, write a lede that starts with this fact. Such a lede should always have an implied question that impels a reader forward. Again, a suggestion:

 

ATLANTA - Brian Choi, A Korean grocer in a mostly black neighborhood near Olympic Stadium, was trying to be a good neighbor. He paid two struggling neighborhood boys to run errands. He let them help customers take their groceries to their cars for a small tip.

 

For his efforts, he got a $6,500 fine from the Department of Labor.

 

This lede uses irony and grabs attention. The implied question her is: "Why was he fined for being a good neighbor?" You must immediately answer that question or you will frustrate the reader. Here is one method.

 

The reason? The boys were underage and his employment violated child-labor and minimum-wage laws, the Department of Labor says.

 

Now that the scene is set and the implied question answered, we must tell people why they are reading the story. You must give them the cosmic relevance . . . Tell them the news. I've added a quote here to continue the relevance idea a little further.

 

For many advocates, the case is an example of how child-labor laws might not work in every situation, and, indeed, may cause harm.

 

"These informal work arrangements are widespread,"says Aminifu Woodrow Smith, community director of the Shrine of the Black Madonna, a church near Choi's store, "and are something the children and their parents have insisted on."

 

Now that you have given people the cosmic relevance and captured their interest, tell the story. Use the narrative approach in much the same way you did before. Do it chronologically and answer most of the questions in a logical sequence.

 

The environment about Choi's store, Hardy's Supermarket in West End, has been tense for many years. It is typical of the tension in many inner-city neighborhoods between Korean merchants and nearby residents.

 

In the early 1990s, he and other merchants were boycotted for allegedly overcharging blacks and not giving enough back to the community. Then, in 1992, a robber shot him at close range. He spent several days in the hospital and still carries a bullet in his chest.

 

Today, the market is open 18 hours a day. Choi keeps a gun in his office and, after 9 p.m., an armed guard patrols the aisles wearing a bullet-proof vest, carrying mace, a knife, a pistol and a nightstick.

You might call this section the response from both sides:

 

"I'm not a hero," Choi says. He was simply, he and friends say, trying, for once, to do the right thing by the neighborhood. He says, further, he did not know anything about child labor laws nor did he willfully disobey any law.

Let's use the same ending as before, but alter it slightly, using a device that returns us to the beginning, the beginning being the thought of being a good neighbor.

 

After the decision, the boys returned to the store several times, but Choi sent them home. Ultimately, he lost track of them. Yet, despite it all, Choi wants to remain a good neighbor. "I've really never changed my mind about helping other youngsters, but I don't know any other way," he said.

 

The Personal News Story lede.

 

Step one:

 

What about this story makes it interesting from a human standpoint?

 

This works much the same as what I am calling the feature lede, but focuses, instead, on the person. Here's one approach:

 

 

ATLANTA - Brian Choi, a Korean grocer in West End, understood what it can take to run a business in the inner city.

 

His market in West End was boycotted in the early 1990s for allegedly overcharging blacks and not giving enough back to the community. Then, in 1992, a robber shot him at close range. He spent several days in the hospital and still carries a bullet in his chest.

 

The market is open 18 hours a day. Choi keeps a gun in his office and, after 9 p.m., an armed guard patrols the aisles wearing a bullet-proof vest, carrying mace, a knife, a pistol and a nightstick.

 

Still, none of that prepared him for $6,500 in fines from the Department of Labor for violation of child labor laws he never understood.

 

Notice another implied question: "What question didn't he understand?"

 

For about two years, Choi allowed two underage boys to deliver groceries to cars for tips and, also, gave the boys money for running errands.

 

The Labor Department decided it was violation of child labor and minimum wage laws.

 

I might put the reactions next, followed by the cosmic relevance:

 

Choi, for his part, says he was simply trying to make a difference in the lives of two young boys who begged him for work in this impoverished section of Atlanta. He says the boys' parents were supportive of the arrangement.

 

The Labor Department refused to comment on the specifics of the case, citing department policy, but a department spokesman said, "when a complaint involves child labor, it goes to the very top of the list."

 

For many advocates, the case is an example of how child-labor laws might not work in every situation, and, indeed, may cause harm.

 

"These informal work arrangements are widespread,"says Aminifu Woodrow Smith, community director of the Shrine of the Black Madonna, a church near Choi's store, "and are something the children and their parents have insisted on."

 

We won't bog you down with further details on the story, save to say, again, at this point we tell the story in more detail, chronologically, much as we did before. Let's move on to our last lede, the dramatic lede.

 

The Dramatic Lede:

 

Step One:

The first step is to ask what about this story makes this dramatic or what is the story's most dramatic moment. The lede becomes one that focuses on action, peril, drama or conflict, not so much on information. It is usually set in a scene or a situation. Here is an attempt here:

 

When an investigator from the Department of Labor asked grocer Brian Choi, a Korean, why he was employing two underage boys, he responded without hesitation and with complete honesty. He was simply trying to help these poverty stricken young men have a refuge from their tough surroundings.

 

He might have answered less candidly because the investigator had the admission of guilt he had been looking for. The department fined Choi $6,500 for his violation.

 

This lede might have been better with more detail about the surroundings or scene setting with more of the interaction. Still, you get the idea that the focus here is on the climactic event and this is where we begin. The character, the investigator is in peril from the government. Hence, the conflict. Note how we have still captured the essential irony of the situation of helping and being fined for it, not to mention we have a similar implied question of "why fine a man for helping children?"

 

From here, we answer a little more about the case and its importance. We first answer the implied question.

 

The simple issue to the Labor officials was Choi had been in violation of child labor laws, hence, needed a substantial fine. This issue, however, underscores to some analysts how a law can be misapplied in a setting that cried out for a good samaritan.

 

We next tell the story in more detail, to set the scene:

 

Choi's grocery sits in West End, one of Atlanta's more dangerous neighborhoods. Each night, a bullet-proof-vest-wearing security guard patrols the aisles of the store carrying a gun, some mace, a knife and a nightstick.

 

Complaints frequent the neighborhood. Choi and other merchants have faced boycotts for their allegedly high prices and for not giving enough back to the community. In 1992, he was shot by a robber. "I can't deny that there's been resentment about racial differences," he says.

 

Still, when Maurice and Willie Martin began showing up at his store asking for things to do, he gave in to their pleas. He gave them a job, letting them help customers go to cars with groceries for a tip. "I thought my store could be a kind of sanctuary for them," he says.

We have covered all the points we wanted to in the top. Of course, we could add further commentary through additional interviews or longer quotes, but you get the general idea. What remains is how to end it.

 

You could simply drop it here. This is acceptable, particularly in the inverted pyramid approach, but the writing isn't quite as nice. You could also go on to some additional facts, such as the history of child labor law or other similar cases. This is a way of diminishing returns and not as clean writing. If your are going to add additional blocks of details, often it is best to reference them in the top of the story.

 

An important approach for an ending is to consider the impact. Some like to return to a main theme established in the beginning. Others try to use a "twanger" that shows the emotional impact or importance of the story. Here is a suggestion:

 

After the decision, the boys returned to the store several times, but Choi sent them home. Ultimately, he lost track of them. "I've really never changed my mind about helping other youngsters, but I don't know any other way," he said.

 

The Feature news story lede.

 

Step one:

 

What about this story makes it unusual?

 

After an answer, write a lede that starts with this fact. Such a lede should always have an implied question that impels a reader forward. Again, a suggestion:

 

ATLANTA - Brian Choi, A Korean grocer in a mostly black neighborhood near Olympic Stadium, was trying to be a good neighbor. He paid two struggling neighborhood boys to run errands. He let them help customers take their groceries to their cars for a small tip.

 

For his efforts, he got a $6,500 fine from the Department of Labor.

 

This lede uses irony and grabs attention. The implied question her is: "Why was he fined for being a good neighbor?" You must immediately answer that question or you will frustrate the reader. Here is one method.

 

The reason? The boys were underage and his employment violated child-labor and minimum-wage laws, the Department of Labor says.

 

Now that the scene is set and the implied question answered, we must tell people why they are reading the story. You must give them the cosmic relevance . . . Tell them the news. I've added a quote here to continue the relevance idea a little further.

 

For many advocates, the case is an example of how child-labor laws might not work in every situation, and, indeed, may cause harm.

 

"These informal work arrangements are widespread,"says Aminifu Woodrow Smith, community director of the Shrine of the Black Madonna, a church near Choi's store, "and are something the children and their parents have insisted on."

 

Now that you have given people the cosmic relevance and captured their interest, tell the story. Use the narrative approach in much the same way you did before. Do it chronologically and answer most of the questions in a logical sequence.

 

The environment about Choi's store, Hardy's Supermarket in West End, has been tense for many years. It is typical of the tension in many inner-city neighborhoods between Korean merchants and nearby residents.

 

In the early 1990s, he and other merchants were boycotted for allegedly overcharging blacks and not giving enough back to the community. Then, in 1992, a robber shot him at close range. He spent several days in the hospital and still carries a bullet in his chest.

 

Today, the market is open 18 hours a day. Choi keeps a gun in his office and, after 9 p.m., an armed guard patrols the aisles wearing a bullet-proof vest, carrying mace, a knife, a pistol and a nightstick.

 

Choi employs 20 blacks, but pays them just above minimum wage. He provides no benefits. He says he has never been in the home of a black person nor read anything on the African-American experience. He finds it difficult to befriend most blacks and adds, "I can't deny that there's been resentment about racial differences."

 

Still, about three years ago, Choi decided to let two young boys, Willie, then 11, and Maurice Matthews, then 12, work at his store after the pair would hang out there and asked for work.

 

At first, Choi discouraged the boys from working, then he changed his mind because the pair seemed to be in need and were eager to please. "I thought my store could be a kind of sanctuary for them," he said. Choi let the pair deliver groceries to cars for customers for a tip. Later, the boys were sent on errands. On some days, the boys would make $15 each.

 

With their parents approval, the boys came regularly. Choi bought them clothes and, occasionally, gave them cash, Choi said. (The parents declined to be interviewed for this story, nor would they allow the boys to be interviewed.)

 

Choi says the experience was helping to open him to the black community.

 

In December, following a complaint, a Labor Department investigator came and demanded to see employee records. He complied. In a subsequent visit, he told investigators he was trying to help the boys.

 

Records from the Labor Department say this admission was used against him, "He admitted to the involvement of Maurice and Willie in his store," the records say. The good intentions, the government said, were not a defense. According to the records, obtained through a Freedom of Information Act, government investigators never tried to question the boys.

 

In January, the Department decided to fine Choi and told him so. Choi decided to appeal to a higher official at the Labor Department. However, the official didn't accept his arguments and, about two weeks later, sent him a notice to pay $5,386 in back wages, but told him they would forego any fines. Then, in May, the department changed its mind and hit him with a fine of $2,875. Choi appealed, but the fine was cut to only $1,500.

 

The Labor Department won't say who filed the complaint against Choi, but he thinks it might be a relative of the boys who came by demanding the children give her their money and, later, began complaining the boys were working illegally. After the complaint, he said he repeatedly asked the boys if he had abused them. The boys and other employees assured him he had not.

 

You might call this section the response from both sides:

 

"I'm not a hero," Choi says. He was simply, he and friends say, trying, for once, to do the right thing by the neighborhood. He says, further, he did not know anything about child labor laws nor did he willfully disobey any law.

 

The Labor Department and its investigators, despite repeated requests for interviews, would not discuss the specific case because of a policy that forbids comment on individual violations. However, a spokesman said the law specifically forbids children under 14 from working in commercial enterprises and it doesn't matter whether children or their parents supported or benefitted from any arrangement, as Choi claims happened in this case.

 

You might call this section the fallout:

 

Many believe the Department of Labor may have gone too far.

 

Dan Yager, an attorney for the Labor Policy Association, an employer group in Washington, says, "This case shows a lack of recognition of humanity by either the law or the people enforcing it."

 

Aminifu Woodrow Smith, the community director at the church near Choi's store, thinks the Labor Department should work out special arrangements allowing small enterprises like Choi's to keep children off the street.

 

Let's use the same ending as before, but alter it slightly, using a device that returns us to the beginning, the beginning being the thought of being a good neighbor.

 

After the decision, the boys returned to the store several times, but Choi sent them home. Ultimately, he lost track of them. Yet, despite it all, Choi wants to remain a good neighbor. "I've really never changed my mind about helping other youngsters, but I don't know any other way," he said.

 

The Personal News Story lede.

 

Step one:

 

What about this story makes it interesting from a human standpoint?

 

This works much the same as what I am calling the feature lede, but focuses, instead, on the person. Here's one approach:

 

 

ATLANTA - Brian Choi, a Korean grocer in West End, understood what it can take to run a business in the inner city.

 

His market in West End was boycotted in the early 1990s for allegedly overcharging blacks and not giving enough back to the community. Then, in 1992, a robber shot him at close range. He spent several days in the hospital and still carries a bullet in his chest.

 

The market is open 18 hours a day. Choi keeps a gun in his office and, after 9 p.m., an armed guard patrols the aisles wearing a bullet-proof vest, carrying mace, a knife, a pistol and a nightstick.

 

Still, none of that prepared him for $6,500 in fines from the Department of Labor for violation of child labor laws he never understood.

 

Notice another implied question: "What question didn't he understand?"

 

For about two years, Choi allowed two underage boys to deliver groceries to cars for tips and, also, gave the boys money for running errands.

 

The Labor Department decided it was violation of child labor and minimum wage laws.

 

I might put the reactions next, followed by the cosmic relevance:

 

Choi, for his part, says he was simply trying to make a difference in the lives of two young boys who begged him for work in this impoverished section of Atlanta. He says the boys' parents were supportive of the arrangement.

 

The Labor Department refused to comment on the specifics of the case, citing department policy, but a department spokesman said, "when a complaint involves child labor, it goes to the very top of the list."

 

For many advocates, the case is an example of how child-labor laws might not work in every situation, and, indeed, may cause harm.

 

"These informal work arrangements are widespread,"says Aminifu Woodrow Smith, community director of the Shrine of the Black Madonna, a church near Choi's store, "and are something the children and their parents have insisted on."

 

We won't bog you down with further details on the story, save to say, again, at this point we tell the story in more detail, chronologically, much as we did before. Let's move on to our last lede, the dramatic lede.

 

The Dramatic Lede:

 

Step One:

The first step is to ask what about this story makes this dramatic or what is the story's most dramatic moment. The lede becomes one that focuses on action, peril, drama or conflict, not so much on information. It is usually set in a scene or a situation. Here is an attempt here:

 

When an investigator from the Department of Labor asked grocer Brian Choi, a Korean, why he was employing two underage boys, he responded without hesitation and with complete honesty. He was simply trying to help these poverty stricken young men have a refuge from their tough surroundings.

 

He might have answered less candidly because the investigator had the admission of guilt he had been looking for. The department fined Choi $6,500 for his violation.

 

This lede might have been better with more detail about the surroundings or scene setting with more of the interaction. Still, you get the idea that the focus here is on the climactic event and this is where we begin. The character, the investigator is in peril from the government. Hence, the conflict. Note how we have still captured the essential irony of the situation of helping and being fined for it, not to mention we have a similar implied question of "why fine a man for helping children?"

 

From here, we answer a little more about the case and its importance. We first answer the implied question.

 

The simple issue to the Labor officials was Choi had been in violation of child labor laws, hence, needed a substantial fine. This issue, however, underscores to some analysts how a law can be misapplied in a setting that cried out for a good samaritan.

 

We next tell the story in more detail, to set the scene:

 

Choi's grocery sits in West End, one of Atlanta's more dangerous neighborhoods. Each night, a bullet-proof-vest-wearing security guard patrols the aisles of the store carrying a gun, some mace, a knife and a nightstick.

 

Complaints frequent the neighborhood. Choi and other merchants have faced boycotts for their allegedly high prices and for not giving enough back to the community. In 1992, he was shot by a robber. "I can't deny that there's been resentment about racial differences," he says.

 

Still, when Maurice and Willie Martin began showing up at his store asking for things to do, he gave in to their pleas. He gave them a job, letting them help customers go to cars with groceries for a tip. "I thought my store could be a kind of sanctuary for them," he says.

 

At this point, the story continues, as it has in other versions, chronologically. You get the drift, so we won't waste any more paper.

Good luck on your next story.
