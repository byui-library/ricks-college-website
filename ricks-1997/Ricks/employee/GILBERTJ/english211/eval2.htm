Evaluating Source Material

Once you find a source, it's important to critically evaluate it. Not all sources are created equal. In fact, some information parading as solid research material is really little more than well packaged personal opinion or propaganda. Learn how to spot good sources and refuse to use unreliable ones even if the information they offer supports your own stance. Here are four keys to recognizing good source material.

  • Source Credentials - Who is the author? What experience has she had in the field she is discussing? What educational background does she have? Is she affiliated with a special interests group, or does she make a profit by sponsoring a cause or sharing a particular opinion? How do other experts in the field view her work is she respected in her field?

    Each of these questions deal with the credibility of the author. You may be able to find a doctor touting the healing properties of a new skin lotion, but if he earns money for his statements or holds stock in the company, he may not be your best source of information. An independent study on the lotion's effectiveness would most likely be far more reliable.

  • Bias - This principle is closely tied to a source's credentials. Is the writer or organization biased? Are they working towards a particular political agenda, or do they make money by forwarding a certain perception of the data they present? Make sure you ask yourself what the author or organization has to gain in sharing their information. Now, that doesn't mean you need automatically discount such sources, but you should be aware that they are using facts and statistics as a means to a goal. It's often important to check their sources to make sure they aren't misrepresenting the data to serve their own purposes.

    For example, if I'm writing a paper on gun control, I would be wary of relying on facts and statistics either from the NRA (The National Rifle Association) or sponsors of the Brady Bill (landmark legislation regulating the sale of handguns). Both sources have a political and ideological stake in the argument. As a researcher, I would want to carefully check their sources and find other sources as unbiased as possible to offer an outside perspective.

  • Timeliness - How old is the information I'm using? A book on computer programming from 1969 won't do me much good unless I'm using it as a historical source. Computers have changed quite a bit in the last thirty years. How old are the statistics cited in the source? Murder rates from the 1970's have little bearing on current murder rates unless they're used in comparison. Make sure that the information you use is timely.

  • Application - Is the source material applicable to your work? Make sure the source information you choose is connected to your own research. Twisting sources to serve your purpose is dishonest, and often leads to misrepresentation of the original source. If the source you find isn't applicable, discard it and find another that is.

    Evaluating Web Pages
    The World Wide Web is quickly becoming an important research resource, but you need to be cautious. Virtually anyone can publish a web site. When a book is published by a reputable publisher, editors often check facts and figures, taking time to make sure that the information printed is accurate. Web pages, for the most part, do not pass through that process. Craig Branham of St. Louis University has published A Student's Guide to Research with the WWW. It offers information not only on how to find good research sources online, but how to evaluate them once you've found them. Work through the guide in its entirety. I won't accept web-based sources in your work until you've done so.
