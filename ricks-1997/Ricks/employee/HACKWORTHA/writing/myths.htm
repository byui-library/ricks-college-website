Correcting Myths about Writing
 
Let's begin by answering some questions, dispelling some myths, and learning some generalities about writing.
Is It Possible to Learn to Write Well?  Yes.  
Realize that writing is a skill.  Although raw talent affects one's powers as a writer, all students can improve their writing with appropriate practice.
To learn and improve any skill, a student must get on task, that is, practice appropriately.  Consider any skill: skiing, piano, dance, or guitar. Let's gain insight into writing as a skill by considering guitar playing.
Suppose that in a guitar class, I give the most exciting lectures, show the most instructive films, provide engaging demonstrations, and carry out provocative class discussions.  I do this during every class of the semester. The students are fascinated, pleased, entertained, and they gain some knowledge. But would they be able to play the guitar at the end of the semester?  Not at all.  This skill, like writing, requires regular, sustained, appropriate practice.
Well, it's [writing] a beautiful feeling, even if it's hard work.
-- Anne Sexton
Learning to write well takes time and much effort, but it can be done.
-- Margaret Mead
...Nothing is more satisfying than to write a good sentence. It is no fun to write lumpishly, dully, in prose the reader must plod through like wet sand. But it is a pleasure to achieve, if one can, a clear running prose that is simple yet full of surprises. This does not just happen. It requires skill, hard work, a good ear, and continued practice.
-- Barbara Tuchman
I believe in miracles in every area of life except writing. Experience has shown me that there are no miracles in writing. The only thing that produces good writing is hard work.
-- Isaac Bashevis Singer
Does Good Writing Depend on Inspiration?  No.  
If you believe this, you may sit around waiting for the moment of truth, the stroke of genius, and it may never come. Actually, writing, like Tuchman and Singer have said, is hard work. Perhaps, when you are struggling with your text, you may be inspired.  
The actual process of grappling with ideas can inspire you. You generate a creative atmosphere as you create. But if you wait to write only when you are inspired, you may not write much.  Although good writing depends on several factors, it partly depends on your willingness to rewrite and rewrite and rewrite your original text.
I rewrote the ending of A Farewell to Arms, the last page of it, thirty-nine times before I was satisfied.
-- Ernest Hemingway
I have never thought of myself as a good writer. Anyone who wants reassurance of that should read one of my first drafts.  But I'm one of the world's great revisers.      
-- James Michener
What makes me happy is rewriting.... It's like cleaning house, getting rid of all the junk, getting things in the right order, tightening things up.
-- Ellen Goodman
Writing is rewriting.
-- Donald Murray
Occasionally you can hit it right the first time. More often, you don't.
-- John Dos Passos
My first draft usually has only a few elements worth keeping. I have to find what those are and build from them and throw out what doesn't work.
-- Susan Sontag
Is There a Best Time to Write?  Not exactly.
Although we each follow a different biological clock, basically people can write effectively at any time of the day.  For me, anytime is fine except when I am drowsy. Yet I suspect I do my best work early in the morning when my energy level is high.  
Look at the writing task as you would look at any job.  Punch the clock and go to work.  After a period of time, you punch out. Then you repeat the process the next day.
I prefer to get up very early in the morning and work.
-- Katherine Anne Porter
The desk is in the room, near the bed, with a good light, [I write] midnight till dawn....
-- Jack Kerouac
Is Writing a Process?  Yes.
This means there are certain steps that we work through as we write. Baking a cake is a process.  To bake a cake, you work through several steps. However, different from baking a cake, the writing process is fluid, not rigid. Therefore, the steps of the process may differ in order from one writer to another.  The writing process can be divided into three general steps: pre-writing (invention), writing, and rewriting. But as we write, we may move back and forth from one step to another.  For example, after we start writing, we may go back and adjust our outline which we developed in the pre-writing stage.  
You are always going back and forth between the outline and the writing, bringing them closer together, or just throwing out the outline and making a new one.
-- Annie Dillard
You have to work problems out for yourself on paper. Put the stuff down and read it -- to see if it works.
-- Joyce Cary
What is Free Writing?
Once you have a clear idea about your subject, and once you have identified some points and details which will support your subject, you should do some focused free writing.  This means that you write as quickly and as spontaneously as possible.  Then, after all your words have been transferred from your mind to the screen, begin to edit and revise.  
When you free write, don't worry about any errors or how smoothly your writing will flow.  At this point, your aim is only to be spontaneous and quick.  Such an approach has some advantages over an edit-as-you-go method.  With free writing you will accomplish more work in a shorter amount of time.  Also, you will experience less frustration than with a write-revise-start-over method.
Finally, with free writing you will be more creative.  A reason for this is because free writing can reduce negative inhibitions which tend to diminish your powers as a writer.  For example, if you were to play a piano solo, when would you likely do your best job?  (1) when you are aware of two hundred eyes critically watching you or  (2) when you are completely alone, feeling the presence of no external audience.
The fear of a critical audience can also inhibit our writing.  When you free write, you must completely forget the audience.  Just write, quickly and furiously.  Just get your thoughts out of your head onto the screen without stopping to ponder and re-read what you have written. Once your paper is written, you should begin to edit, proof, and revise.  It is with revision that your paper will gain much of its strength.  
Use your ear to revise, that is, read your paper out loud.  Cause your sentences to flow clearly and logically from one idea to the next.
There may be some reason to question the whole idea of fineness and care in writing. Maybe something can get into sloppy writing that would elude careful writing. I'm not terribly careful myself, actually. I write fairly rapidly if I get going. ... In trying to treat words as chisel strokes, you run the risk of losing the quality of utterance, the rhythm of utterance, the happiness.
-- John Updike
Can Writing Be Used As a Means to Discover Meaning in One's Life? Yes.  
When we write about any subject, we find limitations in our understanding. These limitations may cause us to think about or study more deeply the subject. Also, as we write, we clarify our own thinking. Additionally, we often think of new points about the subject that escaped our earlier thoughts.
I write entirely to find out what I'm thinking, what I'm looking at, what I see and what it means.
-- Joan Didion
How do I know what I think until I see what I say.
-- E. M. Forster
Writing, like life itself, is a voyage of discovery.
-- Henry Miller
I don't see writing as a communication of something already discovered, as `truth' already known. Rather, I see writing as a job of experiment. It's like any discovery job; you don't know what's going to happen until you try it.
-- William Stafford
I am never as clear about any matter as when I am just finished writing about it.
-- James van Allen

What Will I Write About?  
For several reasons, start writing autobiography.  Break away from a tendency to copy sentences written others.  By writing about YOUR life, you gain confidence in your own abilities to speak (write) with your own voice.
 
Usually, when a man quits writing in his journal ... he has lost interest in life.    
-- E. B. White
Keep a journal. Pay so much honor to the visits of truth to your mind as to record them.  
-- Ralph Waldo Emerson
The journal is a record of experience and growth, not a preserve of things well done or said.  
-- Henry David Thoreau
 
Do all Teachers Take the Same Approach to Writing?  No.
In fact, it may be confusing for some students to study writing from different teachers.  One teacher may teach one thing; another may teach something different.  Or so it seems. Actually, each teacher may be right.  For example, your high school teacher may have taught creative writing and perhaps your college teacher teaches expository writing. Try to understand some generalities about two types of writing.
Expository and Creative Writing
 
Expository writing explains things.  For a memory trick, notice that both words, expository and explains, start with the letter "e."  Use the letter "e" to make this connection--expository writing explains.   Expository is the type of writing you do in most of your college classes.  It is also the type of writing done in business.  It includes letters to editors, to businesses and manufacturers, and reports and memos.  Creative writing relates to short stories, poems, novels, and plays.  Next, study the summary which shows the general differences between expository and creative writing.  You will find it useful.
