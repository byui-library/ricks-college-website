Three readings from history:

The Federalist Papers #10 -- http://lcweb2.loc.gov/const/fed/fed_10.html

This most important document in American political thought outlines James Madison's reasoning about why a large republic is better than a small one.

In essence -- in small republics like ancient Greece, one group is the majority, controlling all of the economic and political interests. That means a minority is prejudiced against, easily. Voters can choose to take the rights of others. As in Idaho, Mormons were forbidden to vote by a vote of the rest of the people for a period of time.

In a large republic, such excesses are turned and prevented more easily. While Mormons dominate in Utah, to get anything done nationally, they must combine with Creoles of Louisiana or African-Americans from Chicago or otherwise to get anything accomplished. In a large republic, the idea of one dominant majority is much less coherent than in a small one. That means "factions" are less likely to dominate our country.

You job as a journalist is to help all views be aired so various factions and interests can combine over time on various issues. Another profound idea is the power of "Consent of the Governed" as opposed to the consensus. These are two different things. Consent comes through a long working-through of issues. We, as journalists, must help build consent by helping the public go through the "working-through" process by giving more than just two diametrically opposed views.

The Declaration of Independence -- http://lcweb2.loc.gov/const/declar.html

This remarkable founding document is interesting reading when you consider that, to some people, the very reasons for complaining about Britain as outlined in the Declaration may sometimes exist today in our own system.

Of particular note is the second paragraph that man has a right to life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness. Whence happiness? In the creative process. In association. In owning things you can improve. In learning. In , of course, the religious process. In truth regardless of its forms. These are protected in the First Amendment freedoms. -- speech, religion, thought and association are all great freedoms. This is why the First Amendment is so powerful and what the Declaration was all about and why as a journalist you should understand it.

The Bill of Rights -- http://lcweb2.loc.gov/const/bor.html

Read particularly Amendments 1, 10 , 2 and 14, if you can, though 14 isn't part of the bill of rights.

You will hear numerous people speak of the power of the Bill of Rights. Congress shall make no Law. This gives you -- and every other person -- the right to publish things. By the way, the right exists with those who publish, not the writers, unless the publisher and the writer are the same person.

Korihor spent much of his time railing for the rights of the people. Rights though sacred, must be coupled with responsibility. Your responsiblity is to truth, fairness and excellence in writing, reporting and content.
