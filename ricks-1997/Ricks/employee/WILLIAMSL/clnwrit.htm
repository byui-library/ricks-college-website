A Guide to Cleaner Writing

Basic comma rules:

Known to strike terror in many hearts, the comma is actually a simple friend that helps clarify writing.
· Commas separate independent clauses - a group of words that acts like a sentence - connected by a coordinating conjunction - and, or, but, nor, for, while, yet.
I love to eat ice cream, and my wife enjoys taco salad.
He started to play guitar, but he turned to the flute instead.

· A comma separates items in a series of three or more. Use a comma after all items in the series except the last. (Exception: If you write for a newspaper, you will put one after all but the last two.)
Jane plays, basketball, volleyball, and tennis.
Joe plays baseball and football, drinks milk and juice, reads books, and watches videos.

· A comma separates equal adjectives modifying the same noun.
Equal adjectives could add "and" between them without changing the meaning.
I faced the cruel, unusual punishment with a sense of humor. (You might say cruel AND unusual.)
I ate the delicious Chinese food. (You would not say delicious AND Chinese food, would you?)

· Commas separate non-essential phrases from the flow of the sentence. Call this the polygamist rule. If you need information to differentiate among items in a group . . . no comma is needed. If the information is extraneous, insert a comma. That is, if you have more than one husband, it would be my husband John. If you have only one, his name is "non-essential." That makes it my husband, John. Another classic example of this is that vs. which. That sets off essential information so no commas. Which sets off unessential information and, therefore, requires a comma. Some other examples of this principle follow:
I strongly believe Bill Bixby, a leading contender for the presidency, will receive the award.
Latisha Doe, 14, ate a burger.

· Commas separate introductory phrases from the sentence's subject.
Known to strike terror in timid hearts, the comma is a simple friend. (The comma is the subject here.)

· Commas separate quotations from the rest of the sentence.
The official said, "I think we should leave the room now."
"I'll study tomorrow," the student told a friend.

· State names following city names are non-essential.
Bill Clinton is from Little Rock, Ark., and is a respected leader.
· Commas separate big numbers . . . though not years.
I ate 1,237 peas at one sitting.
I was born in 1980.

· Commas eliminate confusion with repeating words.
What the matter is, is hard to know.
Comma Problems:

Comma errors come like nightmares. Here's how to wake up.
· The comma splice connects sentences that need a conjunction.
Red is incorrect style . . ..
(I love to eat ice cream, my wife enjoys taco salad.)
· Commas hate being around non-essential prepositional phrases.
(Bill Riley, of Rexburg, hit the cop.)
"Of Rexburg" is a prepositional phrase.

This would be correct, however:
Bill Riley, Rexburg, hit the cop.

· Commas hate interrupting one subject that works with only two verbs.
(My boss drew up plans for reorganization, and left on a long vacation.)
This would be correct: My boss drew up plans and left on a vacation.

· Commas hate interrupting a verb from only two objects.
(The mayor praised the students, and the members of the PTA.)
This would be correct: The mayor praised the students and the members of the PTA.

· Commas connecting independent clauses get tricky when you use something other than coordinating conjunctions.
(I went to the store, however I forgot my credit card.)
This would be correct: I went to the store; however, I forgot my credit card.
Some Other Thoughts:
Antecedents: One writing problem is the reference to the antecedent . . . the word a pronoun represents. Rewrite to correct these errors: John told Bill his car was ugly. ( John's car or Bill's car?) John told Bill John's car was ugly. Or . . . They make poor cars in Detroit. (Who does? GM? Ford?) AMC makes poor cars. Or . . . The chickens cross the road. The stop signs fall. This makes traffic dangerous. (This? The chickens? The signs?) This situation makes traffic dangerous.
Apostrophes: An apostrophe shows ownership, not more than one. Put an apostrophe before an "s" if you have a singular noun; after with a plural one - unless it is made plural without an "s." Jane's car; the Johnsons' house.;Women's Week; Mothers' Week. I bought 16 banana's. Use apostrophes when deleting existing letters for a contraction. Do not becomes don't. Hence, the early '80s is a contraction. Be careful about its and it's. Its does show possession, but this is a pronoun. Would you use an apostrophe for his or her? It's is a contraction - it is.
· Fragments. Sentence fragments lack a subject or verb. Bill went to the store and bought food. Pretzels and other snacks especially. Bill went to the store and bought food, especially pretzels and other snacks.
· He vs. they: When a person goes to the store, should he buy groceries? Many say a "he" used here is sexist, so timid writers stumble. Some writers write, when a person goes to the store, they should buy groceries. One person, however, cannot be a they. Still other writers replace he with she or even the awkward he/she. Gulp. Both approaches draw attention to the writer showing his or her sensitivity, just as this sentence does. To keep the attention on the meaning, do this: Change the antecedent to a plural and then use they. When people go to the store, they should buy groceries. . . . When writers use this approach, they show more skill.
· It vs. they: A group cannot be an it. If a team plays well, they did not play well, but it played well. The team (or council or organization or any group) is an it. The team members, however, are they.
Which word should I use?
Accept/Except: Accept is to take and except is to exclude.

Affect/Effect: Affect is usually a verb and effect is usually a noun, unless you mean to cause . . . then use effect as a verb.

Among/Between: Among is three or more; Between involves two.

Bad/Badly: Use bad to describe feelings or character - He feels bad. He is bad. Use badly for adverb - He plays badly.

Disinterested/Uninterested: Disinterested is impartial. Uninterested is bored.

Ensure/Insure: Use ensure unless you are talking about insurance in the business sense.

Farther/Further: Use farther when you would use far. Use further for things like time.

Fewer/Less: Use fewer if you can count them. Fewer than 10 $1 bills. Less than $10.

Good/Well: Use good to describe character - He is good. Use well for adverbs and feelings. - He plays well. He doesn't feel well.

Imply/Infer: Imply is what a speaker does. Infer is what a listener does.

It's/Its: "It's" is a contraction for it is. Use its as you'd use his or hers.

Lay/Lie: To lay is to set down. To lie is to do it yourself without help.

Laid/Lay: These are the past tense versions of Lay/Lie.

Like/As: Use like as you would a preposition, use as like a clause.

There/Their/They're: Use there for a place like here. Use their when you would use her. Use they're when you would use "they are."

To/Too/Two: Two is 2. To is to go to Rexburg. Too is too much to go too (also).

Try to/Try and: Always try to do; never try and do.

Who/Whom: Use who when you would use he and whom when you would use him.

Who's/Whose: Use whose like hers and who's as you would who is.
Some Important Writing Principles:
(Again, red shows incorrect examples.)
Use active voice:
John hit the ball. The ball was hit by John.

Avoid the word not.
Do not bless the proud. Curse the proud. Bless the meek.

Use specific, concrete language.
A period of inclement weather set in. It rained for six days.

Avoid using there as a subject.
There were a dozen at the game. Twelve men went to the game.

Avoid "izing" nouns to make a verb.
He utilized green paint. He used green paint.

Omit needless words.
Follow the earlier rules. Remember the adage attributed to Mark Twain that if he had had more time, he would have written shorter. Many phrases add nothing but clutter. Some examples of needless words, though others abound, include:
very, really, extremely; whether or not; the fact that; the reason for that is because.
Show, don't tell.
This means using specific, detailed language. When you mean cold, don't just say cold, but describe for readers the frost curling on a mustache or the visible breath of an antelope. Make it so a reader can feel cold without you ever using the word cold itself.

Say what you mean. Mean what you say.
Writing is an act of bravery. Be direct. Readers must understand your meaning without equivocation. Give the meaning with a straight needle. Be brave. Find your voice. You have something to say worth saying. Your readers can decide what to read.
