A Short Biography

A little about myself? Well, here goes.  I was born on an Idaho dairy farm and spent much of my youth moving pipe, hauling hay, and milking cows—just the sort of motivation a youth needs to get an education. And yet I despised school: the regimentation, the social terrorism, the irrelevance, and, most of all, the boredom. My form of rebellion was to escape to the woods along the Snake River where I spent hours tracking deer in the snow, watching beaver ferry back and forth, soaking in the beauty of nature, pondering and writing, and generally eschewing responsibility. From my desultory and aimless youth I was saved by a series of apparently banal, but for me momentous, events.

First, when I was fifteen, I chanced upon a book by a Greek writer, Nikos Kazantzakis, entitled The Greek Passion. To this point my reading had been haphazard and uneven in quality. But here was a book with punch, a book about the passion of religion and the transformation of the soul. 

The next year my mother died of cancer. She was bright, educated, and a voracious student, and she loved life. This, too, deeply affected me. From her I learned to love beauty and to enjoy the puzzles of existence.

That same year an English teacher introduced me to Thoreau’s Walden. I read it ten times that year. The language enchanted me, the humor delighted, but the thought…here was a new world of ideas I had never heard of nor imagined. I had to know this world, the whole of it, to drink it down to the dregs. And so, Thoreau plopped me down in the world of ideas, where I have remained, a vagabond, ever since, much to the chagrin of my dear wife, who would have me in this world more often.

At seventeen I came to Ricks College, infinitely naïve and full of dreams, and studied French and English. My first mentor Larry Thompson introduced me to a myriad of writers and thinkers, and their ongoing conversations. T.S. Eliot and John Milton, in particular, deeply influenced me during that time. After a brisk and delirious ten months at Ricks, I graduated, worked a summer at a gold mine in the Mojave Desert, and was off on a mission to Ecuador, "halfway up the Andes, in a clime that knows no autumn."

After that, came Brigham Young University and an almost immediate marriage to the beautiful, brilliant, and redoubtable Barbara Gale, who has so filled and shaped my life since that I cannot begin to do justice to her and her influence. Most of all, she has taught me to respect spirit and heart, as well as intellect. And she assiduously reminds me that I do not know what I often suppose I know. 

At BYU I was consumed with a longing to know, and I thought that by taking as many classes as possible, I would come to know. As a result, by the time I graduated from BYU four years later, I had completed majors in Greek, Latin, English, and philosophy. It wasn’t that I couldn’t make up my mind what I wanted to do and be. Rather, I wanted to do and be too many things—a rather pitiable state if one expects to fulfill one’s desires.

Oxford University, the next year, was a watershed place and time for me. I had heard of Oxford, I think, when I was very young, because I had wanted to go there since I can remember. But how could a country bumpkin like me even dream of it? This thought, of course, brings to mind Thomas Hardy’s Jude the Obscure, whose titular character I hope I do not resemble. But I was accepted and I did go, for as long as the money held out, which, unfortunately wasn’t long enough to complete the degree. But I did read ancient history and philosophy with some of the finest scholars. And the experience of the tutorial system has profoundly shaped my educational philosophy. The day Barbara and I decided that we could no longer live on potatoes and water and that we would have to come home was one of the most regrettable of my life.

Ricks College, to my great fortune, had an opening in the English department that year. I called from Oxford, got the job, and so enjoyed the year teaching at Ricks that I resolved to teach here for good. But first I wanted the vaunted Ph.D. So we were off to study philosophy at the University of Texas at Austin, whose philosophy program is among the best in the country. Meanwhile, Barbara and I had two children, a boy and a girl, and, after much labor and anguish, a dissertation and a diploma.

Now I am settling down to continue my education—now that I've done remodeling our home—because, after all of this schooling, my education has only just begun. And I think that I have been, and will in this life always be, a child playing with bobbles and shells on the seashore, while a vast and dark continent of meaning and mystery stretches out from where I play into infinity. But oh, what play!

B. L. Merrill
25 January 2001
