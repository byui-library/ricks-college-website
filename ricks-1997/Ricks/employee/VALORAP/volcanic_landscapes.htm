Features of Volcanic Landscapes

                                                                                                      Figure 1.  A composite volcanic landscape.   

     If queried about  a volcanic landscape, it is probable that most people would describe a typical composite cone such as Mt. Fujiyama, Mt. Rainier in Washington, or another of the numerous symmetrical cones of the Pacific Northwest.  While these beautiful mountains are deservedly recognized, volcanic landscapes are much more complex and varied.  Figure 1, above, is a drawing of an idealized volcanic subsurface and some of the surface manifestations of   the underlying igneous mass.  Features at the surface include a composite cone which is constructed of alternating layers of lava, ash and other pyroclastic materials.  Figures 2 through 5, below are examples of composite cones.

    Composite cones are potentially dangerous because of andesite lava which is very explosive.  In the foreground of the block diagram are two other volcanic deposits.  A laccolith, which is shown on the left, is an igneous mass deposited concordantly between existing rocks materials.   The overlying materials are domed upward where they may be subjected to vigorous erosion.  Below are pictures of a number of laccoliths in eastern and southern Utah.  If one visits the east and west sides of the Henry Mountains he can observe the erosional remnants of titlted sediments which originally overlaid the mountains.  Navajo Mountain, shown in figure 6 is still covered by the original sediments and it is inferred that it is underlain by the igneous mass of the laccolith, as shown in the diagram of figure 7.

     In the center foreground of the surface features is a cinder cone (see also, Figure 12.10 in your text book, pg. 321).  This is the smallest of volcanoes, produces little lava and is made up largely of pyroclastic materials.  They are found throughout the Western United States, including Utah and Idaho.  As a matter of fact, the Menan Buttes (the northern cone is locally known as "R" mountain) are recognized as classic cinder cones, and are frequently cited in Geology and Physical Geography texts (Figure 12).   The cones which have formed along the rifts of Craters of the Moon and adjacent areas are also cinder cones.  Another well know example is Sunset Crater cone a few miles north and east of Flagstaff, Arizona.

    Scattered throughout the world are many mountain ranges of igneous origin.  These usually have their genesis as large very deep intrusions of magma called plutons.  Because they were first deposited considerable distances below the surface these plutons are exposed at the surface only after millions of years of erosion.  Small exposed plutons covering a surface area of less than 40 square miles are called stocks.     Batholiths are larger exposures associated with the development of significant mountain ranges.  They originated as molten fluid magma, which, as it intruded melted and incorporated the surrounding rock materials into its mass.  The margins of batholiths are the site of large scale (Regional) metamorphism.  As one travels westward across the Front Range of the Rocky Mountains north or south of Denver, Colorado, he will first traverse tilted sedimentary rock, (Figures 14 & 15, adapted from http://www-cs-students.stanford.edu/~andgh/Colorado/flatirons.html) then metamorphics and finally the batholithic igneous core of the range.

        Two other rather common features of volcanic landscapes sills and dikes, are also identified in the Figure 1 sketch.     Sills (Figure 16), form as magma intrudes concordantly between existing horizontal layers of rock.  Dikes, shown in Figures 17 and 18, form when the magma intrudes into existing rock materials cutting across the bedding planes.  Often a dike is more resistant than the material into which it has intruded.  As the weaker material is weathered and eroded, the dike will then be exposed and elevated above the original rock mass as demonstrated in the photos below.

    One of the characteristics of volcanic deposits is their tendency to form columnar joints as they cool.  This joint pattern is apparent in Figure 16 above.  Devils Tower in Wyoming (Figures 19 & 20) is a superb example of this characteristic joint pattern.  As a matter of fact, these joints were the basis of the Native American myth on the origin of the tower.
