Our faculty work hard to keep the program running and care about the students they teach. We appreciate the excellent job they do for everyone they work with. Below is a quick biography of each of them.

Jeff Andersen            HOME PAGE

Jeff Andersen has taught Humanities at Ricks College since 1988, having previously worked for Saudi-Arabian Airlines in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia and International English Institute in Fresno, California. He completed his formal training at Brigham Young University. Brother Andersen currently serves as Chair of the Department of Humanities and Philosophy at Ricks. In addition to Humanities, he has taught German, English, and Religion courses. Brother Andersen has a passion for travel, having visited 29 countries in Europe, Asia, Africa, and the South Pacific. He taught for the BYU Study-Abroad Program in Vienna, Austria and has directed seven student tours to Europe for Ricks College. Climbing a pyramid in Egypt, scuba diving in the Red Sea, and riding an elephant in the jungles of Thailand are among his most memorable experiences.

Robyn Bergstrom         HOME PAGE

Robyn Bergstrom has been teaching at Ricks since 1994 in the Communication Department. She attended Ricks College, and moved on to Brigham Young University for her Bachelors Degree in Communication with a minor in Psychology and English. She later received a Resource Specialty from the University of Utah, and her Masters Degree is in Communication from the University of North Dakota. She loves teaching both Interpersonal Communication and Small Group Communication. She is the Assistant Coach to the Forensics team, and advisor to the Interpersonal Communication Club. She has worked with the Chicago Urban Studies program, the Humanities tour of Europe, and the Connection program for incoming freshman. She enjoys spending time with her family and friends. She is married to Scott Bergstrom and have four children. Mark, Angie, Alison, and Erik. She loves the statement made by Nelson Mandela. "Our deepest fear is not that we are inadequate. Our deepest fear is that we are powerful beyond measure. It is our light not our darkness, that most frightens us. We ask ourselves, 'Who am I to be brilliant, gorgeous, talented, fabulous?' Actually, who are you not to be? You are a child of God."

Rick Davis          HOME PAGE

Rick Davis may have grown up in Rexburg but he has seen the world. His mission was in France and Switzerland where he gained his love for Europe. He holds degrees from the University of Idaho and Arizona State and was in law school when he was drafted into the Viet Nam War in ‘68. Since coming to Ricks in 1971 he has taught courses in Western Culture, Humanities, Music Appreciation, and Piano, the latter being something he has played and enjoyed his entire life. He served as Chairman of the Humanities department for 14 years, and is currently the Director of the Honors Program.

A lover of diversity, he has participated in departmental tours to Europe, co-authored two books, owns and operates a professional photography studio, currently serves as the Madison County Coroner, a post he has held for 20 years, and just this past spring was the narrator/bass soloist in the college’s production of Immanuel. Favorite quote: "Men should be anxiously engaged in a good cause, and do many things of their own free will, for the power is in them."

Lynn Firestone          HOME PAGE

I started my road to the Ricks College biology department when I enrolled at Slippery Rock University in Western Pennsylvania where I was raised. After attending Slippery Rock University for two years, I was introduced to the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-Day Saints through my brother and a set of missionaries. I was a "golden contact" and quickly joined the Church. I immediately made plans to transfer to BYU where I finished my Bachelor’s program in Secondary Education, Zoology. I then remained at BYU and earned a Masters Degree in Botany, doing my research in genetics and mine reclamation studies. Next , I migrated north to Utah State where I began a Ph.D. in genetic engineering. However, when my major professor left biology to become a Computer Science teacher, I was left high and dry. I then began a program in Plant Systematics. I later transferred to Idaho State University where I received a Doctorate of Arts degree in Biology. While I was working on my doctorate at ISU, I became aware of a position here at Ricks and "the rest is history", as they say. My first love is botany but I have received thorough training in 11 different areas of biology.

I sincerely enjoy being a part of the honors program because of the dedicated and motivated students who respond well to my philosophy of teaching students how to appreciate their ability to THINK and to become lifelong learners.

Murray Hunt

Although a native of Idaho, I grew up in the San Francisco Bay Area before serving a mission to Winnipeg, Canada. I returned to the Bay Area for a graduate degree in Humanities and Philosophy from Stanford after marrying my girlfriend, Maryjo Colton, and graduating from BYU. Before landing at Ricks, however, I raked leaves for my mother, delivered newspapers and pizzas, wrote procedures for purchasing frozen lunches at a top-secret government installation, and taught at Washington State University--whatever it took to support our nine children.

I thoroughly enjoy Rexburg, Ricks, and the wonderful students (especially those in the Honors Program). My goal in the classroom is to make the academic experience exciting, interesting, and lively, but demanding; for if learning isn’t a joy, then students will not choose to do it on their own out of the classroom, which is what a higher education really ought to be about after all.

Eric Walz                HOME PAGE

Eric Walz has been teaching at Ricks since 1987 in the History Department. Early in his academic career he graduated from Ricks before going on to receive a Bachelors Degree in History from Idaho State University, a Masters Degree from Utah State University, and a Doctorate Degree in History from Arizona State University. In graduate school, Brother Walz's major areas of study included Modern U.S. History, the History of the American West, and the History of Modern Japan. His personal research focuses on Japanese immigration to the United States between 1882 and 1924 and the development of ethnic communities in the states of Arizona, Colorado, Idaho, Nebraska, and Utah. Bro. Walz has presented papers in conferences in New York City; Ann Arbor, Michigan; and Sacramento, California. He has several published articles in professional journals and is expecting the publication of his first book in the near future. His greatest joy, however, is getting to know the students at Ricks on a personal level. Eric and his wife Lynne have seven children, five girls and two boys, ranging in age from twenty-two to seven. They also have one granddaughter.

David Ward           HOME PAGE

Speak your latent conviction, and it shall be the universal sense; for the inmost in due time becomes the outmost, and our first thought is rendered back to us by the trumpets of the Last Judgment . . . Yet we dismiss without notice our thoughts, because they are our’s. In every work of genius we recognize our own rejected thoughts; they come back to us with a certain alienated majesty . . . The power that resides in us is new in nature, and none but we know what that is which we can do, nor do we know until we have tried . . . [Yet] we but half-express ourselves, and are ashamed of that divine idea which each of us represents. (Self-Reliance, Ralph Waldo Emerson)

Notwithstanding the fact that I have graduated from Brigham Young University with a Masters degree in American Literature and Composition Philosophy, and from The University of Michigan with a Ph.D in English and Education, I continue to feel like a kid in an adult world. Perhaps this is why I live to learn from a variety of sources. However, this zeal for learning didn’t begin in elementary school. In fact, I don’t think I ever experienced the excitement endemic to genuine learning until college–particularly during my graduate work at the University of Michigan. There my eyes were opened to the marvel of making connections between myself and a myriad of ideas. Those who shared their ideas in speech and in print were often on the cutting edge of their discipline of their time. Whether it was in science history, in art history, in language, in myth and culture, in the philosophy of education, in social psychology, in music, music history, or in religion, I became enamored of the transforming power of creative thought as manifest by the miracle of the spoken and written word. Consequently, in all of what I teach--in my writing classes, my literature class, my reading class, my C.S. Lewis class--I am moved to invite my students into the wonder of thinking for themselves while I teach to develop in them a commitment to pay the price necessary to connect authentically with wisdom greater than themselves.
