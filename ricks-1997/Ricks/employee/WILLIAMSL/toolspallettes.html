 An Introduction to Adobe Photoshop:

Tools and Pallettes

Adobe Photoshop offers many unique tools and pallettes.

By TOOLS, I mean electronic tools that change all or parts of your photograph by hand.

By PALLETTES, I mean small boxes that give the parameters of your tools and the parameters of the changes you make.

By PIXELS, I mean the smallest dots of color that together make up the total picture. The more pixels, the higher the resolution of your image. For all of your web work, always use 72 pixels per inch.

Let's start with the most common element of Adobe Photoshop, The Tools Pallette:

This image above shows all of the tools available to you. Note how some of these images -- such as the one with a lasso -- have small arrows at the bottom. That means more than one tool is available here. Click and hold on those icons and the new tools will appear.

Now, what is in the tools pallette:

1. If you click on the very top -- bumpy -- portion of the pallette, you can move it around your computer window as you click and drag.

2. Click on the small marbled image below it and you will get on your screen information about the Photoshop you have -- including the serial number -- should you ever need it. Chances are, you'll rarely use this feature.

3. The next item down is the Marquee Tool. This is the one selected in our example and is the first tool at the top left. The marquee tool -- which can also be in a circular shape -- is one of the most common tools you'll use. It selects a portion of the image. To use, click on the marquee tool, then click and drag your tool across a certain portion of the image and let go. A rectangle -- or circular shape -- will appear across that portion of your screen surrounded by a moving dotted line. You can make changes to this portion that won't affect the rest of the image. You can cut it out or copy it.

4. To its right is the Move Tool. The move tool moves selected portions-- or layers. Since we really haven't defined layers for you, this may be confusing, but basically, you move a portion of the image. You move the selected part from the marquee tool, for example-- leaving the background color showing. (When you move a layer, the background image will show, not the color.) This is another common tool. With the move tool selected, you can also do some tranformation, such as rotation, etc., under the layer menu.

Time for a quick diversion to tell you about the colors.

Right beneath the tools shaped like a hand and a magnifying class, you can see two squares -- a black one over a white one. These are colors you will use to modify your image. You can select different colors than just black and white. You can reverse the background and foreground colors -- making the background black -- by clicking on the small, two-way arrow just to the right and above those two squares. (The miniature squares just below and to the left let you go back to where you started from -- black and white.)

5. The next is the Lasso Tool. This enables you to select an area of the picture that is neither square nor round. (The Polygon Lasso Tool is part of this too.) Just run the tool around the area with a click and drag. Let go of the mouse button when you've got the area selected. With the polygon lasso tool, you can click at points along the way to change direction. This often makes it easier to select parts of an image.

6. The next tool, to the right of the lasso, is the Magic Wand Tool. When you select this tool, put it on a color you wish to select. (See the Masks instruction set for a broader discusssion of this useful tool.) It will pick all of the colors exactly contiguous -- next -- to that small pixel you picked -- with some adjustments. For example, say you have a black shadow in a picture, merely click on the black part of the image and everything black in that area will be surrounded by that dotted line.

7. The next is the Airbrush Tool. This applies your foreground color to your image in a way similar to an airbrush. You can have it solid -- like this -- notice the solid dot . . .

8. The next tool is the Paintbrush Tool. This tool allows you to draw over the picture. In pallettes we'll show you later, you can change the width of the brush and how clean the line at the end of the brush is. (It might have a feathered edge, not one as abrupt as shown.) 9. The next is the useful Eraser Tool. This will eliminate portions of your image and make the background color -- as shown in this example -- (or background layer) emerge. This tool is also useful in getting rid of portions of a selection. We'll show you more about this feature later in quick-masking, but say you had just a little too much showing in your marquee selection, then the eraser would cut that amount back.

10. The Pencil Tool acts like an electronic pencil and is used like a narrow brush:

11. The next is the Rubber Stamp Tool. This tool is rarely used but can be useful. Select the rubber stamp and then option-click on a portion of the image. Next, click and hold at another spot in the image. As you move your tool around, from where you option-clicked, you will copy that part over the other part of the image. (It might be useful in cutting out, oh, say a disliked family member from a portrait and replacing him with the background behind. )

15. The Pen Tool is next. Use this to create paths. Essentially, this is another way to create selections and draw lines over portions of your image. With the increasing sophistication of selection in photoshop, this tool has become less useful. One thing that you can do, however, is to outline objects -- create a path -- and then apply certain effects to that path. We won't show you the paths pallette, but spend some time with this on your own, if you would like.

16. Next to the pen tool is the Type Tool. In subsequent lessons, we'll spend some time teaching you about type effects. Essentially this tool allows you to write words over you picture.

17. The Line Tool on the next row is just as described. It lets you easily draw straight lines.

18. The Gradient Tool, which is, again, something we'll talk about in other lessons, allows a user to put a fade -- a gradient -- across the image or parts of it.

19. The Paint Bucket Tool is again relatively rare in usage. When you click on a pixel, it fills in the foreground color in that pixel plus other, nearby pixels, depending on the parameters you set. Other ways of doing this exist by selecting. Still, it can be useful.

20. The powerful Eyedropper Tool allows you to select the color in one pixel. When you click on that pixel in your photo, or in your swatches pallette, that color will automatically become your foreground color. This is the best way to change colors and add effects with color.

21. The Hand Tool allows you to move the image around if all of your image is not showing on screen. If it is all on screen, this tool has no use.

22. The Magnify Tool does just what its design implies. Let's you see an area more closely. You can even get down to the pixel level and erase portions of the image one pixel at a time. Hold down the option key while you click and it de-magnifies.

Now, we've been through all of the tools, the next items here are the colors, which we've already discussed and five small boxes that are about how you view things on screen.

23. Most of the time the left box beneath the color pallette should be selected. However, the box at right is useful. It is the Quick-Mask Mode. The mode allows you to see what items have been selected more cleanly:

You can also see the portion you haven't selected:

(These tools are useful to make your selections more clean and to eliminate some portions of your selection with the eraser tool.) To change these mask modes, simply double click on the quick mask icon and it will give you the options of what to mask and even let you select the color of your mask.

24. Beneath these two mode buttons are other mode buttons. These show how they appear on screen. Use the default -- the one on the left or the one on the right -- which clears everything off your screen by the pallettes and the image -- the rest is black. This can be useful in helping you see how things look and in a less-distracted creative process.

Now, Let's go on to the basic pallettes:

To make a new pallette visible, go under your window menu and select them by the names we use here. If your menu says Hide _______, then the pallette is already open. It might be hiding behind another pallette.

Let's look at different pallettes, in not particular order of importance:

1. The first is the Action Pallette. This pallette allows you to automate tasks on one picture or several. It allows you to create several fun effects that you might use repeatedly. At the botton of the pallette, you can see buttons that look much like a VCR might have. To create a new action, click record and then perform your series of actions to accomplish your effect, and then press the stop button on the pallette. To engage the action you've saved, then you simply select the area you need and hit play. Make sure a check mark box is checked and the window-like icon is visible to run your action. You may need to click a few OKs along the way, but don't fret. It will save you time and trouble. This is powerful pallette. Play and learn.

2. The Brushes Pallette lets you set the width and feathering of your brushes for painting. Merely click on the one you want. In this pallette, you can change the hardness of a brush, making it have a firmer or softer edge, depending on your taste and desire. You can also set widths to exact pixels -- this is done with the menu that appears when you click on the arrow at the upper right of this pallette. Still, you have many options already available to you.

3. The Options Pallette is different for each tool you might have selected in the tools pallette. Here we show the options for the Airbrush tool. You may want to experiment with different modes, pressures. The eraser pallette, for example, lets you change your eraser from a straight block to one more like an airbrush or a narrow pencil.

4. The Channels Pallette lets you see the channels you are using in Photoshop. Channels represent just one color or all the colors, depending on the one your work in. If you were to edit the Red Channel below, you'd have effects applied to the reds of your image, but nothing else. You can add channels to mask (cover) parts of the image, as well. See the lesson on that topic.

5. The Layers Pallette shows you your layers. Layers are basically like where a young elementary student cuts pictures from a magazine and pastes them -- collage-like -- onto other parts of his construction paper world. Each new cutout is a new layer and may cover (mask) parts that have been pasted before. You'll use layers a lot. To select everything in a layer, by the way, load selection under the select menu.

6. The Colors Pallette helps you chooses your background and foreground colors. In my opinion, this pallette is less useful than others because you can just as easily select colors from the picture itself using your eyedropper.

7. The Info Pallette is another rare item to use, but it can be useful crossing platforms to, say Quark. Your seeing the percent colors of a pixel, its location on the scroon and the size of a selection. This can help you pick exact colors in Quark and plan your layout sizes exactly.

8. The Navigate Pallette is used to show your location within a broad picture. Basically, if you can't see all of the image on your screen, the navigate pallette lets you see everything else and what portion you can see. This is less useful on broader screens.

9. The Swatches Pallette is another way to pick your colors with the eyedropper. Since they are exact colors, this can be useful in printing and similar functions because you can more easily guess the final outcome of your color reproduction on the press.

 

Well, good luck and have fun exploring Photoshop.
