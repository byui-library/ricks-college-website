Concrete Language
The following material should be read at least three times.  The points here, if applied, will strengthen your writing.
 
In Gulliver's Travels, by Jonathan Swift, Gulliver takes a voyage to Laputa.  While in this country, he visits a school and learns the professors are recommending the abolition of language.  The professors say, "Since words are only names for things, it would be more convenient for all men to carry about with them such things as were necessary to express the particular business they are to discourse on."
 
In some ways, this plan would reduce misunderstandings during dialogue, but communication would be severely limited.  Suppose you had a "thing bag" and in it you place each day all the items upon which you wished to discourse.  Could you say much to a friend?  If you wished to say, "knife," you would take a knife from your bag and point to the knife.  If you wished to say, "pencil," you would take a pencil from your pack and point to the pencil.  This would work only partially, however, because our ideas are much greater than anything we could carry in our "thing bag."
 
Superior to the Laputian professor's plan, we practice a communication system built on the use of symbols.
 
A symbol is anything that stands for something else.  
           
The word "pencil" is a symbol.  The thing the symbol points to, that is the actual pencil, is the referent.
 
The referent is the thing to which the symbol points.
 
Symbols include more than you might first imagine.  For example, a symbol may be a picture, a printed word, a sound or a group of sounds, a gesture like a smile or wave of the hand, a physical item (like a wedding ring), a notation from math, chemistry, music, or electronics, a color, a map.
 
Abstract and Concrete Symbols
 
A symbol may be abstract or concrete.  There are two ways a word may be abstract.  First, a word is abstract if it has many referents.  Second, a word is abstract if it has no referent.  Both examples will be shown.  Before reading this explanation, notice the words below.  The word, "human," is abstract because it has millions of referents.  The word, "male," has fewer referents and is, therefore, not as abstract as "human."  The word, "Englishman," is not as abstract as "male."  The additional words, "artist and musician," are increasingly less abstract.  Finally, "Elton John" is the least abstract word of all.  It is, in fact, concrete.
 
human
male
Englishman
artist
musician
Elton John
 
We can now draw some conclusions:
 
1.  The words on the top of the list are the most abstract; the words at the bottom are the most concrete.
 
2.  The word "musician" is concrete compared to "human" but "musician" is abstract compared to "Elton John."  So the consideration as to whether a word is abstract or concrete is relative.
 
3.  The more referents a particular word has, the more abstract that word is; the fewer referents a  particular word has, the more concrete that word is.
 
4.  The more abstract a word or sentence is, the more confusion it will cause for the reader.  The more concrete a word or sentence is, the less confusion it will cause for the reader.
 
5.  Concrete language produces clarity.  
 
To help understand points 4 and 5, consider the following sentences:
 
"When will you pick me up for the movie?"
"Oh, whenever."
 
The answer to this question is abstract and, therefore,  promotes confusion.
 
"When will you pick me up for the movie?"
"At 6:50 p.m."
 
The answer to this question is concrete and therefore promotes clarity. Observe this same principle in the next examples:
 
"What was the class lecture about today?"
"It dealt with the Civil War."
 
"What was the class lecture about today?"
"It dealt with three economic causes of the Civil War."
 
The second example is again more concrete and therefore more clear.
 
A second way a word may be abstract is if the word has no referent.  Words like hate, love, freedom, and beauty have no concrete referents and are, therefore, abstract.
 
 
Review
 
1.  At this point you should be able to define the words symbol, referent, concrete and abstract.
 
2.  You should know two ways that a word may be abstract.
 
3.  Additionally, you should know a principle that applies to all symbolic communication, written, verbal, or nonverbal.
 
4.  Finally, note that the concept of abstract - concrete language applies not only to simple words but to complete thoughts, that is, to sentences.  You have been shown examples of both abstract and concrete words and sentences.  Be able to state from memory examples of each.
 
Maps and Territories:  an Analogy
 
The idea of maps and territories provides a useful analogy to aid in understanding symbols and referents, abstract and concrete language.
 
The map is to the territory what the symbol is to the referent.
 
Although the map represents any symbol, for now consider the map to be a verbal statement.  If you said, "I have a stomachache," the statement would be the map and the pain in your stomach would be the territory.  If you said, "I love you," the verbal statement would be the map and the actual feeling of love would be the territory.  Also, if you said, "My car is a Pontiac," the verbal statement would be the map.  Your actual car, the Pontiac, would be the territory.

Here are some conclusions:
 
1.   It is a poor verbal map because it is too abstract, too general.
 
2.   No one could follow this ambiguous map.
 
3.   Any map maker has a choice.  He or she can draw accurate or inaccurate maps.
 
4.   Map readers prefer clear, accurate maps.
 
5.   An accurate map is one that truly represents the territory.
 
6.   A good map must have a certain amount of appropriate details.                                
 
Remember again -- the more abstract, the more confusion;  the more concrete, the more clarity.
 
With the analogy of maps and territories, remember that any symbol is a map.  A gesture is a map.  It may be a good or poor map.  A sentence is a map.  It may be a good or poor map.  Again, a good map is one that accurately and clearly describes (symbolizes) the territory.
 
There will always be a loss of meaning between the territory and the map, yet we can all improve our communication by making better, more accurate, complete maps.
 
Using Abstract and Concrete Language
 
Understanding some theory, you are now ready for some application.  We will consider two accurate, well-drawn maps.  In the following discussion, "abstract" will also mean "general" and "concrete" will also mean "specific."  When we write, we must learn to use both abstract and concrete levels of language to our advantage.  It is proper when writing to often begin with a generality (an abstraction), and then explain that generality with specific, concrete details.  Here are two examples by George Orwell:
 
In a job like that you see the dirty work of the empire at close quarters.  The wretched   prisoners huddling in the stinking cages of the lock-ups, the grey, cowed faces of the   long term convicts, the scarred buttocks of the men who had been flogged with bamboo.
 
The term, dirty work of the empire, is a generality.  Orwell then supports his generalization with specific details:  stinking cages, grey cowed faces, scarred buttocks, wretched prisoners huddling, flogged with bamboo.  Here is the second example.  Underline the generality and circle the specific details.
 
Evidently there was something that the children ought not to have seen.  I rounded the hut  and saw a man's body sprawling in the mud.  He was an Indian, a black Dravidian coolie, almost naked, and he could not have been dead many minutes.  The people said that the elephant had come suddenly upon him round the corner of the hut, caught him with its trunk, put its foot on his  back and ground him into the earth.  This was the rainy season and the ground was soft, and his face had scored a trench a foot deep and a couple of  yards long.  He was lying on his belly with arms crucified and head sharply twisted to one side.  His face was coated with mud, the eyes wide open, the teeth bared and grinning with an expression of unendurable agony.
 
Image
 
At this point, another definition is needed.  The word is "image."  As related to writing, an image is a word picture that appeals to the senses.  There are five senses:  sight, taste, smell, touch, and hearing.  Language which appeals to the senses will be concrete.  Concrete details lead to effective communication and writing.  Concrete language allows us to see, feel, hear, smell, or taste the thing being described.  
 
Here are a few examples of words which appeal to our various senses.
 
Sight: any action, size, or color; words like run, large, blue
 
Taste: salty, bitter, sweet
 
Touch: rough, cold, sharp
 
Hearing: crash, whisper, clank
 
Good Description Includes Images
 
Analyze one more example by George Orwell.  Copy down any generalization and copy down every sensory detail.  You will see that one of Orwell's powers is his use of numerous images, that is, numerous word pictures that appeal to our senses.
 
..a mysterious, terrible change had come over the elephant.  He neither stirred nor fell, but every line of his body had altered.  He looked suddenly stricken, shrunken, immensely old, as though the frightful impact of the bullet had paralyzed him without knocking him down.  At last, after what seemed a long time--it might have been five seconds, I dare say-- he sagged flabbily to his knees.  His mouth slobbered.
 
An enormous senility seemed to have settled upon him.  One could have imagined him thousands of years old.  I fired again into the same spot.  At the second shot he did not collapse but climbed with desperate slowness to his feet and stood weakly upright, with legs sagging and head dropping.  I fired a third time--- but in falling he seemed for a moment to rise, for as his hind legs collapsed beneath him he seemed to tower upwards like a huge rock toppling, his trunk reaching skyward like a tree.  He trumpeted for the first and only time.  And then down he came, his belly towards me, with a crash that seemed to shake the ground even where I lay.
 
Clear Images Produce Clear Writing
 
Although clarity in writing involves virtues in addition to concrete images, particularly for descriptive writing, we need words that appeal to the senses.  We need sensory language. After carefully reading a section of descriptive prose, ask yourself, "Can I picture the thing being described?"  If you use your imagination, you surely can picture the thing Orwell described.  You have circled the words that have helped make that description clear.
