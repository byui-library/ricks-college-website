Session H4

Who Writes Those Placement Recommendations Anyway?

Facilitator:  
John Swiney, University of Washington

Recorder Name:  
Kimberly Helms, University of Washington

Presenter:  
Christine Kerlin, Everett Community College

This session focused on the National Council on the Evaluation of Foreign Educational Credentials (the Council) methodology for the evaluation of foreign educational credentials.  A new methodology to replace the longstanding "placement recommendations" is being developed by the council due to several limitations of the current practice.  Limitations the council has identified are: placement recommendations have the image of being mandates rather than suggestions; uses of placement recommendations apply them in isolation without consideration of specific institutional policies; and the rationale behind placement evaluations is not transparent to credential evaluations.

The new methodology is comprised of three steps: 

  1. List key characteristics of the benchmark credential at your institution 

  2. Determine characteristics to form a profile of the foreign credentials evaluated

  3. Determine if there is an acceptable level of comparability between your institution and the foreign credential.

The session offered a hands-on exercise using a foreign transcript to visualize the new methodology in practice, as well as informational handouts that the attendees take back to their institutions.

Introduction

Six years ago, Arizona State University embarked on a reengineering project in an effort to examine and improve those processes by which we deliver services to our students. Since 1993, several of those processes have improved  - many due to a delivery model we call General Services.  Last year at this conference, we shared information about the origin of general services and took you through the process of developing and implementing a general services model.

One year later, we find general services a thriving, highly effective tool to meet student needs. We have also found it to be even more – an invaluable resource in a time of enrollment growth and high demand for process efficiency.

ASU Institutional Introduction

Arizona State University is comprised of three campuses, all located in the greater Phoenix metropolitan area. The ASU Main campus is located in Tempe and enrolls over 44,000 undergraduate and graduate students.  ASU West is located 25 miles from the main campus in west Phoenix and enrolls approximately 4,000 upper division and graduate students.   The ASU East campus is located 25 miles east of the main campus in Mesa, and enrolls 1,000 students in selected programs including engineering technology and agribusiness.  Although each campus is distinct in terms of setting, academic offerings, and student profile, ASU is promoted as “one university, geographically distributed”. With a total enrollment for all three campuses surpassing 49,000, ASU is the largest university in the southwest and the fourth largest university in the nation.

ASU is unique in many respects.  Though situated in a large metropolitan area, it is one of just a handful of four-year institutions accessible to a rapidly growing population.  It is also adjacent  to one of the largest community college systems in the nation which provides a large source of students.  These factors contribute to a student body very diverse in terms of age, prior college experience, resident vs. commuter, and socioeconomic status.   

Profile of ASU Main undergraduates:

  • Less than half (44%) of main campus students live in Tempe.

General Services Development Overview

In October of 1995, the ASU Student Process Coordinating Team recommended  two pilot projects for development, Cross College Advising Services for Freshmen (CASF) and General Services, where express services to students are provided from strategic locations throughout the university. 

Following the recommendations of the SPRP Coordinating Team and approval of the project sponsors, an eleven member General Services Development Team was created and convened in November of 1995 to begin the task of making general services a reality.   Departments represented on the team included the Registrar, Student Financial Assistance, Student Business Services, Undergraduate Admissions, and the College of Engineering and Applied Sciences.

The General Services Development Team set about to accomplish the following:

  • Determine potential general service delivery sites,

  • Determine a budget for “start up” costs,

  • Determine other resource needs including technology support and staffing,

  • Compile general services information in a useful and easily accessible format.

Over time, two models for general services deliveries were developed.

  •  First, two general services sites were created – one adjacent to a registrar site, the other within and academic college (Engineering & Applied Sciences).  At each of these sites, students could access information about admission, registration, fee payment, and financial aid – and actually complete many transactions related to those services.  Eventually, the Engineering College site closed due to its relative inaccessibility to the greater number of students, and another opened in the College of Liberal Arts and Sciences, a more accessible location.

  • Second, general services staff, numbering three in the beginning, set about training front line staff in offices across the university.  These include both academic and student service offices. In this way, students could access information on a variety of services in multiple locations, thus minimizing the need to go from office to office.
