Remembering Faces AND Names

We have all been there before. We meet new people - at school, w almost every day. Wouldn’t it be pleasant if we could remember all of their Remembering names is not just a courtesy - it is a necessity. Names are personal! Failing to remember someone’s name often results in embarrassing apologetic However, when we greet someone by their name, we make them feel important, which encourages good feelings. We would surely make many more friends if we could recall

So, how can we learn to remember names more easily? Try these three basic and face recall:

 

#1. Pay Attention - get the name

 

The biggest reason we forget things is that we never learned them in the first place. Memory comes in three parts: input, storage, and output. Often, the output seems to be the toughest part. However, it is really the input we should work on. If we can learn to focus all our attention on whatever we are doing - “be here now” - then we will better retain important information. When we genuinely listen to a person s name with the intention to remember it, we have taken the first step in storing that name.

We must try to maximize the degree of the first impression made by the name. Right after hearing the name, we should repeat it to the person. This tests whether or not we heard it clearly and correctly. It is important that we heard it right; we do not want to store the wrong name. Also, when we use the name in casual conversation, it stores better since we are repeating and rehearsing.

Some examples of verbalizing the name just after hearing it are:

“Nice to meet you, Jeff.” “Thank you for coming, Kirstin.” “How are you doing, Jenny?” “I’m glad you could be here, Mr. Russell.”

 

#2. Notice Facial Features - get the face

 

Our memories have an amazing ability to remember visual things: pictures, movies, and things we did or places we went. Remembering something we saw is easier than remembering something we heard. It is not as easy to remember words or sounds. Also, we can more easily remember unusual things than usual ones. For example, if we were in a big city, and many people walked past us every day, remembering a particular person would be hard. But what if that person had been wearing a clown outfit when they walked by? We are sure to remember them because they were unusual. Those are the keys to easily remembering anything: make it visual, and make it unusual.

When we meet a person, we should take a moment and concentrate on their face and notice their facial features. We must take a mental picture of anything that stands out, anything unusual, or anything remarkable. Focus on any particular feature that jumps out: a big nose, curly or red hair, a wink, a laugh, a frown, or a nervous twitch. Then, we must use our imagination to exaggerate this feature. We can make it look even more unusual in our mental picture. This will allow us to recall what they looked like. It is also an important part of the next step, keywording.

 

#3. Keywording - store the two items together

 

Keywording is:

- giving visual qualities to words that do not naturally have them on their own.

- choosing words that rhyme with or sound like the word or name we want to remember.

Once we have paid attention to the name and made a mental picture of the face, we could do as many do: repeat the name over and over all day. However, repetition of names is like writing them in the sand; in time, new sand covers the old. With repetition, the name remains visible in our memory for only one day, or perhaps a week. As a result, if we don’t see that person’s smiling face for a few months, we will be in trouble when the time comes to recall their name. So, what if we could create an unusual picture to associate the name with the face? Since forgetting pictures is hard, creating a visual image is like writing the name with permanent ink.

First, we should make a connection between the name and something visual or auditory. If the name is Smith, we can picture a large blacksmith hammering away at a red-hot piece of metal on an anvil. If the name is Doug Wheeler, we can picture a dog (Doug) behind a steering wheel. If the name is Gene, we can picture a pair of blue jeans. Next, we need to associate that picture with the person’s face. Mr. Smith is the blacksmith; Doug drives the truck in which the dog sits; a pair of blue jeans hangs from Gene’s large nose. So, now we are visualizing the face (with that unusual feature) and something to represent the name in the same picture. This picture should be easy to recall whenever we see that person’s face.

Some examples of keywords are:

lace for Alice,                        ant for Ann,                        barbed wire for Barbara,                        chain for Jane,
lizard for Liz,                        helmet for Helen,                        coral, curl for Carl, Carol,                        cherry for Jerry,
judo for Judy;                        uncle Sam for Sam,                        blue jeans for Gene, Jean,                        corn for Karen,
duck bill for Bill,                        bruise for Bruce,                        merry-go-round for Mary,                        dive for David,
deck for Dick,                        gorge for George,                        bobcat, bobsled for Bob,                        pole for Paul,

jam for James

 

When we are creating a keyword, it is important to remember that the keyword must be effective for us. If “judo” does not help us remember the name Judy, then we must choose something else to picture. Also, it may help to picture an action or emotion in your visualization. Make Gene laughing, or Mr. Smith pounding on the anvil.

The power of visual imagery is one reason we easily remember faces and not names. Visual impressions remain much longer than verbal ones. Therefore, if we can create an unusual picture representing someone’s name to go with their face, we will remember it better.

 

Summary

 

Memorizing people’s names is important, but often hard to do. The problem may be that we never stored the name in the first place. We need to focus on remembering the name and pay attention to what the name is the first time we hear it. It is a good idea to repeat the name and use it regularly in conversation. After meeting someone new, we should take time to concentrate on their face and note any unusual or remarkable features. We will more easily remember these because storing and retrieving visual images is simple; remembering unusual images is even easier. We should create a mental picture of the new person (and their unusual features) and associate it with a keyword - a picture for their name. Keywords are visual representations of names, and they should mean something to us. In this way, we can store an unusual picture of the person and their name in our memory.

Finally, we should take the time to actively retrieve and rehearse the newly learned name. Before going to bed, we should picture again all the new people we met that day and their keywords. We should then review these the following day, again in a week, and once more in a month. All this combined can make us a master at remembering faces and names.
