Russians unveil space shuttle for tourists

by Allison Jenks
REE00013@BYUI.EDU
Scroll Staff

The demand for national security through an accurate, technology-rich identification card has intensified since the Sept. 11 attacks.

Similar identification cards are being used all over the world, and Americans may soon be required to follow the example of other countries who have already implemented computerized identification cards.

The national security cards would probably be linked to a network of computers, including those in every government agency.

“The card will confirm who you are,” Brian Jenkins, a security specialist for the Rand Corporation, said. “And it can be connected with other databases to indicate whether or not you are a fugitive.”

One example of an identity security breach is the hijackers who crashed American Airlines Flight 77 into the Pentagon. These hijackers had Virginia identification cards even though they didn’t live in Virginia, according to ABC News.

There are some issues with this advancement in identification technology. Some worry that the computerized identification cards would be too intrusive into private life — freedoms will be violated as a precaution.

Some say it is an illusion of security.

“It’s a chaotic system ... We carry many forms of identity cards,” Jenkins said.

“You can imagine if you go to the airport to check in. Does the security at the airport terminal get access to your FBI files, your employment records, your medical information, your financial information, your children’s names and addresses? Where would the line be drawn?” Marc Rotenberg, privacy activist and the executive director of the Electronic Privacy Information Center (EPIC), said.

The most recent addition to the computerized identification users is Hong Kong.

Hong Kong residents are about to be issued a new identity card, according to The Associated Press. The cards will be cardboard identity cards to sort out the newcomers from the residents in Hong Kong. The cards will be embedded with computer chips that hold names, pictures, gender, residential status, conditions of stay for non-citizens, birth dates and a digital template of both thumb prints. Hong Kong’s government declined to have the cards hold health and bank records, according to The Associated Press.

Hong Kong officials estimate that the seven-year plan to distribute the mandatory cards to residents ages 11 and older will cost $400 million, according to CNN.

In September, Malaysia introduced an optional smart card called MyKad that functions as a driver’s license and contains passport information, according to CNN.

Japan plans to introduce an optional ID containing the cardholder’s signature, photo and address, according to The Associated Press.

Thailand recently approved a proposal for mandatory cards containing social security and health records, according to The Associated Press.

Finland has implemented an optional smart ID card, according to CNN.

BYU-I students carry identification cards that hold an account number, which is that particular student’s social security number. That account number corresponds to scholastic information, financial accounts, and addresses.
