The Energy Future
of a Self-sufficient America
by Mark Herbst
 

Abstract

America is not a self-sufficient country because it has to rely on petroleum producing nations for its energy consumption needs. As the current supply of oil decreases in the next 30 years, America will have to turn to alternative energy source. Unless a new pollution-free source is found, America will have to choose between coal and nuclear power. In a close comparison, nuclear power proves to be the most efficient and safe.   It produces less waste and is managed in a safer manner than that of coal. Thus, as America turns inward for an energy source, she will likely choose nuclear power.

Nuclear Power

This past summer the nation was brought to attention by an oil shortage that caused gasoline prices to skyrocket. I recall going to the gas pumps each week and seeing the price per gallon rise ten cents a visit. I thought the price of gas to be ridiculous, but yet I never saw us Americans stop purchasing the much-needed fuel. Instead, I saw a nation whine and complain about the problem, but not really face it.

Even our leaders didn’t want to solve the real problem of our country. They turned to the oil cartels, got on their knees, and begged OPEC to increase their oil production. This sight sickened me. It made me feel we were not in control of our own country. Our leaders really illustrated that fact well when they revealed our dependency on the mercy of the oil cartel.

It is time for America to become autonomous: no more turning to others and relying on them for our survival. We are a big country now and can take care of ourselves, and that means solving our own problems. Our problem is that we rely on the oil producing nations so much that we can no longer survive with out them. The solution to this problem is simple. It just requires us to accept it, face it, and just do it. We need to become more energy self-sufficient and one way to achieve that goal is to cut our need for petroleum. We do this by starting to phase out our petroleum uses and maximizing our internal energy sources.

In order to phase out petroleum, we would first start with the elimination of minor uses like electric utility and gradually move to the major uses like gasoline and diesel engines over the next twenty years. The May 2000 issue of Poplar Science reported that at our current level of petroleum consumption the world would reach its production climax of 85 million barrels per day by 2020. Then this would decline rapidly and 2030, where petroleum would be produced at a scarce 20 millions barrels per day. That isn’t going to go far when the world consumes more than 72 million barrels a day. So, sooner or later we are going to have to find a new energy source. I want to see America solve the problems of tomorrow today instead of waiting for tomorrow and solving the problems of yesterday while in a panic.

So we begin today by maximizing our internal energy sources.  Our main energy sources are coal, nuclear, hydroelectric, and natural gas. To maximize anything, we have to eliminate what is limited. And since we really can’t make anymore water or natural gas, we can rule out hydroelectric and natural gas, which leaves us with coal and nuclear power. Thus, we are down to the root of our problem, which energy source do we rely on for the future, coal or nuclear power?

The decisive factor between coal and nuclear energy is the relationship of safety and efficiency (energy produced vs. expense). Safety is not only for us citizens, but for the environment as well. We need to choose the one that will give us the most for our dollar and be the soundest method as well. After a study of the data and facts of the waste produced by both nuclear and coal power plants and after an analytical conversation with a nuclear engineer, I have concluded that nuclear power is the lesser of the two evils. Unless a waste free energy source is found, nuclear power is the future of a self-sufficient United Sates of America.

When the data of waste produced between nuclear and coal energy production was compared, it was shown that coal produced a greater volume of hazardous material. In one year, a 1000-megawatt nuclear plant consumes only 27 metric tons (1000 kg) of Uranium for fuel. It then takes that fuel, generates its 1000 megawatts of power, and annually produces 600 metric tons of waste, which occupies a volume of 63 cubic meters. On the other hand, a 1000-megawatt coal plant burns a whapping 26,000,000 metric tons of coal in just one single year and produces 320,000 metric tons of ash.

At a closer comparison of the two wastes it was also found that the byproducts of coal equaled, if not surpassed, the harmful effects of nuclear waste. Of the 600 metric tons of nuclear waste, 400 is classified as low level waste. I asked Alan Herbst, a nuclear engineer at the Idaho National Engineering and Environmental Laboratory, just how harmful low level waste was. He responded by telling me that it was indeed very low. He said that someone could walk around it and even hold it in his hand and no damage would be done to them. The only hazard with low level waste is long term exposure.

A person would have to be exposed by three feet or closer to it for a period of 20 or more hours to receive a significant amount of radiation dose. The remaining 200 metric tons of nuclear waste is intermediate to high level waste. This waste is harmful and must be dealt with extreme caution. This portion of the waste requires deep geological storage for complete isolation. Even though this waste is very harmful, the annual 200 metric tons only occupies a volume of 23 cubic meters.

The safety of the method in which the waste is stored and managed is not in question since it has to pass a strict leach test. The leach test consists of a series of conditions in which the stored waste must undergo. The material the waste is stored in, usually cement or glass, is smashed into fine pieces and then has water run over it.

The water is then tested for radioactivity, which in turn tests the leach resistance of the material or the ability of the stored material to hold the radioactive waste. The leach test provides safety of nuclear waste storage under the conditions of earthquake and underground water contact.

Coal waste, on the other hand, consists of 320,000 metric tons of air and environmental pollutant ash. But even worse, 400 metric tons of heavy metals and 10 metric tons of radioactive material are also produced. The part of concern for coal waste is how the heavy metals, like lead and mercury and the natural radioactive material, are stored and managed.

Coal waste has no comparative leach test to ensure the safety of its storage. Instead, it is often put back and sealed in the caves that the coal was originally mined. That is great until an earthquake causes it to contaminate the environment or it leak onto an underground water supply. Without a leach test or even a method to safely contain the hazardous waste, the environment is susceptible to contamination.

One may argue that it is only materials that are already found in nature. Yes, they are, but not in that concentration. No where in nature will anyone find such a dense area of heavy metals, which at such a high concentration can be very deadly. Without a severe change in the management and storage of coal waste products, it will never be as safe as nuclear waste storage and its strict management regulations and procedures. And as far as I can conclude, the research efforts to reduce coals environmental pollutants are reaching a climax.

With coal as a source of energy only so much waste can be contained. And thus, we are left with the summarizing remarks of Dr. Adam Jostsons on the waste production tests, "The waste quantities and volumes associated with nuclear electricity production are trivial compared to that associated with electricity production from the burning of coal." (Jostsons).

With the conclusion of safety we can now move to the decision of which one is the most cost effective. Coal energy is relatively inexpensive. The mining process in and of itself is easy and cost efficient, along with the burning and power production as well. Coal power would be the way to go in terms of cost, but is it ethical to chose a power source that is less safe and more pollutant simply because it is cheaper?

Coal is mainly cheap because it doesn’t have the restrictions for safety like nuclear power. Then when looking at nuclear power, it is so expensive in the U.S. due to all of its safety regulations. In the U.S. there are two sides of nuclear safety, the reactor side and the waste management side. I believe that the regulations should stay the same on the waste management side, but I think the regulations on reactor side should be streamlined.

When I take a look at France and Japan, both whose power is supplied by over 50% nuclear energy I see a cost effective energy plan. In those countries they don’t have what is called a pedigree for reactor parts and supplies. In the U.S., reactor parts are so expensive because of unneeded specifications and checks. In a pedigree, everything is listed from the origin of the part to all the little trivial components that went into making it. For example if a U.S. reactor needed a new valve, they couldn’t just go out and get the valve like France and Japan.

They would have to get a special valve that has a pedigree listing all the components, all the materials used to make it, along with the specifications of the parts that machined the valve. All this costs money, which inflates the cost of the valve up to three times its original value. All this is unnecessary as proven in Japan and France who haven’t required pedigree on their parts and haven’t had a problem or accident resulting from the absence of a pedigree.

We trust our lives with cars and airplanes, but don’t require pedigrees on all their parts; it is just the same for reactors. Plus reactors already have high safety features that if a part were to malfunction the reactor can be shut down and contained in a matter of seconds. Thus with the reduction of safety regulations on the rector side of nuclear power, it can become a cost effective energy source in the U.S.

Finally, I’ve come to conclude that America needs an energy plan. We are too dependent upon petroleum and need to maximize our internal sources. We can do this and become self-sufficient by maximizing our technology of nuclear power. With all current power sources there are waste products and in America we are left to choose between coal and nuclear energy which are both sources that we can utilize in our own country. In comparison of the two, nuclear energy proved to produce a smaller volume of hazardous waste and with a cut in regulations can also be a sound cost effective source as well.

When it comes down to it, America needs to do two things. First, we need our leaders to recognize the energy problem, decide on a solution for the future, and then begin to implement that solution today. Implementing means designating waste storage facilities and to stop dwelling in red tape. Second, in realizing that nuclear energy is the most probable source of energy for the future, the citizens of America need to be educated in the safety of nuclear power so that the fears of nuclear energy can be put to rest. If these two things happen, I am confident that America will be self-sufficient for their own energy and thus continue to be prosperous and at the head of the world’s leading nations.
