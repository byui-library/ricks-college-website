Elder Boyd K. Packer has observed that the root word for ordinance is order. He notes that "The Oxford English Dictionary (Oxford, England, 1970) gives as the first definition of the word order, "arrangement in ranks or rows," and as the second definition, "arrangement in sequence or proper relative position." At first glance that may not strike a person as having much religious significance, but indeed it has.



Among the ordinances we perform in the Church are these: baptism, sacrament, naming and blessing of infants, administering to the sick, setting apart to callings in the Church, ordaining to offices. In addition there are higher ordinances, performed in the temples. These include washings, anointings, the endowment, and the sealing ordinance, spoken of generally as temple marriage.The word ordinance comes from the word order, which means, "a rank, a row, a series." The word order appears frequently in the scriptures. Some examples are: ". . . established the order of the Church" (Alma 8:1);" . . . all things should be restored to their proper order" (Alma 41:2);" . . . all things may be done in order" (D&C 20:68); "mine house is a house of order" (D&C 132:8). Mormon even defined depravity as being "without order" (Moroni 9:18).

The word ordain, a close relative to the other two words, has, as its first definition, "to put in order, arrange, make ready, prepare"; also, "to appoint or admit to the ministry of the Christian church. . . by the laying on of hands or other symbolic action."From all of this dictionary work there comes the impression that an ordinance, to be valid, must be done in proper order.



Order-To put in ranks or rows, in proper sequence or relationship.

Ordain-The process of putting things in rows of proper relationship.

Ordinance-The ceremony by which things are put in proper order.



Jacob's ladder. Jacob dreamed a dream in which he saw a ladder which joined heaven and earth which angels ascended and descended. The Lord standing above it renewed with Jacob the Abrahamic covenant. Jacob named the place of his dream Bethel, or house of God (Gen. 28:12-14, 19). Joseph Smith said, "Paul ascended into the third heavens, and he could understand the three principal rounds of Jacob's ladder-the telestial, the terrestrial, and the celestial glories or kingdoms" (Smith, Joseph. Teachings of the Prophet Joseph Smith. Joseph F. Smith, comp. Salt Lake City: Deseret Book Co., 1976., pp. 304-5).

The idea that certain places have been designated for holy purposes, and as such are known long in advance, is common to Latter-day Saints, who look for a day when the New Jerusalem will be established in Jackson County, Missouri. Along with123Sinai and Moriah, Bethel ("the house of God") appears to be another such place. It will be remembered that as Jacob was traveling from his father's house at Beersheba to seek a wife in Haran, the Lord appeared to him by night in a marvelous dream. Jacob rose up early the following morning and took the stone that had been his pillow and "set it up for a pillar, and poured oil upon the top of it. And he called the name of that place Beth-el: but the name of that city was called Luz at the first." (Genesis 28:10-22.) Years later, upon Jacob's return, God appeared unto him again at the same place and once more we read that "Jacob set up a pillar in the place where he talked with him, even a pillar of stone: and he poured a drink offering thereon, and he poured oil thereon. And Jacob called the name of the place where God spake with him, Beth-el." (Genesis 35:14-15.) Yet, if we accept the precise definition of Genesis 12:8, the name Bethel existed at this spot even before the arrival of Abram in Canaan. In future times it would be to Bethel (or as it is rendered, "the house of God") that Israel would go in times of distress to ask counsel of the Lord (Judges 20:18, 26, 31; 21:2).

In his dream Jacob saw a ladder reaching from earth to heaven, with angels ascending and descending on it. Above the ladder stood the Lord, who (and we must assume that Jacob ascended the ladder) covenanted with Jacob, as he had with Abraham and Isaac, to bless him and his posterity throughout all generations. He was told, as had been his fathers, that through his seed all the families of the earth would be blessed. Joseph Smith tells us that the "three principal rounds of Jacob's ladder" were the same ascended by Paul (2 Corinthians 12:2) and that they represented progression from telestial to terrestrial, and from terrestrial to celestial degrees of glory (Smith, Joseph. History of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. Salt Lake City: Deseret Book Co., 1927 (published by the Church). 5:402). When he awoke Jacob designated the place as "the house of God," and as "the gate of heaven" (Genesis 28:12-17). Describing the second appearance of God to him at Bethel, Jacob mentions the emphasis given to his new name (Israel), the command that he multiply and replenish the earth, and the promises associated with his posterity (Genesis 35:7-12).
 
 

The Endowment of Israel- Jeffrey R. Chadwick

When the Prophet Moses wrote the Book of Genesis he seems to have constructed certain passages in such a way that only the spiritually enlightened would completely understand them. This appears to be the case with Genesis 28, the story of Jacob's vision at Bethel. When fully understood, this account is relating the endowment of Jacob, whom we also know by the name of Israel. As Jacob travelled north from Beersheba to Haran, he stopped to spend the night at a "certain place"-- a high, rocky hilltop just east of the Canaanite settlement of Luz. This"certain place" was the same hill, east of Luz and west of the ruin called Hai or Ai, where Jacob's grandfather Abraham had built an alter and received from the Lord an assurance of the Abrahamic Covenant concerning the growth and mission of his posterity and their connection to the land of Israel (see Genesis 12:8, 13:3-17). Before leaving Beersheba, Jacob had been given those same blessings, the blessings of the Abrahamic Covenant, by his father Isaac (see Genesis 28:1-4). Now, at the top of that rocky hill-- that "certain place"-- the Lord repeated the blessings that covenant to Jacob as a part of a transcending spiritual experience during the night (see Genesis (28:11-15).

As a part of at great experience, which most English Bible translations indicate was a dream (more properly, a vision, cf. 1 Nephi 8:2), Jacob saw a ladder reaching from earth to heaven, with angels of God ascending and descending on it (see Genesis 28:12). Of this event, President Marion G. Romney, teaching the general membership of the Church, said " . . . Jacob realized that the covenants he made with the Lord there were rungs on the ladder that he himself would have to climb in order to obtain the promised blessings-- blessings that would entitle him to enter heaven and associate with the Lord." President Romney concluded that" . . . "Temples are to us all what Bethel was to Jacob" ("Temples-- The Gates to Heaven," Ensign, March 1971, p.16.)

President Romney's conclusion may have a more literal meaning than generally supposed. For those members of the Church who have been to the Temple and received the holy endowment, verse 12 of Genesis 28 is highly significant when translated literally from the original Hebrew. The Hebrew word sulam, rendered as ladder, would be more properly understood as a stairway, and the Hebrew term malakay elohim, usually translated as "angels of God," literally means messengers of God. To the endowed Latter-day Saint, the scene of messengers going up and down a stairway from heaven to earth and back should be quite familiar. In addition to these insights, further understanding of Jacob's experience is gained from the Prophet Joseph Smith, who taught that ". . . the three principle rounds of Jacob's ladder (are) the telestial, the terrestrial, and the celestial glories or kingdoms" (Teachings of the Prophet Joseph Smith, p.305).

Knowing all this, what regular Temple visitor could fail to recognize what happened to father Jacob on that rocky hilltop, as he 1) made and received covenants and 2) learned of the three degrees of glory, while 3) messengers came down from and returned to heaven! As a part of the covenant process, Jacob made promises to the Lord which included his oath to pay tithing (see Genesis 28:20-22). But other promises made and received at that "certain place" were not recorded by the prophetic writer for his mosaic-dispensation readers. When morning arrived, Jacob called the site "Bethel", which in Hebrew (beyt-el) means house of God, a direct reference to the Temple experience he had there (see Genesis 28:17-19). Of course, one is cautioned in the Temple to treat the things given there with the utmost reverance and respect, and it is improper to discuss certain parts of the ceremony outside those sacred walls. The comments here are necessarily limited to a retranslation of Genesis 28:12, and to a statement of the Prophet Joseph Smith. To suggest even more detail to Jacob's marvelous experience at Bethel might violate some most significant vows. It is important, in this connection, to remember the words of President Harold B. Lee: "When I meet with our missionaries and they ask questions about things pertaining to the Temple, I say to them, as I close the discussion, 'I don't dare answer any of your questions unless I can find an answer in the standard works or in the authentic declarations of the presidents of the Church'" (Ensign, December 1972, p.3). Still, from a Hebrew reading of Genesis 28, President Romney's statement carries a most significant and literal meaning-- "Temples are to us all what Bethel was to Jacob."

Notes: 1. Only part of what Latter-day Saints call the "endowment" occurred in the Genesis 28 account. As in the days of Joseph Smith, when the ordinances of the Temple came forth over time, the final portion and transcendent climax of Jacob's endowment seems to have occurred in Genesis 32:24-32, which reports Jacob's interview at Peniel, his new and symbolic name "Israel," and his exclamation that he had seen God face to face. 2. The translation of the Hebrew word malak as "messenger" may be found in any lexicon of Biblical Hebrew-- malakay ("messengers") being the plural construct form-- and the rendering of the Hebrew word sulam (root sll) as "stairway" is suggested by E. A. Speiser in The Anchor Bible-- Genesis, gen. eds. W. F. Albright and David Noel Freedman (Garden City, New York: Doubleday & Co. Inc., 1982), pp.217-218. 3. The Canaanite settlement named Luz, west of the "certain place," was also later called "Bethel," but not until several centuries after Jacob, when Israelites captured the city (see Joshua 12:9 and Judges 1:22-23). It is clear from a careful reading of the Genesis account that the spiritual experiences of both Abraham and Jacob took place not in the city, but on the uninhabited, rocky hilltop to the east (see Genesis 28:19).
