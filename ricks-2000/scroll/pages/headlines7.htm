BUSH'S PLAN FOCUSES ON NEW ENERGY SOURCES

President Bush is shown the control room by technician Chad Bednor, left, and Anders Rydaker, center, president of District Power, as the president tours District Energy in St. Paul, Minn., May 17. President Bush called the country's energy shortages the most serious since the 1970s and unveiled a plan aimed at boosting supplies of oil, gas and nuclear power. AP photo archive

by Andrea Scott
scroll staff


President George W. Bush outlined an energy plan May 17 that attacks what the White House is calling a national "energy crisis." The plan calls for a strong push for increasing energy production and more domestic oil drilling as well as the construction of new coal-fired and nuclear power plans.

Speaking from St. Paul, Minn., Bush addressed the need for new sources of energy, ones that are renewable and non-polluting. He mentioned the recent rise of energy costs, including gasoline and natural gas prices.

"If we fail to act, this country could face a darker future," Bush said in the address.

The measure, which is already facing a pitched battle in Congress, calls on federal agencies to take dramatic steps to reduce regulations on the energy industry to encourage more output from coal-fired plants, recommends the construction of more than 1,300 new power plants and calls for new oil and gas exploration, according to CNN - including some on federal lands that environmentalists and many in Congress believe should be off-limits.

"My administration has developed a sane national plan to meet our energy needs this year and every year," Bush said in what the White House billed as a major address on energy policy. He promised a plan "that faces up to our energy challenges and meets them."

The president said the plan consisted of more than 100 recommendations "that light the way to a brighter future" by addressing both demand and supply.

"I'm deeply concerned about the impact of rolling blackouts in California," Bush said. The future of the United States, he said, will be threatened if nothing is done to seek solutions to the nation's energy crunch.

Bush's plan calls for:
· Executive orders and agency reviews aimed at easing regulations the industry says slow the sifting and licensing of power plants and gas refineries.
· A review of the Clinton administration's interpretation of "new source review rules" that the coal industry and refiners say discourage them from making technological and other improvements because they run the risk of tougher environmental rules.
· Opening parts of the Arctic National Wildlife Refuge to oil and gas exploration, and encouraging the Interior and other departments to look at opening other federal lands now off-limits to energy exploration.
· Approximately 38,000 miles of new natural gas pipelines.
· New authority for the government to seize land for new electricity transmission lines.
