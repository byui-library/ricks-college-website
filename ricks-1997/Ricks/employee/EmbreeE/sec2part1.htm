Organization Theory:

Perspectives on Communication

from Communication in Organizations 2nd Ed. Dalmer Fisher  West Publishing

Learning Objectives After studying this chapter, the reader should be able to:

  • Explain how to view an organization from four different perspectives based on organization theory.
  • Discuss the elements that are unique to each perspective.
  • Understand the communication implications of each perspective.

Metropolitan Community Health Centers

Dr. Maria Sanchez, a physician with Metropolitan Community Health Centers (MCHC), a health maintenance organization in a major city, felt excited but worried about her new assignment as director of Rockland Park, one of MCHC's seven neighborhood locations. Hers would be a newly created position.. In a recent memorandum, MCHC's executive director announced her appointment as "an experiment to test this new position in our structureas a way of improving coordination between the specialties." Reporting to Dr. Sanchez would be Rockland Park's physicians and nurse practitioners, each of whom worked in one of MCHC's nine medical specialties: internal medicine, pediatrics, surgery, orthopedics, radiology, optometry, orthopedics, pathology, or ear, nose, and throat.

MCHC began ten years earlier as a single downtown clinic. The physicians and nurse practitioners each reported to the chief of service who headed their specialty. As the various neighborhood centers opened one by one during the years that followed, the chiefs of service remained in charge of their specialties. Thus, each physician and nurse practitioner in a neighborhood center reported to the chief of his or her specialty, who was located at the downtown center.

Until now, Dr. Sanchez had been associate chief of the orthopedic service. She wondered how her new subordinates, the various specialists at Rockland Park, would receive her. She was also concerned about her chances of forming effective working relationships with the chiefs of service, who were accustomed to their direct? sole, supervision over the personnel in their respective specialties. Comments about the new arrangement varied. Three Rockland Park physicians remarked as follows:

"We're going to have an orthopod as our new director? What will one of those mechanics know about our problems in medicine? Can a memorandum from the executive director really change the way we work? It's going to be chaos if we can't continue working directly with our service chiefs."

"I think the change makes sense. A neighborhood center should be run by its own director. A center shouldn't have nine bosses as we do now."

"They've made this change on paper, but what I want to know is how is it going to be handled by Sanchez and by the service chiefs? Are they going to ask for our input, or just tell us how it is?"

Three of the service chiefs added these comments:

"I think the reporting relationships should be more clearly spelled out. If service chiefs can still contact the physicians in their specialties, it should be clear when, about what, and under what circumstances."

"However the thing is set up officially, it's going to have to work itself out over time. Different service chiefs will want to handle things differently."

"If a neighborhood center director is what they want, that's what they shall have. I will no longer communicate with my people at Rockland Park. I will send all my directions and information to Sanchez."


 

Introduction

The wide variety of opinions about the experimental change at MCHC shows how people differ in their views of how organizations should function. Not all at MCHC agree that a change should be made, and those who do agree differ in their views of how the organization should implement it. We can trace their disagreement, at least in part, to the differing perspectives from which people view organizations.

In this chapter, we will examine four major perspectives, each based on a body of organization theory: the classical perspective, the human perspective, and two groups of viewpoints known as integrative perspectives and contemporary perspectives. Each perspective encompasses a distinctive line of questioning about organizations. Classical theorists question organization structure, exploring issues such as the ideal design for an organization, how an organization should exercise control through boss-subordinate reporting relationships, and the manner in which the entity could best group its personnel into specialized units. Researchers taking the human perspective focus on people, asking how people are motivated at work, how interpersonal relationships affect performance, and what part group process plays in task effectiveness.

Integrative perspectives combine the classical and human perspectives, and add several more factors to the picture. A theorist observing organizations from an integrative perspective might attempt to determine, for example, the kind of structure, people, and technology that would enable an organization to function best in its particular environment. Finally, researchers from the contemporary perspectives, believing that organizations result from the thoughts and feelings of the people in them, explore matters such as how an organization thinks and learns, and how it can change its culture.

Each of the four theoretical perspectives focuses attention on some, but not all, of what occurs in organizations. Within many organizations, consensus on perspective is rare. People tend to gravitate toward the perspectives that best reflect their personal views and their situations within the structure. At MCHC, the physician who said, "A neighborhood center shouldn't have nine bosses" was speaking from the classical perspective, while the doctor who wondered, "Are they going to ask for our input or just tell us how it is?" seemed to be speaking from the human perspective. These differing viewpoints will likely affect the way these professionals communicate in the HMO.

Though these doctors each represent a single, different perspective, some persons can see organizations from all four perspectives simultaneously. A rare capacity, this fuller vision enables a person to operate more effectively within an organization: to hear and see more, and to communicate more skillfully. By examining in turn the classical, human, integrative, and contemporary perspectives, along with their communication implications, we will attempt in this chapter to develop such a several-perspectives-at-once viewpoint.
 

THE CLASSICAL PERSPECTIVE

The classical perspective is a product of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the time of the Industrial Revolution. At the turn of the century, large-scale manufacturing was new, and its techniques, such as assembly-line production with which Henry Ford was to manufacture thousands of Model T's, were still developing. Organization theorists of the time were mainly concerned with making work methods as efficient as possible and with making large organizations logical, comprehensible, and controllable. Work methods were the focus of a sub-field of classical theory known as scientific management, represented principally by the work and writings of Frederick W. Taylor. Two prominent theorists, Max Weber and Henri Fayol, represented a second sub-field, bureaucratic theory, which addressed the large organization.
 

Scientific Management

Frederick W. Taylor, a foreman at the Bethlehem Steel Works in Bethlehem, Pennsylvania, in the early 1900's, was one of theorist efficiency experts, systematically observing his workers in order to help them to do more work in less time. Famous for his stopwatch-timed studies of the most minute details of a worker's motions, Taylor embodied the guiding principle of the industrial revolution: productivity increases as cost per unit decreases.

In one of his classic projects, Taylor studied coal shoveling at the Bethlehem Works. Finding, through experimentation, that workers shoveled most efficiently when each shovel weighed about twenty-one pounds, he issued each worker a standard-sized shovel with a twenty-one-pound capacity, replacing the shovels of various sizes that they had previously brought from home. He then gave each worker precise instructions on the most efficient techniques for shoveling and provided pay incentives for those who exceeded certain shoveling norms. The results were astonishing. The average amount of coal by each worker shoveled per day increased from fifteen to fifty-nine tons. Coal shoveling costs dropped from 7.3 cents to 3.2 cents per ton.

Taylor also specified management's responsibilities. These included precisely engineering each person's work by scientifically selecting and training workers, working closely in cooperation with workers to ensure that they followed prescribed methods, and dividing the work load equally between management and the work force, according to the tasks for which each group was best suited, so that managers, not just workers, were fully occupied and were using their time with maximum efficiency.2 Exhibit 3-1 summarizes Taylor's scientific management principles and compares them with the practices in use up to Taylor's time.
 

Bureaucratic Theory

Other theorists who contributed to the classical perspective centered their attention on the structure of organizations, rather than on the activity within them. Two such theorists were the German sociologist, Max Weber, and Henri Fayol, a French executive, both of whom wrote around the same time as Taylor. Like Taylor's principles, their ideas still influence managers' thinking today.

Max Weber developed a theoretical prototype, the bureaucracy, which he saw as the ideal to which organizations should aspire. For many people today, the word "bureaucracy" evokes images of mounds of red tape and incomprehensibly detailed forms. For Weber, however, bureaucracy meant logic, order, uniformity, and consistency. To achieve these qualities, the bureaucratic organization should emphasize impersonality and strict adherence to rules. Such an organization would have the following primary characteristics:
 

Specialization and division of labor. Task subdivision allows each unit to perfect its own special function.

Rules and procedures. Written regulations largely govern the personnel activity and the functions of positions in the organization. Managers devote much of their time to determining how these regulations apply to particular work situations.

Hierarchy of authority. Positions form a hierarchy having the general shape of a pyramid. Supervisors answer to immediate supervisors for the action of their subordinates, and the organization exercises control along these hierarchical lines.

Formal communication. The organization records all administrative acts, decisions, and rules. Information should flow along the lines the hierarchy of authority prescribes.

Detailed job descriptions.. The duties and responsibilities for each position in the organization are defined clearly and in writing.

Employment based on expertise. The organization places individuals in positions according to their technical competence.

Impersonal environment. Relationships are to be detached and impersonal so that the personal thoughts or feelings of neither supervisors nor subordinates will bias their decisions.

A second writer on bureaucratic theory, Henri Fayol, was a very successful manager. Fayol headed a large French mining company, which he brought from the brink of bankruptcy to a record of effective performance and financial soundness. Like Weber, Fayol believed in specialization and division of labor, in a hierarchy of authority reaching from the top of the organization to the bottom, and in orderly, fair, stable organizational arrangements. Certain of what Fayol called the principles of management became bywords for most managers by the mid-twentieth century; still strongly influencing management thought today, these principles include the following:

  • Unity of command. For any action, an employee should receive orders from one superior only.
  • Unity of direction. Each group of organizational activities that have the same objectives should follow one plan and the leadership of one person.
  • Authority. Managers have the right to give orders and to exact obedience.
  • Subordination of individual interests to the general interest. The interest of an individual or group should not prevail over the interests of the organization as a whole.
  • Order. The organization must appoint places for people and materials, and they should be in the right place at the right time.

Despite the seeming rigidity of his principles, Fayol was one of the first Organization theorists to realize that requiring unconditional compliance with a single organizational pattern in all circumstances could lead to problems. Observing that formal communication via the organizational hierarchy is at times cumbersome and time-consuming, he maintained, for example, that a person must sometimes bypass normal channels to communicate directly with someone in another organizational unit (see "Fayol's Bridge," Exhibit 2-3 in chapter 2). Further, he wrote that, in terms of authority, neither centralization nor decentralization is a universal ideal; rather, each organization must determine its optimal degree of authority.
 

Communication from the Classical Perspective

From the classical perspective, communication is an instrument of command and control. Most communication should be vertical, moving mainly downward from the authority residing at the organization's top. Lateral communication (communication between those of equal status), which threatens efficiency, should be used, as Fayol suggests, only when vertical channels are not feasible. Ideally, an organization should limit its formal communication to carefully formulated, concrete, and specialized task planning and task execution.

In classically run organizations, communication derives its character from certain organizational characteristics. Two of these are formalization and centralization. A formalized organization is one in which job requirements are explicit and written. A centralized organization is one in which decisions tend to be made at or near the top. Those who work in formalized, centralized organizations tend to engage in relatively little oral, lateral, and unscheduled communication, and, as organizations become more formalized and centralized, communication between persons in different departments tends to decrease. In other words, communication in a classical organization is less spontaneous than that in a less formalized, more decentralized organization.

Today, the classical perspective continues to affect organizational communication. In the example at the opening of this chapter, the medical professionals at Metropolitan Community Health Centers reported to service chiefs at the HMO's headquarters in a classical arrangement that organized personnel by professional specialization and vested authority in those at the structure's summit. Those at MCHC who feel that supervisors should specialize in the same type of medicine as those they supervise, that the HMO should very clearly spell out reporting relationships and that authority should reside at the top of the organization, not in decentralized neighborhood centers, resist Maria Sanchez's new position. On the other hand, some MCHC personnel see weaknesses in the classical point of view. One staff member feels a need for more direct coordination between the medical specialties at the neighborhood level. Another wants less top-down management and more input from persons at lower levels. A second major body of organizational theory, the human perspective, represents concerns of this kind.
 

THE HUMAN PERSPECTIVE

In the 1920's and 1930's, researchers and writers began to question the rigid formalism of the classical perspective. The human perspective in organization theory emerged at a time when the growing American labor movement was stressing the rights of the individual worker. Then, following World War II, an increasingly well-educated work force pushed organizations to be more aware of workers' needs. Among the principles that emerged during this era were the following:

  • People want to feel important and be recognized as individuals.
  • Interpersonal and group relationships greatly help to determine productivity.
  • Leaders who encourage subordinates' autonomy and responsibility are more effective than those who closely control their subordinates.

The first and second of the above propositions arose from the so-called Hawthorne studies, conducted by Elton Mayo and Fritz Roethlisberger, and from the Kurt Lewin's work on group dynamics. The third sprang from the writings of Douglas McGregor on developmental leadership.
 

The Hawthorne Studies

Beginning in 1924, researchers sponsored by the National Academy of Sciences studied the effects of lighting on worker productivity at the Hawthorne Works of the Western Electric Company, near Chicago. The project's early results were puzzling. Regardless of whether researchers increased or decreased the lighting, worker productivity either remained constant or exceeded normal output. This was true even when the researchers so dimmed the lighting that the workers could barely see what they were doing. Further studies by Mayo, Roethlisberger, and others from the Harvard Business School, and by William Dickson of Western Electric, gauged the effects of rest pauses, shorter work days and weeks, wage incentives, and supervisory practices on output. Again, production stayed constant or increased, regardless of the changes the researchers made. Neither physical conditions (as Taylor would contend) nor organization structure (as Weber and Fayol might suggest) affected output.

Puzzled, the researchers conducted observations and interviews, concluding, finally, that the workers' on-the-job efforts were the result of the attention they were receiving from the research team, not due to the changes in working conditions. This phenomenon, later termed the "Hawthorne effect," became a foundation for the notion that worker attitudes and feelings, as well as their relationships with others they respected, could significantly influence productivity.

As a result, Western Electric began an extensive program in which interviewers asked workers about their experiences and feelings at work. The interviewing program confirmed that the quality of interpersonal relationships, particularly that of workers' relationships with their supervisors, was crucial to morale and productivity. Based on this, the company instituted a training program for supervisors.

More recently, however, the Hawthorne studies have come under fire from researchers who, in re-analyzing the 1930's data, suggest that the increased output resulted more from a depression-era fear of unemployment than from quality work relationships. But, despite possible flaws in the Hawthorne studies, the main conclusion of Mayo, Roethlisberger, and Dickson has had lasting impact: human relationships have a major effect on the quality of organizational performance.

In a later stage of the Hawthorne studies, the researchers observed behavior within a single work group. This project, known as the bank wiring room study, revealed another "human" aspect of organizations: informal associations develop spontaneously among people at work. The researchers found that 1) a group develops informal roles and rules that control and restrict the productivity of individual members; 2) informal leaders emerge in a group; and 3) a group tends to ostracize workers who produce more or less than the group's established norm. This research further confirmed that workers' actions can be understood only by understanding the social processes within an organization and the ways in which the workers themselves view such processes.
 

Group Dynamics

Further insights into the human dimension of work group performance came from the small group studies of Kurt Lewin and his associates. A refugee from Nazi Germany, Lewin had a deep commitment to democracy and a strong desire to apply the science of psychology to improving the world. While Lewin was at the University of Iowa during World War II, the U.S. government asked him to discover ways to dissuade housewives from purchasing meat, which was in short supply. As Lewin saw it, housewives' families, parents, and friends expected them to serve meat, and such expectations created a barrier to changes in buying habits. Hypothesizing that, given the opportunity to discuss the meat-buying issue with other housewives and to decide buying tendencies for themselves, these women could overcome the barrier, Lewin and his associates conducted experiments which supported his idea about group participation. Housewives who participated in discussions were more than ten times as likely to change their purchasing habits than were those who received lectures on the topic.

Later, Lewin's associates shifted their participation studies to industrial settings. Lester Coch and John R. P. French found that employees in a pajama factory in Philadelphia were much more likely to adopt new work methods if given the opportunity to discuss the methods and to exert some influence on decisions concerning how the methods would apply to their jobs. Studies such as Lewin's and Coch and French's heralded an increased attention to the impact of group communication in organizations. A generation of research following this early work has confirmed the relationship between organizational effectiveness and group formation, development, behavior, and attitudes.
 

Developmental Leadership

From the classical perspective, leadership involved directing and controlling subordinates to ensure that they carried out their superiors' decisions. From the human perspective, leadership came to imply that superiors should nurture subordinates' capacities for decision making and self-direction. Douglas McGregor, a management professor at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in the 1950's and 1960's and a former president of Antioch University, distinguished sharply between the two views. From McGregor's standpoint, the classical and human perspectives, which he called Theory X and Theory Y, consisted of contrasting sets of assumptions that managers made about people at work.

McGregor's Theory X manager assumes that people dislike work, are neither intelligent nor creative, do not care about the organization's goals, and will perform adequately only under threats of punishment or promises of reward. The Theory X manager also assumes that people prefer outside direction to autonomy, in order to avoid responsibility for their actions. In contrast, the Theory Y manager assumes people are willing to work for organizational goals and will direct themselves responsibly if provided the right conditions.

McGregor doubted that Theory X assumptions provided the best basis for motivating workers. Building on the work of Mayo, Roethlisberger and others, he proposed that Theory Y offered a more valid and promising set of managerial assumptions. The Theory Y manager assumes that workers find work natural, not distasteful; that they are intelligent and creative; that they are willing to work toward the organization's goals; and that they are capable of self-direction. McGregor asserted that most managers in the 1960's fostered Theory X assumptions and that their organizations thus ignored the true intellectual and motivational potential of their employees. While managerial thinking has clearly shifted toward Theory Y since the 1960's, McGregor's point about untapped potential in organizations remains valid in the 1990's.
 

Communication from the Human Perspective

In contrast to the classical perspective's view of communication as an instrument of direction and control (principally descending from the top of the organization), communication from the human perspective is much less limited. It supports information ascending from lower to upper levels of the organization, as well as information flowing laterally, and facilitates participative problem solving, planning, and organizing. The human perspective also recognizes the potential value of decentralized decision making, where authority is based on knowledge, experience, and access to information, rather than on hierarchical position. From this perspective, communication, at its most effective, taps the full potential of those who work for the organization, encouraging creativity as well as productivity.

The organizational change at Metropolitan Community Health Centers reflects the influence of the human perspective. Control will no longer originate at the top of the organization, the downtown center headquarters. Dr. Sanchez will coordinate specialists at the Rockland Park neighborhood center. Some at MCHC who express reservations about the change reflect the human perspective. For example, the Rockland Park physician who fears that "it's going to be chaos if we can't continue working directly with our service chiefs" recognizes the importance of the HMO professionals' well-established personal relationships. The service chief who predicts that the change will have to "work itself out over time" also recognizes the importance of informal human relationships (which take time to develop). By adding that "individual service chiefs will want to handle things differently," this service chief seems to express the hope that the HMO will allow the chiefs to act creatively.
