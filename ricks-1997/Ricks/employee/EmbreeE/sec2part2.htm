INTEGRATIVE PERSPECTIVES

Critics have faulted both the classical and the human perspectives for failing to recognize one another's importance and for failing to acknowledge the environments in which organizations operate. In its focus on the logic of work design and structure, the classical perspective pays little attention to human relationships. The human perspective, in contrast, focuses on individuals at work, including leaders and groups, but gives little consideration to technology and other influences from outside the organization. From the 1950's through the 1970's, in response to these criticisms, researchers developed more encompassing perspectives. The socio-technical model and the system model embody these integrative perspectives.
 

The Socio-technical Model

In the 1950's, researchers Eric Trist and K. W. Bamforth studied the effects of a change in the mining methods used in the British coal industry. In the old "shortwall" method, the miners worked in close proximity to one another. Each miner was capable of performing all necessary mining tasks, and the miners, working in a highly cohesive group, took turns doing these tasks. This method was replaced by the "longwall" method. Standing far apart, the miners worked across a long coal face, using new power driven machinery. Under the longwall system, each worker had a single task. The first shift of miners carried out the blasting, the first task in the mining process; the second shift loosened the coal; and the third shift shoveled the coal onto a conveyor. This division of labor was intended to make each miner more skillful and efficient in his tasks.

The longwall method was unsuccessful. Productivity dropped; arguments arose over pay schedules, now based on the different values of each task; and workers complained bitterly about their particular jobs. The researchers concluded that the longwall technology had destroyed a social system that was crucial to the miners' morale and motivation. Cohesive work groups had been shattered. Miners no longer shared common tasks and a common pay rate and, due to their physical separation, found it difficult to talk with one another.

To address these problems, Trist and Bamforth designed a way of utilizing the technical advances in mining methods while maintaining the social benefits found in the former groups (hence the term socio-technical model). They suggested that workers should be trained in all tasks involving the technologically advanced equipment, that multi-shift teams should be responsible for setting their own production rates, and that pay levels should depend on the combined output of all shifts. When tested, the socio-technical method produced 5.3 tons of coal per shift, compared with the longwall method's 3.5 tons. Trist and Bamforth recognized that managers of organized activity must attend both to technology (representing the classical perspective) and to work groups (representing the human perspective), preferably considering the two factors in combination. Their success in the British coal industry provides a compelling argument for the value of viewing an organizational setting from more than one perspective.
 
 

The System Model

As discussed in chapter 1, the system concept presents the organization as a complex set of interacting parts acting on its environment and being acted upon it in turn. Applied to organizations by Daniel Katz and Robert Kahn, among others, in the 1950s and 1960s, the system model evolved from economic, sociological, psychological, and natural science theories. In the system view of economics, for example, we may consider the nation's economy to be a set of industries, financial institutions, and individuals whose interactions establish the nation's internal commerce while simultaneously affecting (and being affected by) the world economic environment In medicine, the living human body is a complex of organic subsystems -- nerves, muscles, organs, and bones -- that interact with each other and with the body's surroundings. Between subsystems or between systems, interaction may be favorable or unfavorable, producing healthy or unhealthy bodies, countries with rising GNPs or rocketing trade deficits. Given either prosperity or recession, health or illness, system model offers a framework for explaining results comprehensively rather than simplistically. One proponent of system theory, John Seiler, warns that people tend to make what he calls the "single cause error," jumping too quickly to overly simple one cause explanations of complex phenomena, such as those common in organizations. Thinking in terms of the system model helps us avoid the single cause error.

Within the organization, workers alone and in groups use technology to perform tasks while the organization's structure guides them in coordinating their activities. These elements have mutual effects. Technology influences behavior, for example; but individuals and groups determine the technology the organization will adopt. On a larger scale, the organization as a whole interacts with the environment, receiving input from economic, political, and social conditions, from customers and clients, and from competitors. To such input, the organization responds with output in the form of products, services, and innovations, receiving more environmental input in reply.

As systems, organizations may be described as open or closed, mechanistic or organic. An open system exchanges information with its environment -- the "inputs" get through to it, and it adapts to them -- while a closed system limits its exchanges with its environment and seeks to operate as a self-contained unit. Clearly, open system characteristics are essential for organizations operating in rapidly changing environments.

An organic system is internally flexible, allowing a high degree of employee influence on decisions. Tasks are not rigidly defined, and the formal structure is neither permanent nor unchangeable. In a mechanistic system, the organization is run from the classical perspective. Activities are highly specialized and clearly defined, employees work according to standardized rules and procedures, and those in high places tend to monopolize the decision making. In general, the organic system is suited to change, while the mechanistic system functions best under stable conditions. Internally, organic organizational systems tend to be more open to their environments, while mechanistic systems close themselves off. In the next chapter, we will further examine the idea that the internal structure and communication patterns appropriate for an organization depend upon the nature of the organization's environment.
 
 

Communication from Integrative Perspectives

Socio-technical theory and the system model imply that communication patterns may vary greatly from one effective organization to another, or within the same organization at different times. Unlike the classical and human perspectives, the integrative perspectives propose no "one best way" for communication to occur. While classical theories emphasize the importance of centralized decision making and vertical communication channels, and the human perspective stresses decentralization and horizontal flows, the integrated perspective maintains that, given multitudes of organizations and millions of situations, "it depends."

Both socio-technical theory and the system model suggest that communication patterns depend on the organization's function, its environment, and) the social, educational, and personal makeup of members. Hence, we may expect the direction and frequency of messages, the balance of power in interpersonal and intergroup relationships, and the networks of persons who make decisions to differ between organizations and possibly over time within a single organization. The effective organization is able to adapt its internal communication patterns at all organizational levels to changes in its external environment.

The system model in particular implies that organizational communication includes external communication with customers, communities, governments, suppliers of goods and information, and others to whom the organization must respond, and toward whom the organization must direct information and influence. The organizational system that closes itself to such external transactions does so at its peril.

From an integrative perspective, we can see the organizational change at Metropolitan Community Health Centers as an effort to create a more organic system. MCHC has adapted to its environment by opening several neighborhood centers; this, in turn, has produced a need for direct decision making at the neighborhood center level. Giving Rockland Park its own director is a step toward meeting that need. However, the change will likely require the evolution of new internal communication patterns. For example, the specialists at the Rockland Park center, who once answered only to their service chiefs, may continue to contact the chiefs on medical matters but report to Sanchez on administrative matters such as scheduling and personnel.
 
 

CONTEMPORARY PERSPECTIVES

The 1970's and 1980's produced a new view of organizations, a shift in perspective from objectivity toward subjectivity. The objective view is that the organization is something concrete, with set, observable qualities, much like the physical world is to a scientist; and the objectivist views the organization "from outside," much as an astronomer views a galaxy through a telescope, and understands the organization and its physical qualities empirically, as a scientist would, in terms of his or her own observations and concepts. The subjective view, on the other hand, maintains that the observer can understand the organization only on its own terms, that the order and meanings to be discovered in an organization are those which have been created (more accurately -- which are being created) by the members themselves. The subjectivist sees the organization as an ongoing process of organizing. Thus, the objectivist pays more attention to structure, the subjectivist to behavior.

A device used often by theorists taking the subjective view of organizations is the metaphor. Gareth Morgan, in his 1986 book, Images of Organization, metaphorically refers to organizations as machines, organisms, brains, cultures, political systems, psychic prisons, and instruments of domination. Each metaphor vividly illustrates an aspect of the evolving action and thinking of people in organizations. Morgan notes that each metaphor represents only a single facet of an entire organization and that the kind of critical thinking on which effective action depends requires using several metaphors to recognize the multiple meanings within an organization.

To better understand contemporary organization theory, we will examine two of the metaphors discussed by Morgan as well as by other writers. Both reflect the subjective view of organizations. The first metaphor is that of the organization as a brain; the second presents the organization as a culture.
 
 

The Organization as a Brain

Like brains, organizations process information and make decisions. In performing these functions, brains operate under human limitations, but possess amazing capabilities as well. Observing that the same is true of organizations, Morgan first addresses the brain-like limitations of organizations and then examines their capabilities.

To illustrate the less-than-perfect operation of brains and organizations, Morgan draws on the work of Nobel Prize winner Herbert Simon and his colleagues, whose work in the 1940s and 1950s, explored the similarity between human and organizational decision making ' According to Simon, individuals and organizations are capable of only limited forms of rationality. This bounded rationality reveals itself in the simplistic rules of thumb and the less than thorough information searches people employ in making decisions; it appears in the confined attention and biased interpretations that result from dividing an organization into sharply separated specialties that simplify managers' interests and narrow their vision.

In more recent years, other researchers have employed a brain-like metaphor that of the organization as information processor. Jay Galbraith, for example, explains how many organizations fail to adapt to change because they are unable to process the flood of complex information necessary for coping with a dynamic environment. He argues that organizations buckling under this threat of information overload must either avoid dealing with so overwhelming an environment by simplifying their objectives, or increase their ability to handle information. Making the structure more organic is one way to clear the flow of information among its members. Unlike Simon's bounded rationality, Galbraith's view suggests that organizations are capable of improving their "thinking."

Concerning the capabilities of organizations, Morgan notes that, like brains, they have the capacity to learn. He observes that organizations can accomplish two kinds of learning, single-loop learning and double-loop learning, concepts developed by Chris Argyris and Donald Schon.

Single-loop learning is the ability of any system to adjust its behavior in response to negative feedback. A helmsman steers a ship by noting whether it is off course in a certain direction and by shifting the rudder in the opposite direction. Single-loop learning, involves an ability to detect and correct error relative to a given set of objectives. Many organizations are highly adept at single-loop learning, having well developed information systems designed to keep the organization "on track." An organization may build in single-loop learning, for example, by monitoring its revenues and expenses to ensure that its budget specialists identify unexpectedly high costs in time to take corrective action.

Double-loop learning is a system's ability not only to adjust its performance toward a given objective, but also to question that objective and adjust it if necessary. A thermostat is an example of a system that does not have this capacity to question itself. It just maintains a set temperature. When the shivering occupants of a room decide to turn up the heat, they provide a "second loop" in the system. Morgan observes that, although some organizations are able to review and challenge their basic objectives, many are not. He feels bureaucracies are particularly unlikely to engage in double-loop learning, since it requires an organization to openly discuss its errors, to willingly explore problems from diverse and new viewpoints, and to take a bottom-to-top participative approach to planning.

In addition to learning, a second and very remarkable feature of the brain, Morgan notes, is its holographic character. A hologram is a photographic plate able to present a three dimensional image because each small part of the plate contains all the information necessary to produce the entire image. In the brain, each neuron (nerve cell) is connected with hundreds of thousands of
others, giving the brain something very much like a holographic nature.

Though different regions of the brain specialize in different activities, they are somewhat capable of acting in place of one another. Rather on relying on a central control region for instructions. Each part of the brain has a remarkable capacity both to execute its tasks and to know what is going on elsewhere.

Morgan recognizes the brain's holographic character in organizations that build complex networks of connections between individual parts that operate quite capably as organizations in miniature, efficient and self supporting. As an example, he describes a computer firm that divides its 200 plant employees into fourteen to eighteen member teams, each having complete responsibility for production. Every employee becomes qualified in all the tasks required to produce the whole product. The teams make their own decisions on how to divide work, on scheduling, on problems in supplies and shipping, on improvements in work design, and on hiring new members. Thus, the firm invests each employee with a sense of the organizational whole. The principles of learning and holographic versatility can provide organizations stimulating guidelines for increasing their effectiveness.
 
 

The Organization as a Culture

The culture concept is a currently popular and very rich metaphor for understanding organizations. As you will recall from section 1, Edgar Schein defined an organization's culture as the basic assumptions its members share. To refer to a culture is to take an anthropologist's perspective: to see an organization as a system of knowledge, values, beliefs, ideals, laws, speech, action, and artifacts. According to the cultural view, the manner in which these elements interact creates themes and meanings that become the organization's identity. From the cultural perspective, every aspect of an organization possesses symbolic meaning. Even familiar and routine things like the monthly budget report and everyday terminology assume significance as ways by which the organization expresses its character.

Reports, language, and other visible, audible, or tangible dues provide evidence of the basic assumptions that comprise the organizational culture. According to Schein, an observer may uncover a culture's assumptions by organizing such clues into the following five categories:

  1. Assumptions about the organization's relationship to its environment, including what its members consider to be as the organization's basic mission, its opportunities, its degree of success, and the major threats to such success.
  2. The organization's basic assumptions about reality, which shape its decisions-- such assumptions might reflect tradition ("we've always done it that way"), trial and error ("let's try it and see how it works"), or scientific test ("our research proves this is the way to go"), for example.
  3. Assumptions about human nature, i.e., whether people are basically "good" or "bad," fixed in their ways or changeable.
  4. Assumptions about the nature of human activity, in terms of whether people should act to change things, accept things as they are, or work in harmony with their surroundings.
  5. Assumptions about human relationships, whether, for example, autocracy, paternalism, consultation, participation, delegation, or collegiality should color the relationships within the organization.
In their book, Corporate Cultures, Terrence Deal and Allan Kennedy illustrate the cultural perspective. From studies of numerous corporations, they derive four basic types of organization culture:
  • The tough-guy, macho culture is characterized by individualistic employees who take big risks and get quick feedback on the success or failure of their actions. The stress level is high, and colleagues watch one another, pirhana-like, for signs of weakness. Corporate heroes come and go quickly.
  • The work hard/play hard culture maintains a high level of relatively low-risk activity. The organization's credo emphasizes teamwork, meetings, employee contests, and high-volume output.
  • The bet-your-company culture requires its members to make decisions for heavy stakes, but provides feedback slowly. A sense of deliberation pervades these organizations, which emphasize making important decisions correctly. The central event (ritual, to use the anthropological term) is the business meeting, where top personnel receive information from junior employees and make decisions with careful attention to every detail. Risk taking and immaturity are not tolerated.
  • The process culture is an organization whose people focus not on what is done, but on how things are done: in short, a bureaucracy. Because the culture provides little feedback on actual results, trivial events assume exaggerated importance, as do titles and formalities.
Not every organization has a single pervasive culture. Morgan points out that "in organizations there are often many different and competing value systems that create a mosaic of organizational realities rather than a uniform corporate culture" Accountants, for example, may adhere to one philosophy, marketing people to another. Different frames of reference may guide development engineers and production department members, and each group may speak its own language.

Morgan notes with special emphasis that the essence of culture is "shared meaning." The people in a culture share in a process of "reality construction" that allows them to perceive actions, events, and utterances in distinctive ways. Organizational psychologist Karl Weick labels this process enactment. By using this term, he stresses the active role people unconsciously play in creating their view of the world. Culture, then, is an ongoing process of making meaning; it is, in Morgan's words, "an active, living phenomenon through which people create and recreate the worlds in which they live.''

However, not all of an organization's elements may have equal say in the phenomenon; the process, in fact, can be quite biased. For example, Morgan identifies accountants as the "reality constructors" in many organizations. They endow the corporate culture with a kind of money-based vision, a lens through which reality consists of profit and loss. The corporation overlooks other ways of thinking about organization, and financial considerations, at least for a time, hold sway. Another organization's reality might be dominated by a marketing perspective, to the detriment of sound financial planning and attention to production; still another might be dominated by its computer specialists or other technical personnel. Again, we can recognize the value of viewing the organization from more than a single perspective.
 
 

Communication from Contemporary Perspectives

The contemporary perspectives spotlight communication as the critical element in an organization. The brain metaphor implies that organizations can learn. Whether an organization does learn depends on its establishing communication with its environment and among its members. The quality of that communication -- whether it merely provides information about whether the organization is achieving its existing objectives (single-loop learning) or whether it prompts the organization to reassess its objectives (double-loop learning) -- determines how fully the organization will realize its learning potential. Simon's view of individuals as having very limited ("bounded") rationality on their own suggests the importance of collaborative communication

Open, efficient communication is the hallmark of an organization that seeks the holographic ideal. An organization whose sub-units achieve through communication an awareness of each others' functions can shed bureaucratic rigidity to deal effectively with complex problems and to find useful new ways of organizing itself.

The culture metaphor suggests that it is worthwhile to view communication as an anthropologist would: to acknowledge how people use language, as well as other symbols and artifacts, to aid them in expressing and forming meanings. Communication is the process by which people manifest and share culture; conversely, according to the enactment concept, communication is also the process by which culture is continuously being created. Managers and those who attempt to change organizational behavior, including communication, must realize they are dealing with a culture, a collection of "basic assumptions" (according to Schein's definition) that people use to make sense of their surroundings. Changing an organization's behavior thus requires changing the sense making patterns of its personnel, changing how they perceive the "reality" of the organization.

In the Metropolitan Community Health Centers example, the new director's position at the Rockland Park neighborhood center provides the HMO with a new sense-making capability. By efficiently providing information to the service chiefs and others at the downtown center about the activities and problems at Rockland Park, Maria Sanchez could improve MCHC's single-loop learning ability. If she also solicits suggestions from her employees about MCHC's objectives and uses these as a basis for policy suggestions to her superiors, Dr. Sanchez will help MCHC to develop double-loop learning. In holographic terms, if Sanchez builds effective connections with both her own staff and the downtown personnel, she could help the service chiefs as well as the Rockland Park practitioners to draw on and use each other's information and capabilities more effectively. Finally, in culture terms, by encouraging input and flexibly acknowledging suggestions for change, Dr. Sanchez can create a satisfying and efficient work environment at Rockland Park, one that avoids both the strictures of a bureaucracy and the cutthroat atmosphere of a culture defined by stress. The uncertain and somewhat worried comments made by several MCHC physicians and downtown service chiefs suggest that the center's new culture has yet to emerge.
 
 

SUMMARY

With the aid of organization theory, we can view organizations in terms of four perspectives, classical, human, integrative, and contemporary. Each perspective raises different questions about the organization's nature. Organization members who can view their surroundings from more than one perspective are likely to perform more effectively than those whose awareness is more limited.

From the classical perspective, questions emerge about the duties an organization prescribes for its personnel -- are jobs clearly defined? and do they specify the most efficient possible work methods? -- and about the formal, rational aspects of the total organization -- is there, for example, a logical hierarchy of authority that provides unified direction?

From the human perspective, the focus shifts to people, raising questions about the types of informal communication links and groupings that exist in the organization and their effects, as well as about leadership styles and their impact on morale and motivation.

Integrated perspectives such as the socio-technical model and systems theory examine how well the organization adjusts to its changing environment and to the requirements of its technology and present a view of the organization as a complex system of interacting parts. Understanding these parts and their mutual effects helps to explain why the organization is succeeding or failing in its efforts to adapt.

Contemporary perspectives like the brain and culture metaphors prompt questions about how the organization defines and experiences itself. Does it "learn" in single-loop or double-loop fashion? To what extent does each part of the organization know what other parts know? What significant meanings does the organization create through the events that occur and the objects that are present within its structure? What meanings do its members create through their language and actions?

Gareth Morgan states that "any realistic approach to organizational analysis must start from the premise that organizations can be many things at one and the same time." Taken as a whole, organization theory implies that we may understand an organization most fully and usefully by observing it from several perspectives.
