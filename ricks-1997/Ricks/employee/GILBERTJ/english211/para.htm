Summarizing and Paraphrasing

Writing Summaries

  1. A good summary is brief. Summaries achieve their brevity by eliminating details, examples, facts, and by reporting the "sum" of what the supporting material adds up to.

  2. A good summary is objective. Leave out your own opinions and focus on reporting the author's important points. Write in third person. (Use pronouns like he, she, they, and nouns like the writer, the researcher.) Avoid pronouns like I, we, and you.

  3. A good summary is mostly paraphrase. Think of yourself as a translator: you are translating the author's language into your own. If you decide to quote, keep quotations brief and always place them within quotation marks.

  4. A good summary is written in the present tense. Write as if the idea is unfolding for your readers as they read. However, if you are recounting an incident that is truly "historically past," past tense is acceptable.

  5. A good summary is coherent and clear. Too often summaries sound like lists of ideas. Use transitional expressions to clarify the logical relationship among the ideas you are reporting.

Writing a Paraphrase

  1. Make sure that you understand the source passage

  2. Rewrite the original in your own words. It's not necessary to find substitutes for function words like conjunctions and prepositions, but find synonyms for nouns, verbs and adjectives.

  3. Re-arrange your author's sentence structure to make the syntax your own.

Paraphrase Practice

Paraphrase the following paragraph from "How to Write History" by Dixon Wecter. Take it sentence by sentence, following the guidelines outlined above. If you're not sure what a word means, don't be afraid to look it up.

"For all its huge, able, often highly original output, the last half century of American research has yielded almost no great books worthy to stand as literature beside the classics of our first hundred years. Industry minus art, accumulation lacking charm, data without digestion such shortcomings explain this popular allergy against American history as written. A great many school texts are pretty repulsive, while history for the adult seems hardly more inviting. After diligently harvesting the grain of fact, too few investigators seem to have time left for threshing out the chaff or milling the flour. Their energy is exhausted long before the job is done so that readers have to choose between the pedant's dry straw and the half-baked loaf turned out by historical romancers."
