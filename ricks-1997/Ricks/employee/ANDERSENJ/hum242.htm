Course Objectives:

This course traces the development of Islamic civilization from the birth of Muhammad to the modern age.  Not only will we discuss major historical events, but also the philosophies and values that led to or were influenced by these events, including the basic tenants of Islam.  Our study will show us how Islam, mingling with the tribal cultures of Arabia and central Asia, produced significant and unique forms of literature, architecture, paintings, and music, which spread throughout the Islamic world.  In short, we will try to discover through our studies how Islamic societies approached the perennial human quest of finding meaning and fulfillment in life.

You may ask yourself, "Why take this course?  Of what relevance is it to me?  Will this course help me get a better job or increase my prospects of being accepted by the university of my choice? "  This course may or may not have these effects, but I think there are some more important reasons for being exposed to Islamic culture.

One reason is that the Islamic civilization contributed significantly to the development of mathematics and science, medicine, and the arts that helped form our modern world. Moreover, much of our knowledge of Greek and Roman literature, philosophy, and science was preserved by Muslim scholars while Europe was in a state of turmoil.  Part of understanding the present is knowing how we got here.

Another reason is that the Middle East and Islam are of great significance in current world affairs. There are about one billion Muslims in the world today, or about a fifth of the world's population.  Many of the world’s present challenges and conflicts involve Muslim peoples.  Anything that promotes cross-cultural understanding and aids in the comprehension of world politics, economics, and even business practices is certainly of value. 

Finally, as members of the Church, we have a divine mandate to learn of "…things which have been, things which are, things which must shortly come to pass; things which are at home, things which are abroad; the wars and the perplexities of nations, …and a knowledge also of countries and of kingdoms" (D&C 88:79) so that we can effectively fulfill our obligation to flood the world with the gospel message.  The fact that this message must "be preached in all the world, for a witness unto all nations" (J.S. Matt. 1:31) gives our course of study relevance.

Expectations:
 People sometimes compare the relationship between student and teacher in the learning process to an empty glass and a pitcher of water.   This analogy assumes students have no knowledge of the subject.  Their job is to bring their empty minds to class, and the teacher’s job is to fill it up.  The weakness of this analogy is that it places the entire responsibility for learning on the teacher, when in reality learning is a joint venture.

I see my relationship to you as similar to a jar of Tang to a glass of water.  You already have some knowledge; my responsibility is to build on your experience and add flavor to it.  Your job is to make use of this new knowledge by stirring it in and making it a part of you.  If you don’t take an active part in this process, then try as I might, my efforts will sink to the bottom of the glass, and make very little impact in your life.

My Job:  I commit to do my very best to present our course material in a professional and interesting manner.  I promise to respect your views and opinions, and make any effort to help you succeed.

Your Job:  I expect you to be a scholar, not just a student, as J. Bonner Ritchie explains:

“In the student-scholar model, the individual students not only accept responsibility for their own learning, but for contributing to the quality of the class.  They read and communicate to learn and to grow, not just to fulfill an assignment or to get a grade.  They define learning objectives and activities beyond the course requirements.  They do not continually ask if a reading assignment or discussion point ‘is going to be on the exam.’  ... Scholar students can make almost any class a worthwhile learning experience for themselves and class members”.  (Brigham Young Magazine August 1996)

Reading Response Assignment:
People can learn in a variety of ways, including: listening to lectures, asking questions, watching videos, doing research, participating in group discussions, reading texts, writing, and even taking tests.  I hope to involve you in all of these learning methods.  However, my experience is that students generally rely too much on lectures and notes for the dissemination and absorption of knowledge.  This assignment is designed to help you to make greater use of texts in the learning process.  After completing  each assigned reading, you are to write a response or reflection which may include a summary of what you have learned from the readings, identification of  key ideas, events, innovations, and personalities, or formulation of opinions and questions. As you write, you will simultaneously be preparing yourself for the exams. These responses also benefit the class in that more class time can be spent in discussing meaning and significance in place of lectures that simply disseminate information.  A more detailed description of this assignment is included later in this syllabus.

Group Assignment:

Purposes:

1.         Cooperative learning.  Group work fosters interpersonal relations, provides a built-in support group, and allows individuals to pool their talents and creativity to produce something greater than a person working alone might accomplish.  It prepares students for the work world where groups are often assigned projects to accomplish.

 2.         Scholarship.  Our course is designed to provide a broad overview of the subject.  This assignment gives you an opportunity to delve more deeply into one aspect of Islamic culture and become acquainted with some of the scholarly work on the subject, and to share your newfound knowledge with the class.  At the same time, you will develop skills in researching and in verbal and written communication.

Procedures:
Groups will be formed according to interest in particular topics (see the list of possible topics below). Presentation dates are set according to where those topics occur in our lesson schedule.  As a group, you should decide what course of action you will take to accomplish your task.  Divide up the labor among yourselves as evenly as possible, trying to take advantage of the talents of each group member.  As you begin researching, try to avoid the duplication of work.  Send one person to look for information in encyclopedias, another to look for books, another to seek out articles in periodicals, and so on.  Don’t be afraid to ask a librarian for help or to ask for my suggestions and input.  In fact, I encourage you to meet with me as a group well before your presentation to receive guidance and help with resources.

Evaluation:
The oral report should be informative and professionally presented.  Each member of the group will be expected to participate in the presentation. He/she will be evaluated according to the completeness and relevance of the information presented (15 points), the clarity of the presentation (5 points) and the degree to which the presentation engages the class’s interest and attention (5 points).  You are free to present in whatever manner you choose (panels, role-plays, games, lectures, handouts, videos, music, PowerPoint, internet links, etc).

Possible Topics:

1.      Islamic Mysticism (Sufism):  Explain the basic aims and methods of Sufism.  Describe how the movement became an avenue of dissent.

 2.      Islamic Music:  Identify the primary features of classical Islamic in terms of function, instrumentation, melody, rhythm, and organization.

 3.      Science & Medicine:  Describe significant Muslim innovations in mathematics, engineering, astronomy, geography, physics, navigation, or medicine.  Explain the impact of Islamic science on the development of Western civilization.

 4.      Moorish Spain:  Examine in detail Moorish achievements in architecture, philosophy, music or literature.  Describe to what extent Moorish culture influenced Western European culture.

 5.      Islamic Iran:  What significant influences did the Persians have on the development of Islamic culture?  What are the distinctive features of Iranian/Persian art & architecture?

 6.      The Ottoman Empire:  Examine in detail the characteristics and contributions of Turkish culture in areas such as architecture, painting, textiles, or ceramics.  How is this art form a reflection on Turkish and/or Islamic values?

 7.      European Colonialism:  Describe the role of western nations in the formation of modern Middle Eastern states.  Explain how Western influence has contributed to political instability in the region.

 8.      Arab-Israeli Conflict:  Describe the circumstances involved in the formation of the state of Israel.  Explain the main issues of contention between Israelis and their Arab neighbors.

 9.      Islamic Fundamentalism:  Examine origins and features of the Islamic resurgence movement.  What are the main objectives and methods of the so-called fundamentalists? 

 10.     Gender Issues:  Western criticism of Islam has often centered on the treatment of women.  Does Islam view women as inherently inferior to men?  Are there legal or cultural misogynist tendencies in Islamic countries?  On the whole, do Muslim women feel discriminated against, or is the issue a matter of cultural differences regarding gender roles?

 11.     Islam & the West:  What are some of the cultural sources of conflict between Muslims and Westerners.  What steps might be taken to reduce tensions and promote inter-cultural understanding?

Reading Assignment #1:

The purpose of the Reading Assignment Journal is threefold:

  • To help you make better use of the textbooks in gaining basic information that will lay the foundation for further discussion in class and prepare you for the additional information you will receive in class. 
  • To provide you with an opportunity to digest, assimilate, and reflect on the implications of the information you have learned. 
  • To provide you with an alternate means of demonstrating your learning (besides tests).

            You are to submit a typed journal entry of approximately two pages in length for each of the readings assigned in the Lecture and Reading Assignment Schedule.  Entries may be submitted by E-mail or hardcopy and are due at the beginning of class on the day each reading is listed on the schedule.  Because I believe your reflections on the readings contribute significantly to learning (both yours and mine), I have weighted the entries approximately equal to the exams in grading.  

            Though you may earn up to 10 points for each entry, you must demonstrate effort, comprehension, and thought in order to receive the maximum number of points.  Entries submitted later than the beginning of class will receive a maximum of 5 points.

            The content of the journal is mostly up to you.  You may summarize key events, ideas, innovations, and personalities, record interpretations or feelings you have in response to the readings, and express whether or not you agree with the authors’ assessments and interpretations. Be sure to include at the end of each entry at least one question relating to the reading that you would like to have the class and me respond to.  Hopefully most questions will be answered at some point during classes, but I will endeavor to select at least one question from the summaries to discuss in class.
