Four Ways to Remain Right with Copyright

Over the years I have had my students research copyright as part of the technology curriculum. I too have spent many hours reviewing copyright articles, attending workshops and listening to the legal murmuring of those involved with copyright. For the better part of this time I and many of my students have come away from our research with at least as much confusion about this topic as we had when we began.

However, slowly and steadily I have tried to reduce the wide variety of topics and rules to some simple rules of thumb that I could share with my students to help them remain legal as they build web sites, multimedia presentations and other technology-oriented projects. I have settled on four simple rules that, for the time being, should keep a user of technology legal, when he or she goes looking for resources. These rules are:

  1. If the resource states that the materials are free and can be used without permission, use them.
  2. If the resource states that the materials are free and can be used after meeting certain standards, then see if you meet the standards and if you do then use the materials.
  3. If the resource is copyrighted then leave it alone unless you ask and receive permission to use it.
  4. If the resource is silent on whether or not it is copyrighted, then assume it is and follow the same steps listed in number 3.

One will find, particularly on the Internet, many sites that provide free clipart, audio files, graphics and associated resources for individuals who are constructing web sites. These are the sites that I direct students to find and use. By doing so, the student is safe and can feel assured that the materials and resources they accumulate for use are legal. In the process however, it is always an appropriate courtesy to acknowledge where the resource came from (via a resource page with links or in writing) or at minimum to not claim the resource was of your own making. Sample sites include http://3bgraphics.hypermart.net/ as well as http://www.thefreesite.com/.

The Internet has literally thousands of sites that offer users resources for either a link back to the resources page or by a written acknowledgement of the use to the producer. This type of use is commonly referred to as Postcard-ware (e.g. http://members.tripod.com/~hrgraf/) or link-ware (e.g. http://robinsweb.com/art/art-set1.html). The point to these type of resources are that the authors desire to know who is using their materials and/or are exchanging use for advertising. In either case, the owners terms should be met before using the materials.

As one of my students recently wrote in a research paper, "Virtually everything in the world is copyrighted." The student is correct. The whole purpose of copyright is to "promote science and the useful arts, by securing authors … the exclusive right to their [work]" (U.S. Constitution, Art. I, Sec. 8, Cl. 8). By asking permission prior to using a product of someone else's labor we are acknowledging their effort. We also provide a means for the author, creator or copyright owner to profit from her or his effort, or give him or her to opportunity to acknowledge and grant permission for our use of their product. To do otherwise is theft.

Finally, just because a document, web site or image is not marked with the © symbol does not mean that it isn't copyrighted. Virtually everything created after April 1, 1989, marked as being copyrighted or not, is copyrighted. This is a result of Congress' adoption of the Berne Copyright Convention rules. These rules indicate that anything that is in tangible form (e.g. written, painted, produced) is copyrighted immediately, whether registered or not (http://www.templetons.com/brad/copymyths.html). When surfing the Internet one will find that the vast majority of pages have no indication of being copyrighted. Just because this is the case, one cannot assume that the materials are public domain. The correct assumption is that the materials are copyrighted and in order to use them, permission is required.

I feel confident that if one will follow these four simple rules of thumb that it will be possible to remain right with copyright.
