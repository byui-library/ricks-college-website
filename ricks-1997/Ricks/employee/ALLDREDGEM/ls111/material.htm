Introduction

Knowing more about library materials leads to efficient use, proper handling, and appreciation for the Information Age.

Tools are built into library resources to allow the user to find information quickly. Finding information is easier today than at any other time. Efficient use is a result of knowing how to use tools to find information quickly.

Abusing resources can be a result of ignorance. The way books are made helps understand what will harm the binding. Computers are not easily abused. Understanding what they will do and won't do will lower frustration levels.

Elementary education majors often are required to take classes on using library materials. Textbook based instruction incorporates library skills into the curriculum. A variety of tests measure what students know about using libraries. Study skills which should be mastered in elementary school include the ability to read maps, to know alphabetical order, and to use a catalog and indexes.

Librarians do not read every word in a book to describe it. Technical reading is a term used to look at the parts of library material to describe it. Speed reading and directed reading are methods used to find the information that would be most helpful. This chapter can be summarized: Look for help in the material used. Try to use materials responsibly.

Before the printing press every book was copied by scribes working for rulers or religious groups. Some of the oldest copies of the Bible are masterpieces of art. While copying the text, the scribe would illuminate (or decorate with gold or colored letters and pictures) the manuscript. Rare books or special collections would be the place to find an illuminated Bible.

The size and format of books became standardized as the printing press was used. A single sheet folded was known as a "folio." A sheet folded into fourths was " quarto." The most popular size became half the quarto size. These terms and the word "oversize" are used to describe books which are too large for regular shelves. A plus sign ( "+" ) on the spine above the call number is used here to indicate that a book is oversize. "Atlas," "OV2," and "OV3" are abbreviations that appear in the catalog for these large materials.

A delightful activity in a church lesson manual has children fold a paper into eight pieces. Each folded box is numbered. The idea was to illustrate how the Book of Mormon was published. These folded sections were cut and bound. The Book of Mormon manuscript was secured so that only enough for a folded section would be available to the printer at a time. Contrast these early editions with the latest publications with their varied formats and tools.

Binding

Early books were sewn together by hand. The threads were attached to boards and covered with leather. Paper was placed over the boards to cover the threads.

As mass production methods were used, shortcuts were developed. Today many books are held together only by glue.

Looking at the spine or back of a book is the way to determine how the book was bound. Since the binding is intended to hold sheets a specific direction, bending the spine the opposite direction may break threads or glue which hold the book together.

The spine may have labels which describe the contents of the book. Often a short form of the name of the author or the title of the book may appear on the spine. Libraries often mark the spine with a symbol or call number indicating where the book is placed in the library's collection. A call number is the alphabetical and numerical combination used to file or shelve materials.

The end papers cover threads or cloth which are attached to the boards used to bind the book. These pages often have maps, tables, graphs, or rules. Book ownership and due dates are often stamped on these pages.

Food and beverages are restricted in libraries. Besides the staining that happens when the book is read while someone is eating or drinking, insects are attracted by crumbs and spills. Some insects find the glue in bindings as appealing as food crumbs.

Preliminary Pages

Preliminary pages precede the text of the book. A fly leaf is a blank page which precedes or follows the text. A fly leaf may be used by the library for circulation or inventory purposes. Depending on the point of view these pages are part of the binding process or of the preliminary pages.

The preliminary pages are not always in a specific order. A variety of sources suggest the following order: half title, frontispiece, title, printer’s imprint, copyright notice, ISBN, dedication, acknowledgements, table of contents, list of illustrations, list of abbreviations, introduction, foreword or preface, introduction, and errata. Preliminary pages often are numbered with small Roman numerals, if any pagination is given.

The half-title page only gives part of the information found on the title page. It gives information about the series to which the book belongs. A series is a collection of separate but related books. (The Work and the Glory) is an example of a series. A list of books by the author which are not necessarily related may also be given on this page.

A frontispiece is a preliminary page with an illustration relating to the subject of the book. Art books and biographies use a portrait or art masterpiece for this illustration. The signature on the frontispiece is usually printed rather than an actual autograph. A number of amateur book collectors have been disappointed to discover that they did not have a rare autographed book.

The title page is used to describe a book in a catalog or bibliography. Book publishing and cataloging organizations have developed international standards for bibliographical description and references. The source for an international standard bibliographic description or ISBD for a book is the title page. The information distinguishes the book from others printed at different times and includes the following:

1. Title with subtitle

A subtitle is a descriptive phrase explaining the scope. Subtitles are common on scholarly publications.
2. Name of author and other creators, editor, illustrator, translator, etc.

3. Publication information

Imprint is another term for the place of publication, name of the publisher, and the date of publication. At one time an embossed imprint was put in the front or back of books to authenticate its origin. Diplomas and transcripts often have an embossed stamp.
The verso or back of the title page gives the history of the book and its imprint. This history includes copyright information. An edition has the same information each time it is printed. Edition is a term used to describe revisions. Textbooks are notorious for changing editions when only errata type changes are made. Printings all have the same material. Additional printings of an edition with their dates are often listed. The latest copyright date is the time when the book was arranged in its current form. Rare book dealers often look for first editions, unique printings, and autographed copies.

Think of an edition as a batch of cookies with the same ingredients in the whole batch. Printings are similar to putting a pan of cookies in the oven. Refrigerator cookies could have different dates for each pan of cookies just as printing may have different years.

As part of the copyright process, the Library of Congress may provide cataloguing -in - publication or CIP. The CIP appears as a sample library catalog record on the verso or back of the title page. The CIP is based on an unpublished work. When the publication is complete call numbers may be different.

CIP often gives unique numbers assigned to the book. The ISBN or International Standard Book Number is a unique number used to identify the publisher and the specific edition of book. A Library of Congress number (93-18836) is a number assigned when the book arrives for copyright with the first two digits reflecting the year of copyright. Library of Congress classification numbers (PZ7.S2744Gr 1993) start with letters. Dewey decimal classification numbers starting with numbers are often given. Books for young children may have the letter "E" instead of a Dewey decimal number to indicate that it is an " easy" book. "DC" and a number will tell which Dewey decimal classification edition was used. Examples of CIP can be seen in newer books. A good strategy is to use the subjects on the CIP to find similar material.

Near the CIP appears the symbol for copyright. Material is copyrighted whether a notice appears or not. The law on copyright has existed from the earliest days of our nation. People who create something new in the United States of America can protect their invention or innovation through copyright or patent. They can charge practically anything that they want for someone else to use their protected creation. Sheet music may cost less than a dollar to photocopy but you should be very careful about copying it rather than borrowing or purchasing your own copy.

Fair use is a legal phrase used to describe appropriate copying. Fair use seldom includes copying the entire work but only portions. Copying a single article from a magazine for individual educational use is usually allowed. Some hymns in our hymn book may be copied for choir use while other composers do not allow that use. The verso (or back) of the title page of our hymn book gives an explanation. The creator or his agent is really the only person who can give permission for some uses, including copying the entire work. Those who have sought copyright permission can tell you how difficult getting permission to make copies may become from some individuals or groups. On the other hand some groups or individuals are generous.

The role of libraries is to provide materials for the public to share within the copyright restrictions. When a change is made in the copyright law, librarians across the nation lobby for your right to use copyrighted material for your educational needs. If you have any questions on copyright, several guides have been purchased which will explain fair use.

The dedication was originally the way an author repaid his patron. A letter of dedication written to an author’s sponsor was included in the work two or three hundred years ago. The dedication has evolved from one of financial support to one of respect or recommendation.

Acknowledgements is another formalized way to thank those who assisted the author. Copyright permission to use excerpts may be summarized in acknowledgements. An author may be specific or general in expressing appreciation for help received.

A table of contents and list of illustrative material should not be overlooked in research. The contents outlines the arrangement of the book. Without an index the contents may be the only shortcut to find information. In some cases, the table of contents may be more helpful than an index. A list of symbols and abbreviations may follow the list of illustrations.

Foreword or preface are pages the author or publisher use to give explanations. The reason the author wrote the book may be given. Afterthoughts may be included. The scope, history, purpose, audience and arrangement of the book are often explained in the preface. The scope may describe changes and additions in this edition.

Introduction is a short essay preparing the reader for the text to follow. The introduction may be written by someone other than the author.

Errata is a list of corrections to be made in the text. These are usually noticed after the text was printed. Government and legal printers often send errata sheets or pamphlets.  They may be pasted into the preliminary pages or opposite the place where the error was made. Legal publications often have a pocket for errata or supplement pamphlets. The government often has errata sheets for statistical publications. Be sure to check the front and back of the publication or sheets filed next to the document for these when using government sources.

Text

The text often is arranged by subject in chapters. Reference books may be arranged alphabetically by small topic or chronologically by time. Picture books may have no words, only illustrations. Text is limited only by the imagination of the creator.

The text in computer programs may include " hypertext" which can be selected or "clicked" to go to another topic or part of the program or another Internet address. The text may be illustrated with graphics or examples. Some computer programs have animation and audible illustrations. Hypermedia is a term that may be used to describe symbols that can be clicked to obtain media illustrations.

Auxiliary or reference pages

Auxiliary pages contain additional information. Scholarly books contain more types of auxiliary or reference information than popular titles.

Documentation may be given in auxiliary pages. Footnotes are usually at the bottom of the page of text. Endnotes are placed in a section of notes at the end of the chapter or book. Footnotes or endnotes are explanations or abbreviated source citations. In electronic publications these may be in hypertext which will move the reader to endnotes.

A bibliography is a more complete description of sources or recommended reading. Annotated bibliographies have descriptive notes in addition to the title page information. Subject bibliographies list items on a certain subject. Author bibliographies list an author's works. Other terms are used to describe non-printed material lists. A discography lists audio recordings. A filmography lists motion pictures or visual recordings.

Scholarly publications usually have one or more kinds of bibliographies. Graduate studies require review of existing research. Scholars often annotate bibliographies to provide a more complete description. A directory of organizations interested in a field may also be given to obtain the latest information.

An appendix contains supplementary material. It may be referred to but not explained by the text. An appendix may contain lists, statistics, addresses, or other helpful information. For example, scholarly publications often have tables or charts which were only briefly described in the text.

A glossary defines technical terms or foreign words. The glossary covers words which are not part of the basic vocabulary of the book's audience.

An index to the book is a list of topics discussed in the text. The index may be subdivided into smaller topics. A comprehensive index includes all levels of text, not just main headings. Indexes may cover only people, places, and subjects or a combination of these types of information in the text.

Other Printed materials

Libraries have many types of printed materials besides books. Periodicals, newspapers, looseleaf binders, pamphlets, annual reports, microform, and computer databases are examples of material that is in print but not book form.

These non book materials may not be fully catalogued by other libraries. At this time everything that is currently received at this library is catalogued. Our cataloging software, Horizon combines periodicals, government documents, microform, computer files, and books in the same catalog.
 

Periodicals

Periodicals includes popular magazines and scholarly journals. Title page information is usually in small type on a table of contents or inside the front cover. Title page information includes the address of the publisher, telephone number or Internet address. 

Serials also include information unique to serials with the statement of ownership. The frequency that the periodical is published is often described as annually, monthly, weekly, daily, and so forth. The issue may be given an enumeration, such as, volume and issue number. The copy usually has a chronological label. For example, quarterly publications could be labeled the Winter 1996, Spring 1996, Summer 1996, and Fall 1996 issues. The cost of back issues and current subscriptions may be given. 

Periodicals change titles and frequency. The cover title may include graphics which confuse the reader as to the title. A unique number assigned to serials is ISSN or International Standard Serial Number. The official name of a government publication, U.S. Department of State Dispatch may be indexed by the abbreviated name Dispatch. The eight digit ISSN helps identify publications that may have different titles in different bibliographies or indexes. 
 
 

Microforms

Microforms include a variety of microscopic photographic images on film. Microfilm is a roll of uncut film with these microscopic images. Microfiche is a 4 by 6 inch card with rows of these small images. Microprint is a 6 by 9 inch card; microcard is 3 by 5. 

Magazines and newspapers may be preserved on microforms to save space. Rare documents may appear on microforms. The United States government has started to issue more and more of its publications on microfiche. 

Machines to read these microscopic images are available near where these microforms are stored. Filing cabinets with drawers are where these microform materials are stored. In the government document, periodical, and family history areas are found microform cabinets.. 
 
 

Computer Databases

    Computers are not easily broken. If users do not follow the signs on the machines, they can make the computer unusable to the next patron. Do not unplug them or log off unless you logged on. Leave the computer as usable as you found it. If you have trouble report the problems to those who wear name tags or vests indicating that they are library employees.

Automation of printing has led to a variety of computerized library tools. Many libraries have a computerized catalog. Online services may include databases that can be searched over the telephone line or Internet cable to a computer with the data. Some of these databases can be searched using CD ROM or compact disc read only memory players. A single compact disk can hold more than 250,000 pages of text. More computerized materials will be seen in the future.

Internet is causing some publications to become available only in electronic format. Some institutions are trying to preserve these electronic publications. Many e-mail systems have archives to record what was posted or mailed at given times. Discussion has been made about whether an author should be allowed to purge their contributions to these archives. The "delete" feature on an e-mail system will only remove the publication from the files managed by the system not from every system to which it was sent. E-mail addresses look like this ABC12345@ricks.edu. Internet web pages have a URL address that looks like this http://www.ricks.edu. URL is the abbreviation for Uniform Resource Locator.

Look for help keys or screens. Under DOS or Disc Operating Systems the "F1" function key was usually the help key. Under Windows systems provide menu access to help and tools. Tutorials and tips for searches are invaluable in finding computerized information. Windows and Internet software use hypertext to provide additional options. When a mouse approaches hypertext or an image with hypertext or hypermedia links, the arrow turns into a hand. The hypertext or image can be clicked to go to an explanation, more detail, or another database location.

Microforms and computer software often involves printed documents and is placed in this print materials section. However, the contents of microforms and computer software may be non print or a combination of print and non print. The very format of microforms and computer software is non print.

Non print materials

Non print materials are often those which are audiovisual or which appeal to the eye and ear. Audiovisual materials include pictures and art reproductions; film (slides, filmstrips, motion pictures, transparencies, jpeg, GIF); maps; audio recordings (phonograph, compact disk, and cassette); sheet music; and video recordings. AV is an abbreviation used for "audiovisual." These materials often are listed in a catalog separate from the one used for books. At this library, these audiovisual materials are now being listed in the same catalog as books. Videocassettes also appear in a printed list by the video viewing window. Compact music disc lists are also on the circulation desk.

Equipment is available to use the audiovisual materials in the library on every floor. Headsets may be checked out at the circulation desk as needed. Other equipment check out has some restrictions or charges. The media services department schedules and delivers AV equipment for use in campus classrooms. Ricks has facilities in the library to produce your own AV materials. The latest information about what is available here in audiovisual equipment can be obtained from Media Services.
 

Summary

Library materials are varied but some common characteristics exist. Look for indexes, table of contents, or explanations on how to use library materials. Computer programs have help screens. Some programs have tutorials which teach you the basics. Sessions on using library tools may be offered individually or as groups.
