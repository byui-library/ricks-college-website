Specifications:

Description:  A critique is an argument that makes a judgment about somethingBa work of art or literature, a policy or procedure, an argument, in short, anything that can be evaluated.

An Outline for Your Critique:

 

 

Paragraph #1:  Introduction

What is the issue at question?  And why is this issue of interest to your reader?

Begin your essay with something that captures your reader=s attention.

 

 

Paragraph #2:  Summary

How does X address this issue?

What are X=s reasons?

Summarize X=s argument in about 200-250 words; present X=s views objectively.

 

 

Paragraph #3: Ambiguity

Are X=s key terms clearly defined?

Begin this paragraph with a topic sentence that states your impressions of X=s definitions. Then explain why you think as you do.

 

 

Paragraph #4: Assumptions

What are X=s assumptions?

Are these assumptions acceptable?

Begin this paragraph with a topic sentence that states your impressions of X=s assumptions.  Then explain why you think as you do.

 

 

Paragraph #5:  Evidence

What is X=s evidence?

Is this evidence persuasive?

Begin this paragraph with a topic sentence that states your impressions of X=s evidence. Then explain why you think as you do.

 

 

Paragraph #6: Fallacies

Does X commit any fallacies of logic?

Begin this paragraph with a topic sentence that states your impressions of X=s reasoningBsound or fallacious. Then explain why you think as you do.

 

 

Conclusion

What is your final assessment of the overall strengths and weaknesses of X=s argument?

Begin your paragraph with your overall claim and explain whether or not your find X a writer you can trust.  Your paragraph may begin with a claim that sounds something like this:  

 

A.  X=s argument is seriously flawed because it is based on a false assumption, lacks evidence to support its claim, slants information unfairly, and illustrates the straw man fallacy.

 

B.  X=s argument is persuasive because it is based on sound assumptions, compelling evidence, legitimate emotional appeal, and a fair consideration of opposing viewpoints.

 

C.  While X=s argument is based on ample evidence, it is weakened because of debatable assumptions, unfair slanting, and dogmatism.

 

D.  Even though X=s argument lacks evidence, the writer=s ideas have merit because of reasonable assumptions, compelling emotional appeals, and the integrity of its writer.

English 211:  Writing a Summary

 

Specifications:

Description:  A summary presents the substance of a body of material in a condensed

form or by reducing it to its main points

Purpose:  to report as a summary two Thinking essays

Length:  250 words per summary (give or take five words), double-spaced

Due:  February 6, 2001, at the beginning of class.

 

Characteristics of Summary:

1.  A good summary is brief.  Summaries achieve their brevity by eliminating details, examples, facts, and by reporting the Asum@ of what the supporting material adds up to.

 

2.  A good summary is objective.  Leave out your own opinions and focus on reporting the author=s important points.  Write in third person.  Use pronouns like he, she, they, and nouns like the writer, the researcher).  Avoid pronouns like I, we, and you.

 

3.  A good summary is mostly paraphrase.  Think of yourself as a translator: you are translating the author=s language into your own.  If you decide to quote, keep quotations brief and always place them within quotation marks.

 

4.  A good summary is written in the present tense.  Write as if the idea is unfolding for your readers as they read.  However, if you are recounting an incident that is truly Ahistorically past,@ past tense is acceptable.

 

5.  A good summary is coherent and clear.  Too often summaries sound like lists of ideas.  Use transitional expressions to clarify the logical relationship among the ideas you are reporting.

In ADrinking Hemlock and Other Nutritional Matters,@ Harold J. Morowitz, a professor of biophysics at Yale, argues that students should be taught Athe art of inquiry@ at every level of the curriculumCgrade school through college.  Presently, education is mainly the process of teachers transferring subject matter to students.  In this process, however, students rarely learn where valid knowledge comes from or how it is established.  Without this understanding, students are ill-equipped to deal with the many false ideas they confront and are easy prey for hucksters and demagogues.  Teachers avoid the subject of inquiry because it=s dangerous. When young people learn to ask basic questions like AHow do you know that what you are saying is indeed a statement of fact?@ they may also begin to question the basis of education, religion, government, and morality.  Teachers and parents alike do not appreciate this scrutiny.  Ancient Athenians, for example, condemned Socrates to drink hemlock for Acorrupting their young@Cteaching them to Auncover assumptions and question certainties.@  In spite of dangers like these, the benefits of learning to scrutinize knowledge are less painful than having to listen to media Adrivel@ and watch Ayoung people marching off in lock step and never questioning where they are going.@  Indeed, valid knowledge is hard to come by.  Yet, for their own well-being, students need to understand the arduous processes that distinguish fact from cocktail party prattle and dangerous demagoguery.  To the traditional curriculum of  reading, writing, and >rithmetic, Morowitz says, Aadd a fourth R, >Reality.=@ (250 words)

English 211:  Writing a Synthesis

 

AFacts are stupid things until brought into connection with some general law.@

Louis Agassiz as quoted by Samuel Scudder

 

ANo scientific theory is a collection of facts. . . .  Science finds order and meaning in our experience.@

Jacob Bronowski

 

AData, data everywhere, but not a thought to think.@

Theodore Roszak

 

AThe student who merely cows, robs himself, without knowing it, of his education and his soul.  The student who only bulls robs himself, as he knows full well, of the joys of inductive discovery.@

William G. Perry, Jr.

 

AI finally figured out that the students had memorized everything, but they didn=t know what anything meant.@

Richard P. Feynman

 

Specifications:

Description:  When you synthesize, you discover the general law that makes sense of

 Astupid@ facts (Agassiz).  You find order and meaning among data and information that may appear at first to be only randomly ordered (Bronowski and Feynman).  You discover the thought buried in the sterile heap of data (Roszak).  You infer the proper relationship between Acowing and bulling@ (Perry).  However a writer may describe this synthesis process, you are making an inferenceBthat is, you are exploring evidence, data, facts, examples, illustrations, statistics, information, and Acows@ to discover the ideas, conclusions, laws, hypotheses, theories, theses, and Abulls@ that can reasonably be derived from them.  As you well know from experience and our class reading, this is not easy.

Purpose: to write an essay that develops an original idea and that integrates at least

four Thinking sources

Length: four to five pages, double-spaced

Due: February 20, 2001, at the beginning of class

Audience: Your classmates at Ricks (Imagine your essay appearing in the Scroll)

 

Procedure: 

Whenever you need to write a paper demonstrating you are capable of making your own inferencesBthinking your own thoughtsBa strategy like the one may help you:

 



1.                  Read in the assigned area or an area of personal interest.  For your paper, I would like you to read assigned essays from Thinking About Thinking.  These essays are your data bank. 

2.                  As you read, identify principal issues and sub-issues.  Every non-fiction text addresses questions.  The main question the text addresses is the writer=s principal issue.  (The answer to this question, by the way, is the writer=s thesis.)  As you read each assigned essay, look for this principal issue. Also look for the writer=s sub-issues, other interesting questions the text implies.  Keep a list of these questions as you move from essay to essay.   Try to pull a few questions from each essay.

 

3.                  From your list of questions, select an issue you especially like.  This question will become the principal issue of your paper.  Make certain you care about this question even if you don=t yet know how you would like to answer it.

 

4.                  Take your issue into your source texts.  With your favorite issue in mind, ask how each of your sources helps to answer your question.  As you look for information, watch for relevant examples, facts, data, statistics, definitions, comparisons, anecdotes, evidence, contrasting views.  Mark the most helpful passages or keep notes on cards.  Perhaps not every text will address every question, but as gems sparkle differently in various lights, so texts yield different meanings when seen from different Aquestion perspectives.@  The point is that your source essays will yield different ideas in relationship to different questions and provide the information you need to answer your question.  If your sources don=t give you the information you need, expand your research.

 

5.                   After considering the information your sources yield, answer your issue question.  To arrive at a good answer, ask yourself questions like these: AWhat idea does the evidence in my reading best support?@  AFrom my data what inference can I reasonably make?@   Your answerBin one sentenceBis your thesis.  Remember too that personal experience often provides another good source of information.  For this assignment, if you=d like, you may include your own experiences to support your thesis.  Many essayists use personal experience to develop their ideas.  Think of writers like Feynman, Calandra, Hinckley, Morowitz, Scudder, and Plummer who draw extensively upon their own lives to make their ideas interesting.)

 

6.                  Map or sketch a plan for your essay.  Before writing your essay, try to draw it. In the early stages of writing a paper, many students find traditional outlines too linear and confining.  They prefer to capture the essay they hope to write in a sketchCa spider map, a chain diagram, a comparison matrix, a network tree, a fishbone chart, a process configuration.  Such pictures help you to organize your ideas and visualize a preliminary structureCfor many, the most frustrating task of writing.

 

7.                  Draft your essay.  Using your map as a guide, write through your ideas to see what you have. Remember ample supporting examples will make your thesis lively and engaging. Keep track of your sources according to the MLA format we will discuss in class.

 

8.                   Revise your essay; keep your audience in mind.

English 211:  Writing an Argument

 

Specifications:

Description:  An argument is an essay that takes a stand on an issue.  Its intent is to encourage others to see your point of view or prompt others to an action you see as desirable.

Purpose:  to write a paper related to your major that argues for a position or a course of action you believe in

Length: eight to ten pages (ten to fifteen sources, MLA format)

Audience:   others interested in your major

Due: April 24, 2001, at the beginning of class.

 

Procedure:  What follows is an often-used structure for an argument.

                     


1)  Introduction and Presentation of Issue

          Present the issue at question and show your reader how it is relevant to their experience.  Remain neutral.

 

2)  Background

          Present whatever information your reader needs to know in order to better understand the argument you are about to present.

 

3)  Opposing View

          Present a summary of your opponent=s argumentBboth the claim and reasons.  This summary might be based on one writer=s presentation of the opposing view, or it might be based on a synthesis of several writers= presentation of the opposing view. Present these views objectively.

 

4) Concessions and Refutations

Explain which part of your opponent=s argument you are comfortable accepting and why.  Then explain which parts of your opponent=s argument you are unable to accept and why.

 

5)  Lines of Argument

          After discussing your opponent=s views, lay out your own argument.  Present your claim and your reasons.  Support your points with ample evidence.  Generally speaking, the more controversial your claim, the more evidence you will need.

 

6)  Objections

If necessary, address objections of those who may oppose your argument.  Clarify why your argument is stronger in spite of these objections.

 

7) Conclusion

          Climax your paper in a concluding paragraph.

 

8)  Works Cited

          Include MLA citations for each source included in your paper.   Briefly annotate each source with a verb phrase.

 

9) Summary

Summarize your argument in a paragraph of about 250 words.
