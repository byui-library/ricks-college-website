How to make Shadows

in Adobe Photoshop

1. First open the file you want to use.

This will be the file over which you make a shadow. Then, in the view menu, select Show layers to view the layers pallette . . . if it says hide layers, the layers pallette is already open.

If you're following along, please open grosvner2.gif to do your work in. In this exercise, we're pretending to do a shadow of Utah and the word Utah, perhaps for a travel brochure.

We will, in this exercise, learn another way of doing selection. We will learn about shadows, borders on selections, duotones, contrast and blurring for effect.

Here's what we're aiming for:

Here's what we start with, a picture of Grosvener Arch near Escalante, Utah:

2. Open an image from which you will make a selection.

Then select the portion of that image. Copy the selection.

Here's the image with the tool box overlaying it. If you look closely, in the center/middle of the image there's a selection of Utah in the Image: If you're following along, this is antelope2.gif.

3. To create the Utah shape, you could use a variety of methods, but I've decided on the polygon lasso tool.

That's the tool shown in this image:

If the lasso tool looks round, then double click on it and select the tool with straight lines.

The difference between these two tools is the regular, rounded lasso tool allows you to make a selection in whatever shape you can draw. You run it around the edge of some shape, making it circular or whatever suits your imagination and hand control. With the other, straight lasso tool, you will make a straight lines that join up. Photoshop calls this the polygon lasso tool.

You change direction with this polygon lasso tool with one click of the mouse, and change again with another until you've come back around to the starting point to complete your work.

After making a selection, under the edit menu, select paste.

Before pasting this image onto our orignal, let's manipulate the original image of the arch so we can get it how we want it, so click on the Arch picture again.

 

4. Convert the background image to grayscale.

Under the Image menu, select the mode submenu and several choices will appear, select the second one down -- Grayscale. It will ask you if you want to discard the color information. You do, so click on OK.

Your image will look like this:

5. Convert the grayscale to duotone.

Duotone is applying colors to the black and white image. A monotone would replace a color of your choosing for the black. A duotone uses a combination of the two. The tritone lets you use a third.

To convert to a duotone, under the Image Menu, Mode submenu, select duotone. That's the one under grayscale. Duotone will not be available unless you photo is already in grayscale.

This dialog box will appear:

If you don't like those colors, you may pick others by clicking on the actual color in the dialog box.

At the top of the box, it says, Type, you may pick types other than duotones, such as monotones, by clicking here.

When you click on the color to pick another color, a dialog box like this will appear:

Without a long explanation about how to select colors in Photoshop, know that if you change where it says book to another method, a different sort of dialog will appear. Pantone is a collections of hundreds of colors -- much like paint swatches at the paint store -- you can match exactly on your computer. The long rainbow in the middle of this box shows an approximate color -- click there first -- and then click on the exact match you want at left. Then click OK.

A version something like this appeared:

I want the image to be more dramatic, so I am making the dark portions stand out more and the light ones be more apparent. This is called changing the contrast.

6. Alter the contrast of the duotone.

Under the Image menu, select the Adjust submenu -- the second one down. Pick brightness/contrast. When you do, this dialog will appear:

In this example, I strongly strengthened the contrast and didn't adjust the brightness at all. This was the effect, basically. See how much more dramatic this was. (A brief confession here is needed. In designing this description, I failed to create the uncontrasted image and went back and did it later. Accordingly, the hue is less brown in the above image -- still, you get the idea cleanly.)

7. Convert the image, under Image menu, mode submenu, back to RGB color.

If you don't when you paste in your other image -- the Utah shape -- it will also appear as a duotone.

You will notice no changes to the picture on your computer screen.

8. Paste the selection to the underlying image.

Do that with Command V or under the edit menu, paste.

It will now look something like this:

9. Under the Layer Menu, select duplicate layer (make sure the recenly pasted layer is selected in the layers pallette.)

See description of layers in other lessons.

With the newly created layer selected -- It should say something like layer one copy -- load selection under you select menu. Dotted lines will appear in the shape of the state.

Then, under the edit menu, pick fill -- fill with black at 100 percent opacity.

Because this new black layer is hiding behind the orignal state image, you won't see anything just yet.

10. Another method would be to create a new layer and make sure in the layers pallette it is beneath the one you just created. Skip to step 11 if you've successfully completed step 9.

See description of layers in other lessons.

Then, with the pasted layer open, under the selection menu Load Selection. That will select the who map of the state -- it will be surrounded by the dotted lines.

Then, in your layers pallette, select the recently created layer -- you will notice no change in your computer image. Then, fill with black under the edit menu.

Still, you will notice no change in the picture because your image is hiding beneath it.

11. Select the lower of these two layers -- the black layer -- by clicking on its picture in the layers pallette.

12. Select the move tool.

In your tools pallette, it's the one in the upper right-hand corner.

13. Move the underneath layer slightly so it sits behind the upper layer at a slight angle.

It is as if the upper layer were floating slightly above the image, casting a black shadow beneath.

14. Next, under the filter menu, go to blur and under the blur menu go to gaussian blur.

Here is the dialog box:

You will note that Gaussian blur has a variety of radius options. Pick a number that suits your taste and needs. The higher the number, the more fuzzy the shadow becomes. Apply the filter and you will have a shadow look.

The following image shows what the shadow looks like without the overlying image or text. (Of course, ignore the words, we haven't got to that yet.):

15. Next, apply a border to the state image -- not the shadow image.

To apply a border, load selection -- that's under the select menu -- of the state image on the layer that has the state. Check your layers pallette to see that your in the right place.

Under your select menu, go to the Modify submenu and then pick border. This dialog box will appear:

Pick a number that fits your taste. Two pixels might be better than four for our purposes, though. Click on OK.

Then you should have a border appear something like this -- parallel lines running around your selection. (This is a border of six pixels to help you see the effect better.)

As you've done before, now simply fill that selection with black under the edit menu-- or some other color or even apply a filter -- and the results will appear something like this: (Yours, of course, will have the background still in place.)

 

16. Using the text tool, type in the word Utah.

(You will have selected the appropriate color from your state image you want Utah to be.)

Putting text on images is discussed in other lessons.

Then, follow the steps in 9 or 10 again to create your shadow of a state.

Complete steps 10 through 13.

Though you could, skip step 14 in this exercise to give the image a cleaner look.

 

17. Your layers pallette should look something like this

(The name layer 5 is not important here. Yours will probably say Layer 4 or some name you gave it already.)

18. Flatten the image under the layers pallette menu (upper right hand corner arrow of the layers pallette).

You then have a finished image:

You might change the mode to Indexed color so you can save it as a gif for web publication or you might convert it to CMYK for printing.
