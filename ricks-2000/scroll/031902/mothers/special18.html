Typhoid Mary infects history

by Sarah Lunt
LUN99013@BYUI.EDU
Scroll Staff

In March 1907, George Soper, a civil engineer with extensive experience in researching typhoid fever outbreaks, tracked Mary Mallon to the house of Walter Bowen, where she was employed as a cook.

Upon telling Mallon that he suspected she was a carrier of the dreaded typhoid and requesting stool, urine and blood samples, the outraged woman ran after him with a carving knife.

Thus began Mallon’s long fight against the government.

Mary Mallon was born Sept. 23, 1869, in Cookstown, Ireland. At 15 years of age she emigrated to the United States. Finding that she had a knack for cooking and that the job paid better, she began working as a cook.

From 1900 to 1907, Mary was employed in seven households. In those homes, 22 people contracted typhoid and one young girl died. When Soper found this evidence, he was convinced that Mary was a “healthy carrier.”

Soper was finally able to bring Mary into custody by force. She was convicted and isolated to North Brother Island for two years. After this time the government agreed to release her on the condition that she not cook.

However, when there was a typhoid outbreak of 25 people, in which two died, Soper suspected that Mary had not adhered to the restrictions. He found her working as a cook under the pseudonym Mrs. Brown.

Mallon was immediately returned to North Brother Island for the last 23 years of her life, where she began to help in the hospital. She suffered a massive stroke in 1932, which paralyzed her. She died Nov. 11, 1938.

Typhoid fever now affects 17 million people throughout the world every year and causes 600 thousand deaths, according to www.who.int.

The disease, caused by Salmonella typhi, is characterized by a sudden fever, headache, nausea, loss of appetite, constipation and diarrhea.

Food and water contaminated by human waste of patients will transmit the disease. Patients can transmit typhoid as long as the bacteria is in their systems, and are infectious prior to and during the first week of convalescence.
