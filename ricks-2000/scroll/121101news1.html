Teachers give advice on how to stay cold-free

by Megan Perkins
per00008@byui.edu
Scroll Staff

AP Photo Archive
Finals, papers, projects, tests, work, transferring, scheduling, exercising, classes, sleeping, shopping, Christmasing — as if students do not have enough to worry about. At the end of the semester when it seems as though a mile-long to-do list must be completed before you can even begin to think about counting down the days until Christmas break, just remember this: Do not get sick.

‘Tis the season for caroling, playing in the snow and getting attacked by Rhinos — rhinoviruses, that is — or, as it is better known, the common cold.

Adults have an average of two to four colds per year, according to the American Lung Association. People experience far more colds between the months of September and May. Lynn Perkes, a professor in the Health Science Department of BYU-Idaho, attributes this not to being out it the cold weather, but to being inside out of the cold where the cold virus can run rampantly from person to person in a concentrated area.

In order to avoid the dreaded cold, Perkes and Marie Parkinson, also a professor in the Health Science Department of BYU-I, agreed on three basic principles students should follow to ensure good health.

“Get good nutrition, sleep and physical activity, all of which serve to strengthen the immune system,” Perkes said.

The most important of these, and perhaps the most difficult for students to follow — especially during the culmination of the year when it seems projects and finals are piling up — is getting adequate sleep, Parkinson said.

The average person needs 7 - 8 hours of sleep a night, Parkinson said, although some say they only need five or six and many function on much less.

“You do need at least 6 - 7 hours of sound sleep a night,” Parkinson said.

She recommended that students get their homework done early so they can maximize their sleep time and not lie in bed awake thinking about it.

Getting good nutrition is the next most important of the three, Parkinson said. Students need to make sure they are eating plenty of fresh fruits and vegetables each day.

Exercise, the next step in the stay-healthy process, seems like an easy thing to cut out in a day filled with tests and studying, but it is vital to maintaining good health. Parkinson recommends that students get at least 30 minutes of exercise four times a week.

Another positive benefit of exercise is its ability to relieve stress, according to www.mentalhealth.com.

Numerous studies have shown that stress is directly related to health, Perkes said.

He encouraged students to learn how to handle stress effectively if they want to stay healthy because stress weakens the immune system.

“Don’t stress too bad over finals,” Perkes said.

Students should “cut out wasted time in lives and schedules.,” he said.

“It simply comes down to a better use of time and adjustment of priorities,” Perkes said.

He and Parkinson both agreed that students should have been prioritizing their schedules from the beginning of the semester.

“Catch-up doesn’t work,” Parkinson said.

Another preventative step to maintaining good health is to wash hands several times a day, Perkes said.
It is nearly impossible to avoid indoor places where sick people are because of the weather; students should be sure to avoid things like rubbing their eyes or touching their mouth with contaminated hands, Perkes said.

If students do feel a cold coming on, Perkes and Parkinson said to drink plenty of fluids, get lots of rest and continue to have good nutrition.

“Preventative medicine is the only good medicine,” Parkinson said.
