A Trio of Responsibilities

As a supervisor you have responsibilities in three areas:

To Management:

  • Support and carry out management's goals.
  • Get work done on time.
  • Follow existing rules and policies.
  • Stay within a budget.

To Your Work Group:

  • Represent management's needs.
  • See that they're treated fairly.
  • Recommend them for advancement when opportunities develop.
  • Communicate clearly what you expect from them.
  • Listen to their needs and act on them when appropriate.

To Yourself:

  • Recognize your limitations.
  • Take care of stress as it arises.
  • Communicate your needs to management.
  • Keep yourself challenged and involved.

What Do Employees Want?

There are certain things that everyone wants and needs. These requirements are called a hierarchy, because before each one can be achieved, all the others before it must be fulfilled. For instance, you can't think about belonging until you've accomplished survival and a level of security. This hierarchy of needs is based on Maslow's Hierarchy.

Survival
     In workplace terms, this means getting paid enough to survive. It's the most basic need.

Security
     The assurance that your job will still be there tomorrow.

Belonging
     A need to be part of a community.

Prestige
     Public recognition for work well done.

Self-Fulfillment
     A sense that the work is personally meaningful and rewarding.


Most people are happiest when they have fulfilled needs at all levels. When people are asked what they want as employees, they most often list these requirements:

  1. Efficient managers
  2. The opportunity to think for themselves
  3. To see the end results of their work
  4. Interesting work
  5. To be kept informed
  6. To be listened to
  7. To be respected
  8. To be recognized for their efforts
  9. To be challenged
  10. The ability to improve and expand their skills

First Steps for New Supervisors

Your first moves as a supervisor could be the most important ones you make. To get off to a good start:

Get Acquainted with Your Group

  • Take a few days to meet everyone in your group. Start with a group session, but have a private discussion with each individual.
  • Find out each person's background, interests, and ideas about how effective the group is.
  • Share your background, philosophy on supervision, and expectations.

Identify and Support Informal Group Leaders to Win Their Cooperation

  • Find out who in the group has the respect and attention of the other group members.
  • Consult these informal leaders on group needs.
  • Discuss ideas you are considering with them.

Keep in Touch with Your Group

  • Circulate among your group.
  • Be available to answer questions and offer suggestions.

Start Small

  • Make the most easily acceptable changes first.
  • Move cautiously to wider or more controversial changes such as work hours or group size.

Downplay Your Status

  • Be on a first-name basis with employees.
  • Be readily accessible to group members.
  • Be willing to help when there's a crisis.

Stay Positive

  • Express confidence in the group.
  • Show a willingness to get involved in group success.
  • Express the idea that you and the group are working together.

Prioritizing Your Work

No matter how fast you are, you can't get everything done at once. By prioritizing, you concentrate on what has to be done, setting aside the rest for later. The first step is to make a list of all tasks and activities, then prioritize. Everything you have to do can fit into one of the three following categories:

Must-Do

Critical tasks that must be done to meet deadlines, fulfill management directives, meet customer expectations or provide opportunities for success or advancement.

Should-Do

Tasks that contribute to improved performance, but don't have immediate deadlines.

Nice-to-Do

These tasks can be postponed, scheduled for less busy periods or eliminated.

Use these guidelines to accomplish prioritized tasks -

  • Review priorities daily. Tasks may move to a higher priority as a deadline draws near.
  • Don't waste excessive time on low-priority tasks.

The Problem-Solving Process

Effective problem-solving is a four-stage process.

1. Investigate the Situation

  • Define the problem.
  • Identify the decision objectives: Ask yourself, "What must be accomplished?"
  • Diagnose the causes: Ask yourself, "What changes inside or outside the organization may have contributed to the problem? What insights do those most involved with the problem have? Do they contribute to the problem?"
  • Gather information.

2. Develop Alternatives

  • Brainstorm possibilities.
  • Avoid evaluating until all possible alternatives have been proposed.

3. Evaluate and Make a Decision
     Consider the following factors:

  • Goals and resources of the organization.
  • Consequences of the decision for the organization.
  • Willingness of staff to carry out the decision wholeheartedly.
  • Effect of the decision on competitors.
  • Previous experience with this type of solution.
  • How well it actually solves the problem.

4. Implement and Follow-Up

  • Acquire and allocate resources.
  • Set up budget and schedule.
  • Assign responsibility for specific tasks.
  • Develop a strategy for dealing with the risks and uncertainties of the plan.
  • Measure progress of the solution in specific terms.
  • Take corrective action as required.
  • Evaluate the results: Ask yourself, "Did things work according to plan? Were there any unexpected consequences?"

Involving Others in Decision Making

1. Why Involve Others?

  • To get more information (Others may have insight and knowledge to contribute to the decision-making process.)
  • To gain commitment (People who are involved in a decision are usually more committed to making it work.)
  • To gain more creative input (Groups usually come up with more possibilities than individuals do.)
  • To contribute to the education and development of others (Staff members gain knowledge and experience that will help them to contribute in the future.)

2. How Much Involvement Is Right?
     Consider these five levels of involving others:

Decide Alone

  • When the reasons for involving others don't exist.
  • When a decision must be made quickly.
  • It's best to explain why the decision was made without help.

Discuss with Individuals

  • When some information is needed to make a decision.
  • When it's not possible to meet as a group.

Discuss at a Meeting

  • When creative input is needed.
  • When the final decision must be made alone.

Participate Equally in the Decision with Others

  • When a high degree of commitment is desired.
  • When all of the reasons for involving others apply.

Turn the Decision Over to Others

  • When you are willing to abide by the group's decision.
  • When others are interested, informed, and motivated to make the decision.
  • It's best to clearly state imperatives that the decision must meet.

Conducting Performance Reviews

Most employees are nervous when faced with a performance review. If a performance review is to be effective, it must be conducted in a positive, reassuring tone that emphasizes goals for continued improvement and development of potential.

Advance Preparation
Do your homework to set the stage for a successful review.

  • Review employee records.
  • Get data on performance.
  • Note specific examples of performance to address.
  • Make an appointment: private, interruption-free, a week in advance.
  • Give the employee some questions to consider in advance, such as:

1. What were your biggest achievements during this period?
2. What improvement would you most like to make?
3. What would help you make that improvement?
4. Are there any questions or concerns that you'd like to share?

During the Meeting

  • Review the agenda
  • Emphasize the purpose: to help employees work as closely as possible to their potential. Explain that you will spend some time discussing strategies for continued development and improvement.
  • Set the employee at ease: if the employee is nervous, engage in general discussion until the signs of nervousness (rigid posture, high-pitched voice, limited response) subside.
  • Use specific examples when discussing the employee's behavior.
  • Go beyond numbers: employees are more likely to accept the 1-5 rankings on the typical evaluation form if you have given qualitative feedback in each category.

Helpful Tips

  • Be supportive.
  • Encourage conversation by restating the employee's opinions and by asking for more information.
  • Be open to new information.
  • Ask appropriate questions, such as:

1. What parts of your job do you enjoy the most? Why?
2. What do you see as your major strengths?
3. What else would you like to talk about?

  • Limit questions that have a yes or no answer.

Ending the Discussion

  • Emphasize your support for continued improvement.
  • Check employee's understanding: ask for a summary of the key points.
  • Make sure the employee understands performance requirements for the coming period.
  • End on a positive note.

Following the Meeting

  • Summarize for the record. Have the employee review the summary.
  • Complete administrative details, such as forms and reports, immediately, while your memory is fresh.
  • Keep commitments, if you promised to do something or provide information for the employee.
  • Get employee feedback on effectiveness of the evaluation meeting.

Supervising People with Disabilities

Most people with disabilities are eager to do their job with as little fuss as possible. Here's how you can be sure they reach their full potential.

Use the Same High Standards and Expectations for All Employees

1. Reward and praise high performance.

2. When performance is poor, treat the person the same way you would treat any employee:

  • Explain your concern about the performance.
  • Ask the employee for ideas about how to improve their performance.
  • Agree on a plan for improvement.
  • Follow up as necessary.

3. Provide reasonable accommodation in the area of the person's disability to enable the worker to meet performance standards. This could include special equipment to compensate for blindness or loss of a limb, for instance. Make sure your facility is in compliance with regulations for handicapped access.

4. Avoid pity or special treatment. It makes both disabled people and other employees uncomfortable.

5. Communicate with the employee to learn his or her needs and preferences.

6. Include the employee in all appropriate company activities and social events.

When to Keep Your Mouth Closed

You've heard a lot about the benefits of good communication. But when is it better to keep quiet?

Subjects That Should Not Be a Public Matter

  • An employee's work performance.
  • Your feelings about company policy -- especially if they're negative.
  • Difficulties with your boss.
  • Any personal problems that employees bring to you.
  • Anything anyone tells you in confidence -- unless keeping quiet involves breaking the law or company policy.

One way to encourage good communication with your employees is to demonstrate to them that they can trust you not to reveal confidences to others.

It Is Probably a Good Idea to Keep Quiet in These Situations

  • When employees ask you to take sides in their personal dispute.
  • When your opinion is asked on a controversial political or social issue.

Getting involved in these areas is a threat to team spirit and may even result in feelings of favoritism.

Staying out of such discussions sends the message that diverse arguments don't belong in the workplace.

Giving Effective Feedback

Employees need to know how well they're doing at their jobs. Here's what you need to know to give effective feedback.

Types of Feedback

  • Positive: "You're doing a good job in this area."
  • Neutral: "Here's some information you need."
  • Negative: "You need to improve in this area."

The Best Feedback Is

  • Selective: Concentrate on important areas, rather than listing every detail of behavior.
  • Specific: "You did a good job on the Jones project," rather than "You've been doing a good job lately."
  • Timely: Give feedback as soon as possible after the event.
  • Descriptive: Give facts; talk about what you observed rather than what you concluded from those observations.
  • Sensitive: Allow a cooling-off period if either you or the employee is angry, emotionally upset or very busy.
  • Helpful: When feedback is negative, explore alternatives for improvement, so the employee has some idea of how to improve.

When Giving Negative or Corrective Feedback

  • Don't beat around the bush. Simply and clearly describe the situation or behavior that needs correcting.
  • Ask for a reaction. Get the employee to talk about the situation and your evaluation of it.  Expect defensiveness, and be prepared to express empathy and understanding. Avoid arguing with the employee or debating points raised.
  • Seek agreement. If possible, try to persuade the employee, at least partially, that the situation needs correcting.
  • Develop a plan. Work with the employee to develop an agreed-on plan for improvement. Be specific. Don't be overly ambitious, most improvement proceeds in small steps.
  • Summarize the discussion. This ensures that the employee understands the problem and what you have agreed to do about it.
  • Specify consequences if improvements are not made. Document necessary improvements.
  • Follow up. Set a date to get back together and review progress.

For Feedback to Be Effective
The employee must...

  • Understand what you are saying.
  • Accept the information.
  • Be able to do something about it.

Characteristics of Effective Coaches

Coaching, the process of helping employees become better workers, is a necessary skill for all managers. Here are some characteristics of effective coaches:

  • They work with employees' strengths.
  • They ask for and listen to ideas.
  • They see employees as partners who are important to the success of the department.
  • They give employees visibility.
  • They set high performance standards and model them.
  • They hold employees accountable for their performance.
  • They orient all employees to company values and business strategy.
  • They help employees understand their role in fulfilling company goals.
  • They provide appropriate training and support when needed.
  • They give employees the freedom to do their jobs.
  • They are clear about priorities and performance expectations for each employee.
  • They recognize their position as role models.
  • They keep confidences.
  • They explain reasons for decisions.
  • They give advance notice of changes whenever possible.
  • They give employees regular feedback about their performance.
  • They give employees credit when they deserve it.
  • They protect employees from unreasonable on-the-job stress.
  • They provide encouragement and support when employees are discouraged or about to take on difficult or new assignments.
  • They build trust with their employees.

Review this list and pick some coaching skills you'd like to develop or improve. Concentrate on one or two areas a week. Periodically check the list and reevaluate your skills.

Effective Delegation

Ten Steps for Better Delegation

1. Look closely at your responsibilities and tasks to identify those which, if delegated, would free up your time for other job needs.

2. Select an employee who is best suited for the task. Keeping some kind of record of each employee's strengths and background is helpful.

3. Be careful not to overuse one employee. Give everyone an opportunity to contribute by rotating tasks.

4. Instruct the employee verbally and by demonstration how you wish the task to be completed. Detailed instruction is important.

5. Set objectives that are measurable and attainable.

6. Provide opportunity for feedback. Encourage questions and be available if problems arise.

7. Schedule time for problem-solving and feedback.

8. Resist the temptation to overscrutinize. Trust the employee to make use of the experience you are providing them.

9. Follow through. You are ultimately responsible for all tasks that you delegate. Keep track of your assignments and their progress. Listen and watch for problems as they arise.

10. Praise and recognize successfully completed tasks.
