LOGICAL FALLACIES

Checklist of Common Logical Fallacies

A fallacy is an error. A logical fallacy represents an error or mistake in logic. People often make fallacious arguments that violate logic without realizing it.

  • Non sequitur: (Latin for "it does not follow") A statement that does not follow logically from another.

Ex. "I worked hard during the entire semester; therefore, I deserve an A for the course."

  • Hasty generalization: A conclusion based on insufficient evidence.

Ex. "All adolescents are reckless drivers."

  • Stereotyping: Assuming without sufficient evidence that members of a group think or behave alike.

Ex. "Irishmen and Italians are quick-tempered and moody."

  • Either-or thinking: Limiting possible explanations to two.

Ex. "Either the Democrats band together now behind one candidate, or they will kill each other off politically."

  • Post hoc, ergo propter hoc: Assuming that an event is caused by another simply because one event occurs after the other.

Ex. "I washed my car; therefore, it rained."

  • Begging the question: Assuming as true what needs to be proven.

Ex. "Hondas are reliable because they are Japanese cars."

  • Circular reasoning: Asserting the same point in different words.

Ex. "The growing popularity of aerobic exercise shows that people are becoming increasingly interested in aerobics."

  • Special pleading: Arguing without considering opposing viewpoints.

Ex. "Doberman s are a good breed of dog. They are alert, highly trainable, and elegant."

  • Red herring: Introducing an irrelevant or distracting consideration into an argument.

Ex. "Why worry about a few terrorists when we ought to be doing something about acid rain?"

  • Appeal to ignorance: Assuming something is true because the contrary cannot be proven.

Ex. " If you cannot prove that God doesn't exist, it must mean He does exist."

  • Ad populum: (Latin for "to the people") Appealing to the prejudices of an audience.

Bandwagon: Ex. "Be on the winning team." "Everyone else is cheating, so why shouldn't I?"

  • Ad hominem: (Latin for "to the man") Attacking a person's character rather than addressing the issue at hand.

Ex. "His arguments might impress us if we were not aware of his immorality."

  • Slippery slope: An assertion that one undesirable action will lead to a worse action, which will lead to a worse one still, all the way down the "slippery slope," until we reach some terrible disaster at the bottom.

Ex. "If you take that first drink, you'll die in the gutter, an alcoholic."

  • False analogy: Making an illogical connection based on irrelevant similarities.

Ex. "Since a lawyer is allowed to take briefs into a courtroom, why can't I take notes into the testing center."

False appeals

  • Appeals to authority: When some famous or knowledgeable person's testimony is evoked, though the issue is outside of this authority's expertise.

Ex. "I saw _________ at Jiffy Lube the other day; I'm going there to get my oil changed also."

  • Appeals to pity: (Ad misericordiam): The reasons offered are designed to make us feel sorry for the person involved and so agree with the conclusion out of sympathy.

Ex. "I not done with my research paper, but I have a good excuse: I just broke up with my girlfriend; my sister is having emotional problems too, and my dog died."

Appeals to fear or force:

Ex. "Senator, I represent 300 contributors to your party."

Also remember that a critical reader must learn to sort out material reported as "fact" from mere "inference." Inference is a judgment mad e by the writer based on his/her interpretation of a situation. A reader should especially look critically at the writer's use of emotionally charged words versus neutral words. Emotionally charged words are often a clue to inference, along with statements of value or judgment, whereas neutral words are used most often to report, without judgment, some event. Ask yourself, are you reading facts? Opinions? Supported opinions?

Report: A statement of fact that can be verified:

Ex. "Water freezes at 32 degrees Fahrenheit."

Inference: A prediction made on the basis of what one observes. It states an idea about the unknown based on the known:

Ex. "If we see someone smile at us, we may infer that the person likes us. This may or may not be true."

Judgment: This is an evaluation, one that offers an opinion or a point of view rather than information.

Ex. "To say that former Pres. Nixon was a liar may seem to some like a report, but it is actually a judgment, even though Nixon was shown to have lied to the American public during his presidency. It is more accurate as a report to say simply that Nixon lied to a Congress about Watergate. Calling Nixon a liar judges him rather than reports his behavior."

Creative Versus Logical Thinking
Creative thinking puts things together; it synthesizes.
Logical thinking analyzes things, takes them apart.
Creative thinking generates new ideas.
Logical thinking develops and evaluates ideas that have already been formulated.
Creative thinking explores many different alternatives; it is unconcerned with being correct every step of the way.
Logical thinking focuses on finding an answer and being correct at each step.
Creative thinking is inclusive, admitting all ideas no matter how trivial or outrageous they may seem.
Logical thinking is selective, screening out and eliminating unpromising possibilities.
Creative thinking is random. Moving backward as well as forward, in circles as well as in straight lines, it permits jumps beyond the next step in a logical sequence.
Logical thinking is linear and sequential. It moves from point to point in a straight line, allowing for no skips or gaps.
Creative thinking forestalls judgment, deliberately delaying critical evaluation.
Logical thinking encourages making judgments, assessing whether an idea or piece of evidence is valid.
Creative thinking is questioning, tentative, provisional. It adds the hypothetical, the "what if?" to logical thinking's "how?"
Logical thinking is more assertive, confident, sure of itself.
Both creative and logical thinking ask "Why?"
In logical thinking, the "why" answers a question or proposes a solution to a problem. In creative thinking, the "why" stimulates further thought.
Creative thinking encourages humor and fluidity; it is unsystematic and flexible.
Logical thinking prizes seriousness and rigor; it is systematic and methodical.

Elements of Inductive Reasoning:

  • begins with a specific observation
  • continues with additional specific observations
  • arrives at a general claim or a reasonable conclusion that is based on available evidence
  • attributes causes to events or circumstances, resulting in a hypothesis that can be tested further
  • offers probability rather than certainty

    EX. Advertisements

Elements of Deductive Reasoning:

  • begins with a general idea or major premise
  • continues with an additional minor premise applied to particular case
  • concludes with a specific statement derived from the premise


Deductive Arguments:

  • can be true or false, depending on how true or false the premise is
  • can be valid or invalid, depending on the structure of their syllogisms
  • when true, provide certainty rather than probabilit

    EX. Major Premise: When Jean drinks Coke, she always gets a headache. (fact)

Minor Premise: Jean is drinking a Coke.

Conclusion: Jean will get a headache.
