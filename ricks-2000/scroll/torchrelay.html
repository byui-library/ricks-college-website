Olympic torch relay reaches Alaska Thursday

JUNEAU (AP) — The 2002 Olympic Torch Relay reaches Alaska on Thursday when the flame touches down on board a Delta Connection charter jet at about 2 a.m.

Roughly 11,500 people will carry the torch as it winds its way across America, and 45 to 50 of those will be Alaskans in Juneau.

Torchbearers were nominated and judged using a four-part criteria that examined the potential torchbearers' ability to inspire others to greater achievement, inspire their communities, embody the inspirational spirit of the Olympic movement, and motivate others by encountering and overcoming adversity. Each torchbearer will carry the torch for two-tenths of a mile.

Among the torchbearers is former Juneau resident Mike Miller, a swim coach during his nearly 20 years in Juneau. When he was diagnosed with inoperable prostate cancer, Miller became an advocate for cancer education and early detection. He now lives in Beaverton, Ore., so he can be closer to his doctors.

Another torchbearer will be Jack Eddy of Petersburg, a high school science teacher and cross-country running coach at Petersburg High School. Eddy continues to train with his runners, even though he suffers from Parkinsons disease.

Constance Trollan of Juneau was nominated by coworker Joanna Goldman because of her years of work in the health care industry. Goldman's nomination letter not only earned Trollan a chance to carry the flame but also earned a spot in the relay for Goldman.

Wendie Marriott nominated fellow Unalaska resident and cancer survivor Mandy Anderson and they both won spots in the relay. Some of the other torchbearers include 96-year-old former Iditarod musher Col. Norman Vaughan of Anchorage, who competed in the 1932 Olympics as a sled dog racer; former University of Alaska Fairbanks hockey player Erik Drygas, who broke his neck and was paralyzed during a University of Alaska Fairbanks practice but now coaches the West Valley High School hockey team from his wheelchair; and Nick Parker of Anchorage, who had leg and lung damage from childhood polio but recovered enough to guide 30 trips up Mount McKinley.

Juneau also will host a specialty leg. Torchbearer Ethel Lund will be paddled across Juneau Harbor in a Tlingit canoe by nine members of the Tlingit Warriors crew and two members of the Johnson Youth Center crew. The canoe crew members will all wear full regalia or Tlingit vests, and a U.S. Coast Guard boat will serve as escort.

The Salt Lake Organizing Committee originally asked about using a sled dog team in Juneau, but the local relay task force persuaded the committee to use the canoe instead.

The flame will return to a safety lantern Thursday and fly to Spokane, Wash., to continue the Olympic Torch Relay that night.

“I hadn't really thought about carrying the torch until I was chosen,” he told the Nevada Appeal. “It makes you think about being in the Olympics.”

Young said participating in the torch run and Olympics as a whole have instilled “pride and spirit in our country, especially when we need it.”

After a brief ceremony in downtown Reno, the torch headed toward Sparks.

The torch will pass through more than 20 hands on its way to a cauldron located at the Plaza bandstand.

By JAMES HANNAH
Associated Press Writer

KETTERING, Ohio (AP) _ Sarah McClelland, 102, propped the Olympic torch in her wheelchair as she joined the cross country relay Thursday. She finished the route with the help of a walker, then flashed a thumbs-up sign.

“I feel great, only tired,” McClelland, the oldest torch carrier so far for the 2002 Olympics, said with a smile. “I enjoyed it. I'm glad I could walk part way.”

McClelland lived alone at her home in nearby Xenia until she fell in September, breaking her shoulder in four places. She moved into an assisted-living center to recuperate, then fell again in November, breaking her hip.

Gerri Creech, director of the center, said McClelland was determined to walk in the relay despite her injuries and even trained for it.

“Our therapist caught her out in the parking lot with the walker,” Creech said.

Olympics organizers said they did not know yet whether anyone older than McClelland would be joining the relay.

In Lima later Jan. 3, some people jogged or walked alongside the flame, which was on its way to Fort Wayne, Ind.

“I'm thrilled this day has finally come,” torch carrier Victoria Clark, a bus driver from Perrysburg, said.

McClelland drew cheers in this Dayton suburb when she handed the 3-pound torch to a relay organizer, stood up from the wheelchair and completed the one-fifth mile on foot.

Many well-wishers lining the streets waved small American flags. The temperature was in the low 20s, and some in the crowd were bundled in blankets.

“It's exciting to see that there's so much spirit and to see that people in the U.S. are so excited about the Olympics and that the Sept. 11 events haven't put a damper on any of these events,” Maria Turney, 50, of Kettering, said.

At 14, Sarah McClelland sold liniment, vanilla and pepper door to door on horseback. Married at 18, she and her husband worked a 97-acre farm. She still drove a tractor when she was in her 80s, and drove a car until she was nearly 100.

McClelland arose at 5 a.m. to prepare for the relay and powered up on her regular breakfast of Raisin Bran, a cracker and orange juice, her great-granddaughter said.

After she finished the route, McClelland was hugged by well wishers and then gave a thumbs up to runners who were following in a bus.

Greg McIlvaine, 21, of Xenia, compared McClelland to some of the professional athletes he has seen over the years.

“But I have to say I've never seen anyone move as gracefully as Sarah,” he said.

Jan. 1
Ordinary Joe' runs first leg of 2002 Olympic torch relay
By JOHN WAWROW
AP Sports Writer

BUFFALO, N.Y. (AP) — With tears in his eyes and a smile on his face, a man who called himself an “ordinary Joe” carried the Olympic torch through snowbound Buffalo on Jan.1, beginning the year of the 2002 Salt Lake City Winter Games.

Sam Rotella was chosen to be the first to run the torch relay from Buffalo's City Hall steps — but he wasn't sure it would really happen after the city was hit by a paralyzing storm that dumped nearly 7 feet of snow last week.

“Ordinary Joe, doing something special,” Rotella, a 36-year old courier service driver, who coaches Little League football and is the father of two, said “It's amazing. Just to be selected is amazing. But to be the first. ...”

Overcome by emotion, Rotella couldn't finish the sentence.

“I try to be the tough guy and not get worked up over this,” he said. “It's incredible.”

Rotella was nominated by his wife, Charlene, who called him “an inspiration” for volunteering to serve in the Gulf War years after his Air Force commitment had ended.

“Great dad. Great husband. Incredibly patriotic,” she said. “When they gave the opportunity to carry the torch, I couldn't think of anybody but him.”

Despite temperatures in the 20s, and a biting wind coming off Lake Erie, Rotella elected not to wear a hat or gloves.

“Come on. We Buffalonians are used to this,” he said. “This is nothing.”

He circled Niagara Square before handing off the torch to the first of 36 others who ran the 350-yard portions of the Buffalo leg before the relay moved to Erie, Pa.

James Almond, 83, was among those who followed.

For Almond, it was an opportunity to finally be part of the Olympics — 62 years after he lost the chance to be in the Tokyo Games, which were canceled because of World War II.

“All these years later, I'm finally going to have a torch and be part of the Olympic movement, a small part, but it's a kind of closure,” Almond, who fought in the South Pacific as a member of a commando paratroops unit, said.

The torch-relay leg was close to the 200-meter distance Almond would have competed in.

“I used to run it 21 seconds,” Almond said. “They're going to need a calendar to time me today.”
