Session D1

Key Ethical Virtues for College University Administrators:
How They'll Help You Do Your Job

Facilitator:  
John Patterson, Kwantlen University College

Recorder Name:
David Woolley, Kwantlen University College

Presenter:
Diane Whiteley,  Simon Fraser University

We live in times of changing values and in places where they is a great diversity of values.  As leaders today, to be successful, we are called upon to develop virtue of character to do our job well (with success) and to live well (have happiness).

From Aristotle's writings in the fourth century to current philosophers, the principles necessary for a virtuous character have remained fairly constant.  Virtue of character results from habit and ultimately it is what is with-in that determines whether one lives well.

Several examples of failed leadership were shared where leaders had not been true to character and virtue.  Decision-making techniques are not enough to be a good leader.  One's character determines how decision-making techniques will be used.  Virtues are the habits of character that precondition us to do what is right, good and ethical.

Session D2

Re-Engineering Information and Admissions Services at Oregon State University

Facilitator:
Saskia Knight, Chapman University

Recorder Name:
Masa K. Fujitanai, University of California Irvine

Presenters:  
Dr. Bob Bontrager, Oregon State University  
Michele Sandlin, Oregon State University

Constant re-evaluation and change are required to meet today's challenges in Admissions.  In response to meet these challenges, the presentation combined a senior, comprehensive level of implementing change with front-line, practical how-to-information on process re-engineering.  Dr. Bob Bontrager provided the comprehensive overview defining the context, enrollment challenges and decline as well as identifying the solutions, support need and goals for the re-engineering project.

Michele Sandlin provided the implementation and details of identifying the problem issues, focusing in on how the project proceeded and was succeeded in changing processes/workflow, office/staff attitudes (and culture) and the keys to the success.  Three components were essential:

  1. Change processes

  2. Resolve human resources issues

  3. Physical environment changes

A 6-month deadline for change was set (July 1, 1997).  To set the re-engineering in motion first dictated a collection of detailed data, establishing an office mission and philosophy.  This included a team structure in place of 5 solo units of individual unit processing, cross training and communication strategies development.  Re-engineering also included downsizing and reassessing staff classifications, pay scales, changing attitudes about hiring, training and supervising student assistants.  Staff development was mandatory and included dealing with change and improving customer service (from a students perspectives as priority).  These sessions were developed through HR.  Michele stressed another key point -- to build in (even if forced).  Some ideas included managers bringing donuts once a week to retro days, ice cream, and  staff picnic (with activities).  Other suggestions included consulting by telephone if travel costs prohibited visits to other colleges (BYU, UCLA, WSU) or having managers take staff involved with the process to visit nearby campuses.  Other institutions were receptive and OSU has reciprocated. 

Review of policies and procedures are essential to determine if processes are meeting current guidelines.  Change in presidency provided top level support for this project.

Keys to success: 1) top level support - comprehensive 2) approach, communication, managing 3) ambiguity, timing, and perseverance

Results - Smaller staff (augmented by student assistants), improved morale, better service to students, improved campus relations (internal and external), enrollment increase.

Session D3

Thinking about Bi-cultural Perspectives

Facilitator: 
Clifford Ramirez, University of California at Los Angeles

Recorder Name:
Anne Delfin, Antioch University, Los Angeles

Presenter:
Valerie White, Lewis and Clark College

In the U.S. American mainstream culture (yes, there really is one!), there is the notion that people use one cultural perspective to figure out how the world works, how to make sense of what is happening and how to act appropriately.  As the world becomes increasingly complex though, more and more people actually operate from more than one culture.  People from "minority" groups may be examples of this. 

People who "use" more than one culture may be thought of as bi-cultural, even when they don't necessarily display both or all the cultures at the same time.  This is because bi-cultural people act in a way they think is appropriate to the cultural setting they are in at the moment.  Sometimes an employee or student might describe what they do as a bi-cultural person as "leaving my other culture at home to fit in somewhere else."

There are different ways to be bi-cultural, both in how one makes sense of how the world works and in how one acts.  Examples follow:

Assimilation:

The person no longer uses his or her "original" culture and just uses the one from the culture in which they now live or work (assuming that culture is different).

Separation:

The person no longer uses his or her "original" culture rather than the one used by the culture in which the person lives.

Marginal:

The person can't use the "original" culture no can they use the culture in which they live and work.

Bi-cultural:

The person uses both the "original" culture and another culture, such as the one they live or work in.  Sometimes they alternate between cultural perspectives.  Others might blend pieces of cultures together.

Culture is a lens that people use to think about how the world works.  It's what they use to make sense of what happens.  Cultures have groups of members, even though some of its members may not think about themselves as members; the culture may not be part of how a person identifies himself, or herself, but others might.  Culture is made up of many things: core values the group has, basic (and sometimes invisible) assumptions and beliefs, objects, and behaviors, such as how people communicate.

Not all people in a particular culture have to think or act the same way, though sometimes we think they do.  For example, there's no one-way to be a woman, or there's no one-way to be Asian American.  But it's ok to think about some generalities that apply to the cultural group when we're trying to imagine the different ways people make sense of how the world works.  But when we assume the person in front of us fits the generalization we have about the person's culture group, that is when it becomes a stereotype.

Session D4

Why Did you Come?  Why Did you Stay?  Recruiting Professional Staff

 

Facilitator:
Julia Pomerenk, Pacific Lutheran University

Recorder Name:  
Sue Sanders, California Lutheran University

Presenters:  
Nancy Youlden, Washington State University  
Patricia Dell, Idaho State University  
Laurence Seno, Willamette University - College of Law  
Julia Pomerenk, Pacific Lutheran University

 This informative session with a panel format dealt with the topic "why did you come?" and "why did you stay?"  Panelist shared ideas and information about how to recruit and retain professionals to Student Services.

A common theme existed for the panelists.  They loved being in school.  They loved working with students and felt that "students first" should be the motto for all student service offices.  The panelists found their careers in higher education to be very rewarding and challenging.  They all stay because they love the variety and are stimulated by all of the opportunities.

In order to retain employees it was suggested that supervisors take the time to evaluate and understand them.  Supervisors need to work hard at helping the staff move to the next level.  Finding out what makes each employee "tick" is critical.  Teamwork is essential.  To do this a supervisor must build relationships.  Finding out what motivates each employee is all part of building relationships.

Each employee should be encouraged to grow professionally.  Some examples might be allowing employees to take courses during regular work hours or involve them in the decision-making or office practices.

A good quote to take back to your college "know who you work best with and then work hardest with the ones you don't.

Session D5

Developing Web Applications

Facilitator:
Ray Pillar, University College of the Cariboo
 

Recorder Name:
Kathie Westerfield, College of the Desert

Presenters: 
David Glenn, University of Victoria
Merrilee Hoen, University of Victoria

The University of Victoria in Victoria, British Columbia has an enrollment of 18,000 undergraduate and graduate students.  In recent years, it has experienced a large increase in enrollment with no increase in staff.  In order to provide expected services to students, the staff turned to technology.  Through the collaboration of the Offices of the Administrative Registrar and Computing and Systems Services, several Web applications were designed and implemented in the past 24 months.  Services to students include online application to the University, Admissions Inquiry for the status of the students' applications, access to students' records in the student data base, selecting and changing of PINs, and registration over the Web.

In 1984, the University manually processed 9,798 applications; in 1988, 11,182 undergraduates registered via telephone registration.  In 1999, 14,749 undergraduates registered via telephone registration and/or online registration over the Web.  WebReg provides the same registration functions as telephone registration, but is much quicker and more efficient for students to use and enables students to register no matter where they are located.  The University created online services to students in an effort to provide the service needed and wanted by today's students.  In addition, it grants students the responsibility over their own registration.

The presenters demonstrated the use of the online application, Admissions Inquiry feature, and some of the functions of WebReg.  They also discussed security features and gave tips for the development stages of online services to students such as keeping designs as simple as possible.  Advantages of online services for students and staff were discussed.  Services planned for the future include real time access to the course timetable, using the Web to vote in university elections, grade submission by faculty, and access by faculty of class lists.  As the expectations of students and faculty rise, so will the online services provided.

Session D6

Advice for New Admissions Officers

Facilitator:
Betsy Ellsworth, Reed College

Recorder Name:
Ben Bradley, Reed College

Presenter:

Mae Brown - University of California, San Diego

Mae shared her insights about managing and leading staff in the UCSD Office of Admissions. Besides explaining personal challenges she faced (increasing responsibility, budgets, institutional and policy changes, increasing institutional popularity), she outlined solutions such as extensive cross-training/back-up, not undermining communication, and the importance of the "team concept."  She also offered a theoretical framework of management from the writings of James Kouzes and Barry Posner, emphasizing that they key to effective leadership is credibility.
