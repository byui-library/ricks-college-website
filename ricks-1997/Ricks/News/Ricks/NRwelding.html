Ricks College News Release, June 23, 1998

Ricks College reaches across border
with innovative welding program

By Whitney Galbraith
Ricks College News Bureau

REXBURG, ID — Ricks College has extended its educational reach across the border with an innovative welding program now being taught in the heart of Mexico.

The pilot program is designed to assist young members of the LDS Church in Mexico and Central America develop job-related skills.

Ricks Academic Vice President Donald C. Bird and Charles Cartmill, chairman of the Division of Engineering and Technology, first suggested a plan to host students on the Ricks campus during the summer months where additional educational training in a vocational field could be offered.

Proposals were made and approved for a pilot welding program to be taught on the Rexburg campus. However, upon second thought, rather than requiring prospective students to leave their homelands and travel to Ricks, a proposal was made to take the program to them.

The key was Benemerito de las Americas, a large high school in Mexico City owned and operated by the LDS Church. This high school campus schools 2,300 students during the school year and is vacant during the summer months. With older welding equipment sent from Ricks, an interpreter and an instructor, this facility is housing the program that began June 1.

A special committee comprised of Ricks employees and two former mission presidents selected the first students for the welding program.

When the applications were made available in Mexico and Central America, the response was tremendous. There were more than 180 people applying for the training.

After an intense application process of essay questions and other criteria, the committee narrowed the applicants down to 22.

The 10-week course in welding was designed by Cartmill and Leo Castagno, an instructor in the welding department who is teaching the classes. Language expertise is being provided by teacher aides Ramiro Galvan and Lane Jacques, welding students from Ricks. Both assistants are returned missionaries from Spanish-speaking missions.

Reporting from Mexico City, Cartmill says, "The challenges have definitely been there since the inception of the program, but all have thus far been met with hard work and the help of the Lord. We are extremely optimistic about the results of the program and the expected results from the participants."

The costs of tuition, housing and food is being covered by the Church. The students are housed in the campus dorms and remain there throughout the course.

Students are instructed in welding for eight hours a day on techniques and skills. Two more hours are spent in instruction of English as a second language. The program requires 400 hours of instruction to certify the students.

After completion of the course the students will earn a certificate in welding. A special graduation ceremony will be held August 15 to honor the efforts of the students.

It is hoped that the graduates will find better jobs through the program. Bird says, "The anticipated salary will be increased from as little as $100 a month to $500 a month." This is a tremendous increase that can make the difference in the lives of many members the of church in Central America and Mexico.

In six months a follow-up study will be conducted to report the progress and success of the students who have completed the welding program. If the pilot program proves to be a success, plans would be made to train a graduate of the program to supervise and run the program on a continuing basis.
