Figurative Language
 

Earlier in the web pages you were told that some characteristics of creative writing are also found in expository writing.  Such is the case with figurative language.  Figures of speech include antithesis, apostrophe, synecdoche, metonymy, climax, hyperbole, irony, metaphor, and simile.

 

Here we will focus only on two common figures, metaphor and simile.  A metaphor is a comparison.  The comparison is generally implicit, but not always.  With language, we compare item A to item B.  

 

The metaphor can occur in the noun, verb, adjective, or adverb.  Let's compare item A, a person, to item B, a snake.  We will imply that Bertha (A) is a snake (B).  The implication will be in the verb.

 

Bertha Sue slithered into the room.  The word "slithered" is metaphoric. This word implies that Bertha Sue is a snake.

 

The snake took my car without asking.

 

Here, the metaphor is in the noun.  Notice, however, that in this case, the metaphor (the comparison) is not implicit, but explicit.

 

Bertha leaves her scaly skin on all she touches.  Here the metaphor is in the adjective, scaly.

 

Extended Metaphor.  If you start a comparison and continue it into the next sentence, the metaphor is extended.  For example:  

 

Bertha Sue slithered into the room, hissing at the students near the board.  Then, moving rhythmically to the back of the room, she finally coiled into her nest.  Her tongue darted in and out of her mouth as she surveyed her prey.  Then striking without warning, she said, "Larry, you're a creep!"  Every day she is full of such venom.

 

In the above example, these words are metaphoric:  slithered, hissing, moving

 

rhythmically (in the context), coiled, nest, darted in and out, prey, striking, and venom.

 

Mixed Metaphor.  Sometimes in a single sentence, a writer gets two different images going at the same time.  This should be avoided.  It indicates that the writer is not paying attention to what he or she is saying.  Example.  Bertha Sue slithered out of the room, chirruping all the while.  The metaphors, slithered and chirruping, are mixed.

 

Personification.  A metaphor which compares something not human (inanimate) to a human is called personification.  Example.  The chair groaned under my weight.  The clouds are crying.  A weeping willow tree guards our entrance.  My guitar, a lady with fine curves, often sings for me.

 

Simile.  A simile is a comparison using like or as.  Example. Her touch is as gentle as a warm, summer breeze.  Her beauty is as perfect as a single blade of grass.
