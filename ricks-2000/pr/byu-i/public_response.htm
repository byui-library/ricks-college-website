Here are a few editorials from the press concerning the proposed changes:

In our opinion
    If talking with Ricks College President David Bednar about the future of Ricks College as Brigham Young University-Idaho and as a four-year university doesn’t get you excited about the future of the school and the area, nothing will.
    His enthusiasm for the project is infectious. And if you have reservations about any aspect of the change, he’ll win you over.
    Intercollegiate sports fans may not buy into the elimination of those sports at the new school. But to the merely skeptical, Bednar’s vision of spontaneous football games involving teams made up of every male student at the school, pictures of female teams organized to play in night baseball leagues, expansion of the Guitars Alive and other musical offerings and many other events designed to involve every student at the school in some type of activity is convincing.
    Bednar is quick to point out that the what ifs and maybes he uses as examples while talking of the future of the school are nothing more than possibilities, and people of the area should be cautious about giving credence to rumors floating around.
    He says one rumor he heard puts the number of student at the school at 28,000, a figure that has absolutely nothing to do with official reality. Sure, there will be more students. But Bednar will not speculate at all on that number.
    There’s much to be done at the school before such projections can be made. So Bednar has a promise for all of us - when official have some hard numbers about anything, they will share them with us. Meantime, beware of any other claims.
    But if you talk with Bednar about the concept of the changes in store for Ricks, you get a glimpse of someone who sees the new BYU-Idaho going where no education institution has gone before.
    The school is owned and operated by The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, one of the fastest growing churches in the world with a membership projected to number 280 million by the year 2080, as Bednar notes. So there will be plenty of young people wanting to attend BYU-Idaho. . . .
    So school officials and others in the church are looking to the past and to the present as they make plans. But Bednar believes fervently they are also guided by a vision of the future that is inspired by the leadership of the church.
    And this vision will lead BYU-Idaho to a future that will draw leaders from throughout the world to see what has been done.
    A talk with Bednar will convince you this is true. And we believe it is.
    We also believe that we in the communities surrounding the new BYU-Idaho will witness some pretty remarkable things ourselves. There will, of course, be growth in our communities associated with the changes at the school.
    But we will also watch as far-reaching visionary plans are developed and put into place at the school.
    If we are lucky, some of it will rub off. We may become visionary about our own communities and guide and develop them in ways that will b an example for others.
Roger Porter, Rexburg Standard Journal, July 21, 2000

The high cost of athletics
    It’s easy to understand LDS President Gordon Hinckley’s motive for dropping intercollegiate athletics from the new four-year Brigham Young University-Idaho. These programs cost more dollars than they bring in from ticket sales and other activities.
    Hinckley decided it wasn’t worth the expense, even though Ricks College has one of the most successful junior college programs in the country. That’s caused some heartburn in Rexburg and among Ricks alumni. But Hinckley’s decision should give the people running Idaho’s system of higher learning reason to reconsider the cost of athletics in the state.
    During the 1999-2000 school year, the intercollegiate athletic programs at Idaho State University, the University of Idaho, Boise State University and Lewis-Clark State College cost about $21.5 million.
    Those same programs generated only $10.4 million in revenues. Students, taxpayers and the institutions themselves kicked in about $11 million to subsidize these programs.
    The sports subsidy will grow to nearly $12 million in the new school year.
    That picture is even more lopsided at ISU. Last year, the university’s athletics generated just 25 percent of the money needed to support its $4.7 million program: And ISU athletics is growing faster than its ability to raise money. Three years ago, students and taxpayers covered 65 percent of ISU’s athletic bills; next year, that subsidy will be nearly 76 percent.
    No Idaho college sports programs can get by on its own. The UI athletic program pays about half its costs.
    BSU - which draws from a larger population base and has far more corporate support - can cover about two thirds of its bills. But, even after capturing a football championship and playing in a bowl game last year, BSU still needed more than $3 million from taxpayers and students.
    Idaho isn’t unique. Few university athletic programs of any size cover their expenses.
    College administrations keep these programs partly out of tradition and partly because of politics. Nothing gives a college better exposure than a winning football team. A higher public profile brings gifts from alumni and the public. Even a losing program helps. Last year, ISU received more than $5 million in donations - not including the $68.5 in gifts and pledges its capital campaign has received in the past 18 months. Athletics played at least some role in that.
    Earlier this year, the State Board of Education moved to slow the raid on student fees and university budgets to cover athletics’ bills. Still, next year’s nearly $12 million sports subsidy represents 2 percent of everything Idaho higher education spends. That’s a great deal of money for a college and university system so cash-strapped that it’s had to raise student fees every year for decades.
    How much academic improvement - or research or public service - would that $12 million buy next year?
    ISU President Richard Bowen, UI President Robert Hoover and BSU President Charles Ruch don’t have the luxury of even raising that question. Unlike Hinckley, they operate public schools and must answer to alumni, boosters, legislators and corporate interests. The minute one of them even suggests shutting down athletics, he’ll be on the carpet.
    But Hinckley has shown that there is another path - and reminds us that Idahoans are paying a price for not following it.
Idaho Falls Post Register, Idaho Falls, July 9, 2000

Swimming upstream
    Few events in a small city's life can outshine the founding of a university. Ricks College's transformation into Brigham Young University-Idaho is obviously enormous news for Rexburg. Its value to the rest of southern and eastern Idaho is less apparent but no less genuine.
    Universities generate economic and cultural ripples that spread throughout a region. Ricks and the surrounding area will benefit from faculty wages, institutional purchases and student expenditures on all kinds of goods and services. Money aside, a university's cultural effects are profound as well. A university raises a community's educational level and its focus on quality of life. A church-based school brings an additional element–an emphasis on values that can only be seen as a positive social influence.
    BYU's arrival in Idaho automatically reshapes this state's higher education landscape. Idaho State University traditionally attracts a couple of hundred students each year who have completed Ricks' two-year program. That relationship has given ISU an unofficial but potent link to Salt Lake City. When Ricks begins accommodating its own upperclassmen, ISU will need to redefine its institutional mission.
    That redefinition may be good for the Magic Valley. With empty seats to fill, ISU and other universities in the region will compete more aggressively for transfer student from the College of Southern Idaho. Meanwhile, ISU may find competitive advantage in re-emphasizing its sometimes disappointing outreach programs here.
    Another potential plus in the new university is its distance from Boise. If BYU-Idaho enhances eastern Idaho's economic and cultural vitality, it will help that region counterbalance Ada County's increasing dominance of the state.
    The university's arrival also will add a new wrinkle to Idaho's religious diversity. An enhanced Mormon higher-ed presence may inflame the state's latent religious tensions, or it may inspire increased mutual tolerance. We'll hope for the latter.
    Perhaps the most surprising aspect of last week's announcement is the plan to eliminate intercollegiate sports at the Rexburg campus.
    Athletics is a dominating presence in most U.S. universities, reshaping campus life as well as institutional priorities. But LDS President Gordon B. Hinckley signaled the church's intent to distinguish BYU-Idaho as a campus devoted to academic and religious pursuits.
    An institution that swims against society's prevailing currents deserves admiration. If this step is an indication of the new university's willingness to be a contrary spirit in our secularized, liberalized, trivialized contemporary America, its presence in Idaho will be welcome indeed.
Twin Falls Times-News, Twin Falls, Idaho, June 2000

Ricks  graduates to BYU-Idaho
    Ricks College's transformation into BYU-Idaho should be good news for LDS college students.
    LDS Church President Gordon B. Hinckley and other church officials said the transition reflects the institution's commitment to academics and providing a spiritual atmosphere for LDS students entering college life. We think this move was one that was long awaited and will benefit many people in years to come.
    Many LDS Church members want their children to get their higher education at Brigham Young University because of its national recognition for many of its academic programs, as well as providing s a solid background in religion.
    But BYU's enrollment cap, combined with the rapid growth of the LDS Church in recent years, have made a BYU education hard to obtain for many people. In some ways, BYU has become the Harvard of the West because it is as hard to get into as that Massachusetts university.  That has left many LDS college students with the option of trying to get into BYU's Hawaii campus, going to the private LDS university in Virginia or using LDS Institute programs to supplement secular college programs.
    Part of Utah Valley State College's growing pains is from BYU's tight enrollment cap.  By expanding Ricks' mission, LDS students will have a better chance of getting the BYU experience. For parents, it will mean their children are going to school where their standards will be respected and upheld.
    It's also a good move for Ricks, which has been a jewel in the LDS Church's academic crown. While providing the religious training its big sister is famous for, Ricks was also a place for students to try out college life and determine if they are ready for a full education.
    But, as a two-year institution, Ricks was limited. Twenty or 30 years ago, an associate's degree could get you a good job, but today it no longer carries that same academic weight.  So, the logical course of action was to make Ricks a four-year college.
    Granted, the transition to a four-year school will mean no intercollegiate sports at Rexburg, but we think that is a small price to pay for providing students with education for a lifetime.
    We commend the decision, and wish BYU-Idaho success in its new mission.
Daily Herald, Provo, Utah, June 29, 2000

Hinckley's vision brings promise
    Only a man considered to be a prophet could eliminate football and get away with it!
    When Ricks College becomes Brigham Young University-Idaho, a lot more will happen than just the elimination of intercollegiate athletics and two more years of education added to a private institution. The region will be profoundly and favorably affected for a long time to come.
    Latter-day Saints President Gordon B. Hinckley is a vigorous 90-year-old who makes vigorous decisions. This was one of them. Where others would have pondered and agonized, polled and calculated – or called for a multiyear transition – refreshingly, he simply said, "It's the right thing to do, let's do it now."
    A visionary, he sees higher education as critical for a new, diverse generation of leaders and scholars for a fast-growing and increasingly far-fling church. With BYU at capacity, converting Ricks meets an obvious need.
    Every decision has its pluses and minuses, including this one. Other regional institutions taken by surprise will have to adapt to what Hinckley has done.
    Already clean, attractive and enjoying the best of small-city living, Rexburg must seem "about right" already to many who live there. Rapid growth, with its increasing in property values, will not be universally welcomed.
    Idaho State University has grown largely by attracting Ricks College students for their last two years of undergraduate education. Once considered unfriendly to Mormons, today 60 percent of ISU's student body is LDS, according to the Idaho Statesman. Those students may choose four years at BYU-Idaho instead of ISU.
    Idaho Falls, with its Higher Education Center staffed largely by faculty from ISU and part-time professors, may suffer from being the only major city in the region without its own university. Increasingly, jobs flow out of universities and from the interaction between business and higher education.
    Then there's the only consistently winning athletic program in the area, at any level, gone in a flash.
    And, of course, sadly, some anti-Mormon sentiment won't applaud the expansion.
    But the long-range positives will be enormous. Another four-year university has got to be a plus for the region. BYU-Idaho students will end up taking more courses in Idaho Falls. Research and development capacity will increase up and down the valley. University-driven growth tends to be the best kind. Think of the increase in telecommunications capacity alone.
    Did the University of Utah of Utah Valley suffer from the growth of BYU? Or is there a relationship between high-tech industry growth and higher education along with the Wasatch Front?
    Ricks College already provides a phenomenal education in the arts and offers public entertainment seldom seen at a two-year college. Much as intercollegiate athletics will be missed, a true university will provide intellectual and artistic resources the region has never had.
    Change is not easy, but 10 years from now the end of a revered, pioneer college in favor of a true university will look and feel less like loss and more like the wisdom that it is.
Post Register, Idaho Falls, Idaho, June 25, 2000

Spuds and Rex-burgers
   The new, larger Ricks College at Rexburg–or actually, the new, larger Brigham Young University, Spudland version, at Rexburg–probably means the new, smaller Idaho State University, Boise State University, Lewis-Clark State College, and University of Idaho.
    The LDS Church announced last week that its BYU overflow school, the two-year Ricks College, will be upgraded to a four-year school and renamed Brigham Young University-Idaho.
    Until now, all those Ricks students needed some place to go after their first two years of schooling at Ricks. Several hundred of them have chosen the four state schools of Idaho each year. Presumably, the bulk of those students will now remain at Ricks.
    Most recently, Ricks has had 8,628 students. That will probably double in a handful of years. Enrollment at Idaho's state schools may be heading in somewhat the opposite direction as a consequence. At the very least, it will slow growth at the state schools (if extortionist increases in tuition haven't already done that).
    Moving students from state schools to the new BYU-Idaho is not necessarily all bad. For one thing, this is very good news for Rexburg, where Ricks is already the dominate local industry. So congratulate the Rexburgians or the Rexburgers.  Whatever you call them, they're going to be a bigger burg now.
    And while the state colleges and universities always like the bragging rights and the alleged economy of scale of a bigger and bigger enrollment, any slowing of growth could also represent a reduction in budget demands.
     True, the Idaho Legislature could stand to plow more money per student into those schools. But there will now be fewer students to educate. Maybe the lawmakers can use that slack to pump a little more quality per person into Idaho education.
    Meanwhile, raise your glass of root beer to the Rexburgarians who are proudly well on their way to hosting the fastest growing school in Idaho. The LDS have decided they want more Idaho spuds with their Burgers.
Lewiston Morning Tribune, Lewiston, Idaho, June 25, 2000
