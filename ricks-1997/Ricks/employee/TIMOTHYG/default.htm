How many psychologists does it take to change a light bulb?
    It depends on which school of psychology they have adopted:

     Psychiatrists (psychodynamic):    Only one.  But the light bulb has to really want to change.

     Humanist:   Two. One to reflect the lightbulb's potential until it changes itself, and one to change the bulb.

     Cognitivist: Two. One to attempt to help the lightbulb change its filament until it works, and one to change the bulb.

     Behaviorist:    Three. One to examine the cues that led to the light going out, and making adjustments, One to reinforce successive approximations of lighting, and One to change the bulb.

     Industrial Psychologist:  Three.  One to redesign the socket, one to redesign wiring to fit the needs of the lightbulb, and one to change the bulb.

      Social psychologist:   Four.  One to change the socket, one to change the wiring, and one to explain how the lightbulb is a victim of its environment, and one to change the bulb.

      Psychobiologist:   Seven.  One to examine the creation of the lightbulb, one to examine the wiring, one to examine the connections between the wiring and the lightbulb, one to examine the area of damage to the filament, one to diagnose the syndrome of darkness, one to bemoan the fact that research has not been done to allow replacing the filament yet,  and one to actually change the bulb.

"Why did the chicken cross the road?"

Philosophers:
Aristotle: To actualize its potential. It was in its nature.

Douglas Adams:  Forty-two.

Buddha: If you ask this question, you deny your own chicken-nature.

Darwin:  It was the logical next step after coming down from the trees.  If it had not crossed the road it probably would not have survived, and therefore would have no offspring, and the trait of road crossing would have died out.

Albert Einstein:  Whether the chicken crossed the road or the road crossed the chicken depends upon your frame of reference. Did the chicken cross the road, or did the road move beneath the chicken?

Ralph Waldo Emerson:  It didn't cross the road; it transcended it.

Epicurus:  For fun.

Johann Friedrich von Goethe:  The eternal hen-principle made it do it.

Hippocrates:  Because of an excess of phlegm in its pancreas

David Hume:  Out of custom and habit.

John Locke: To exercise his natural right to liberty.  And because experience had written on its original "tabula rasa" that it would be profitable to the chicken to do so.

Machiavelli:  So that its subjects will view it with admiration, as a chicken which has the daring and courage to boldly cross the road, but also with fear, for whom among them has the strength to contend with such a paragon of avian virtue?  In such a manner is the princely chicken's dominion maintained.

Watson:  The point is that the chicken crossed the road.  Who cares why?  The end of crossing the road justifies whatever motive there was.

Nietzsche:  Because if you gaze too long across the Road, the Road gazes also across you.

Plato:  For the greater good.  It recalled a memory from its soul and knew that crossing the road was the right thing to do

Pyrrho the Skeptic: What road?

Jean-Paul Sartre:  In order to act in good faith and be true to itself, the chicken found it necessary.

Socrates:  Why do you assume that the chicken had to have a reason to cross the road?
If it did, what else must be assumed?
What evidence supports that assumption?
What implications does road crossing have on other roads and other crossings?

The Sphinx: You tell me.

Henry David Thoreau: To live deliberately ... and suck all the marrow out of life.

Atheist’s Bible: And God said unto the chicken, “Thou shalt cross the road” And the chicken crossed the road, and there was much rejoicing.

Psychologists:
Albert Bandura: It had to be an act of free will.  If it were not, there would be no hope of ever having the ability to make independent and actuating choices in order to improve one’s life and make it more productive.

Sigmund Freud: The fact that you are all concerned that the chicken crossed the road reveals your underlying sexual insecurity, mixed with aggressive fantasies.

Carl Jung:  The confluence of events in the cultural gestalt necessitated that individual chickens cross roads at this historical juncture, and therefore synchronicitously brought such occurrences into being.

Robert Ornstein: The signal was given in the motor side of the sensorimotor strip, sent to the substantia nigra, which activated the walking circuits in the cerebellum and through the peripheral nervous system’s effector neurons, to the muscles, which both moved the chicken across the road step by step, and kept it from falling down in the process.

Carl Rogers: It was clearly an act of free will.  Without free will, the chicken could no longer transcend the animal kingdom into the human realm.

B.F. Skinner: The chicken obviously crossed the road because of its history of reinforcers, or present reinforcers associating themselves with road crossing, thereby strengthening the road crossing response.

Ludwig Wittgenstein:  The possibility of "crossing" was encoded into the objects "chicken" and "road," and circumstances came into being which caused the actualization of this potential  occurrence.

Stephen Jay Gould:  It is possible that there is a sociobiological explanation for it, but we have been deluged in recent years with sociobiological stories despite the fact that we have little direct evidence about the genetics of behavior, and we do not know how to obtain it for the specific behaviors such as road crossing, that figure most prominently in sociobiological speculation.
