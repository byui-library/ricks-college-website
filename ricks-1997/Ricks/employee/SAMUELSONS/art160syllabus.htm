Course Description:

In this course we explore various methods of forming, decorating, glazing, and firing through fourteen weekly projects. Most Mondays we will begin with a critique of the projects due that day. Following the critique I will introduce the next assignment/skill by demonstration and example. We will have student reports on many Mondays throughout the semester. There will usually be at least an hour studio time on Mondays. Wednesdays will almost always be devoted to studio work. You will find that to do well you will need to put in an hour outside class for every hour in class.

Course goals/objectives:

1. To learn with your hands, heart, and mind the feel of clay and the joy of working it.

2. To learn the basic terminology of ceramics--in theory and, where possible, in practice.

3. To develop self-discipline--meeting deadlines, working toward projects goals.

4. To practice principles of art and craft--communicating ideas visually.

5. To practice and improve skills of critiquing, oral reporting, writing,

Fourteen Projects:

These fourteen assignments (one per week) will constitute 40-60% of your grade. Their actual due dates and times and a description beyond the project overview will be given to you in class. They will be graded in their formed stage (not bisqued or glazed). Part of the forming grade includes inscribing your putting name or initials, section, and date on each of your pieces.

Late work:

I will accept late work, but it will be averaged with the zero (0) you received when you did not have it ready to hand in on time. Translation: the highest you can receive on a late project is 50% of its total points. Late work will kill your grade.

Attendance:

Besides the fact that 10% of your grade is based on attendance, you simply need to be in class to receive instruction, participate in critiques (including turning your work in on time). Plan never to miss this class. I will keep accurate attendance records. Students sometimes come in after midterm grades to ask what they can do to salvage a grade. Unfortunately, there is no way to make up for absences. Simply don't miss class. And don't come late or leave early (both which I mark as partial attendance). The reality of the grading in this class is that few students will be able to achieve and maintain an A average based on the projects only. Students with few or no absences will receive a grade increase for effort that goes beyond the mere 10% attendance points. In the rare event that you do not have an assignment completed on the due date, do not miss class out of embarrassment. Come and admit, "I am not prepared today." Being there will help you from getting further behind; and you will have the added confidence of knowing you did what you were supposed to just by coming. If you are discouraged or depressed, come to class. If you are exasperated by insensitive roommates or unlucky in love, come to class. If the check has not come of if someone has yelled at you, come to class. If you are having a particularly rotten day, come to class. Working clay can solve many problems, can often calm the savage breast.

Journal/Sketchbook: (unlined paper, about 9 x 12 or so) Bring to class every day.

This record will serve at least five functions:

1. A place to take notes on class lectures/demonstrations.

2. A place to take notes on classmates' reports.

3. A place to plan your work. Explore ideas in your journal sketchbook. It is sometimes wise to explore an idea in two dimensions before committing yourself in clay.

4. A place to record your work. Make sketches, notes, as well as written responses to what you are learning/have learned. It is, for example, important to record your glazes, so you will know how to reproduce a certain finish if you want. Beyond that, you will understand your frustrations and triumphs even better if you express them in words. When you bring a particularly successful piece from the kiln, I may ask you what glaze you used. You should be able to turn to your records and tell me precisely. You may think at the time of glazing that you will remember, but it may be weeks before the piece has been glazed, and by then you may have forgotten. Keep good records.

5. A place to record the work of others. When you run across a form, a technique, an idea you are particularly attracted to, make a record of it in your journal, together with documentation.

I will check the journal/sketchbook at midterm and at the final. Bring it every day. I may ask to see it anytime as a way of checking (and encouraging) its progress.

Grading the journal sketchbook: one 50- point check at midterm, one 50-point check at the final. For each check I will give 10 points for each of the five areas listed above.

Oral and Written Report:

Each student will give an 6-8 minute oral report with an accompanying two-page typed, documented version of the information. Some report topics will be assigned or suggested; others will be open. Both the written and the oral reports should include drawings, pictures, and documentation. Each oral report should share two objective questions (and answers) that I will use on the midterm and final exams. We will sign up for these on Wednesday, September 9.

Grading the oral report; 10 points each for these five areas: time, interest, content, visual aids, presentation (enthusiasm, clarity, polish).

Grading the written report; 10 points each for these five areas: length, documentation, content, visual aids, writing.

Quizzes & Exams:

I will give unannounced 5-10 point quizzes over the reading and terminology. I will give a 20- point terminology test at the end of the term. I will also give a 40 point practicum exam where you draw at random from a hat a skill and demonstrate your ability to perform it.

Final projects:

At the final exam period you will bring your six (or more) best, completed (glazed and fired) projects. They can be projects that have been graded for the other assignments or completely new work. They should, of course, represent you very best work of the semester.

Judging Quality:

Ceramics is a progressive process. Sometimes you will want to fire and glaze a piece just because you have made it. But frankly, not all of the pieces you form should be glazed and fired. You may neglect to trim them on time. Their walls may be too thick or too thin. They may show cracks in the bisque firing. If these or other problems occur, be brave enough to recycle. Only well-crafted pieces will be fired.

Studio Hours:

The Ceramics studio is open six days a week 7 a.m. to 10:30 p.m. except when another class is in session. Students registered for ceramics classes are welcome to come during these times to work.

Safety:

Most aspects of forming clay are not inherently dangerous. But ceramics is not without its potential hazards. The heat associated with firing, the chemicals used in mixing glazes, and the glazed surface and processes of grinding glitches in those surfaces, and other areas need to be treated with much care and with proper safety equipment and precautions. I will give further guidance at key moments, and common sense will help. Proceed with care, and ask questions.

Getting Better:

At some point in the semester you may get frustrated with your performance and ask, "How Can I do Better on My Pieces?" In anticipation of that moment, let me offer these time-tried suggestions:

1) Spend more time. You may simply have to come in more that your one hour out of class for every hour in class. Six or seven hour instead of merely five hours a week.

2) Get better ideas bfore making your piece. You may want to look through more Ceramics Monthly magazines. Spend more time making sketches in your journal/sketchbook.

3) Make it bigger. Bigger is not necessarily better, but sometimes it may be. Make more. You may need to make three instead of one and choose the best one.

4) Finer crafting. This is partly a function of time, partly of learning. You need to master the skills: perhaps cleaner edges, a base.

5) Have a clearer conception of what you are tying to communicate.
