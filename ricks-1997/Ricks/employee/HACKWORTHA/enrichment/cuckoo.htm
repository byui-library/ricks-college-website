Back to the Mental Hospital

 

 

 

Ken Kesey

 I recently returned from my third visit to a mental hospital. My first was as a young elder helping to provide a church service for a group of inmates at the Utah state mental hospital;  the second was with Deborah Blau, a sixteen-year-old schizophrenic in the novel,  I Never Promised You a Rose Garden; and the third visit allowed a pleasant yet painful acquaintance with Patrick McMurphy, Big Nurse, Chief Bromden and others in Ken Kesey's six million copy seller, One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest.

In this captivative narrative, Kesey implies various themes: the positive theraputic value of humor; the weak canabalized by the strong; the oppression of bureacratic order; and the destructive power of domineering women. It is relating to this last theme that these comments will deal. In One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest, women are seen as destroyers of man's masculinity.

The most prominent destroyer is Big Nurse (Miss Ratched), a therapist who is most in need of therapy. This unborn female is at war with her male patients partly because she has never accepted her role as a woman.    With such an acceptance she would be less hateful toward her imagined enemy, maleness.

Miss Ratched's resentment towards her female role is suggested by Chief Bromden.  He feels the otherwise "perfect work" of wretchedness has a flaw. Speaking of her, Bromden says,  "A mistake was made somehow in manufacturing, putting those . . . on what would otherwise been a perfect work, and you can see how bitter she is about it."

Early in the book Kesey's hero, Patrick McMurphy, identifies Big Nurse's motive and methods.  After-a session of group therapy, he informs the men that they had just been to a chicken pecking party, that Miss Ratched was doing the pecking and that she was drawing blood.  Harding thought she was pecking at their eyes, but McMurphy said it was not their eyes she was pecking at.

If you're up against a guy who wants to win by making you weaker instead of making himself stronger, then watch for his knees, he's gonna go for your vitals. And that's what the old buzzard is doing, going for your vitals.   (p. 57)

Miss Ratched proceeds throughout the book with porcelain - smooth, calculated precision to accomplish her goals.  With great endurance and power, this stiff-walking, orange - lipped machine constantly hammers away at her male patients' sense of masculinity.

The patients finally confirm that McMurphy is accurate.  "You are right," Harding says, "about all of it. . . .   No one's ever dared come out and say it before, but there's not a man among us that doesn't feel just as you do about her and the whole business - feel it somewhere down deep in his scared little soul" (59).  Sorrow and fear, produced by the narrative, is felt because Miss Ratched is effective and successful.

Another women seen as a destroyer is Billy Bibbit's mother.  At one time Billy was in love and wanted to marry, but his mother talked him out of it. She would not loosen the Oedipal chain which bound and restrained Billy from reaching his own emotional maturity.

Because of mounting insecurities, Billy eventually committed himself into the mental hospital. Then, when his mother visited him, by her dialogue, Mrs. Bibbit continued to reinforce Billy's unhealthy dependency upon herself.

On one occasion McMurphy smuggled some women, Candy and Sandy, into the hospital and arranged for Billy to spend some time with Candy.  Billy and Candy were later caught together and Big Nurse threatened to tell Billy's mother. The potential embarrassment was too threatening for Billy, and he committed suicide.

Here a domineering woman, unwilling to share Billy's love with any women, unwilling to allow Billy's own healthy emotional development, is seen as a contributing cause of Billy's death.  Actually, none of the women in the novel strengthen the men's sense of masculine identity. Even the good women, Candy and Sandy, are consorted with only while drunk.

My third visit in a mental hospital with Big Nurse, Bromden, McMurphy and the other inmates was a bitter - sweet experience. Confronting Big Nurse, the dominating destroyer, was particularly interesting, memorable, and fearful.  However, at the conclusion of my admittance, I am consoled to realize that such destroying women in my life are confined only to Kesey's novel.
