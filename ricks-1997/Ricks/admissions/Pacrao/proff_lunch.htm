PROFESSIONAL ISSUES LUNCHEON

Dr. Terry Roberts, Antioch University, was originally scheduled to be the Professional Issues Luncheon speaker.  In October 1999, it was announced that Terry and other members of the Little Rock Nine were to receive the congressional Gold Medal on Tuesday, November 9, and so Terry was unable to honor PACRAO's invitation.  In Terry's place, Clifford A.  Ramirez was slated to share from his experiences as a Staff Welfare Coordinator for UCLA's Division of Student Affairs.  

Getting to What Matters Most:  Reflections for the New Millennium

As we approach the millennium, three major areas of concern occupy student services staff-the desire to make a contribution, the need to be valued and to be involved in something worthwhile, and a need for balance and fulfillment in life.

Gone are the days when people just came to work "to do their jobs and go home."  Not only do our workplaces demand more from us, staff members want to have a sense of contributing to their work.  As managers, we seek individuals with high emotional intelligence, those who are able to control their own emotions and regulate their actions as well as control their emotions and behavior in relation to others.  In the philosophy of The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People, we call these areas the private victory and the public victory.

Blaine Lee, in his book The Power Principle, charges us with this fundamental challenge: "The principles you live by create the world you live in; if you can change your principles, you can change your world."  In other words, we have power and influence over the kind of world in which we live and work.  Imagine that!  We have the power to influence our world!

Higher education is a worthy endeavor, indeed, but if we are really ready to take up the challenge, we must broaden our perspective to include our organizations, our communities, and the greater world in which we live.  The millennium doomsday prophets are already at work, but their predictions need not be our future.  The question for all of us is about how much more we can do for our world.  What, after all, is our attitude toward the world around us?  Like National Geographic's mission to "Celebrate what's right with the world," our individual mission statements can reflect a broader perspective and a compelling responsibility that creates a positive affirmation of our world.

In the midst of ever-increasing demands, we are all crying out for greater balance in our lives.  We ask ourselves what matters most and identifying these first things, we gauge the amount of time we spend engaged with our first things.  It is the gap between what we say our first things are and the actual time we spend with what matters most to us that is the source of our frustration.  What are we to do?

Our paradigm of balance is flawed if we look to models like the scales of justice to ensure equal time for all of our priorities.  They are not all equal.  And although everyone is talking "productivity," we have essentially lost our appreciation for the true meaning of the word.  From the Latin words pro, meaning and duce or ducere, to lead forward, productivity means "leading forward."  To what?  To accomplishing what matters most in our lives.

We have to be persistent in achieving our goals.  In the race of life, we need to keep our vision before us to motivate us despite the momentary setbacks and temporary failures.  As the poem goes, "For winning is no more than this: To rise each time you fall."

We have to stay focused so that at the moment of choice, our decisions reflect our integrity and contribute to really making a difference in our world.  Like Shakespeare's character, we ask ourselves, "What win I if I gain the thing I seek?"  And it the answer to this question that influences the choices we make and the legacy we leave behind.
