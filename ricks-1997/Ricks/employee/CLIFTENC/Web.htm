Lesson 7 Periodical Indexes  pp. 39-48

    Library terminology can be confusing and is often used interchangeably.  Periodicals are a part of a broader designation known as Serials (publications issued regularly or irregularly and intended to be continued indefinitely).

All these are considered Serials:
    Periodicals which include journals and magazines
    Newspapers
    Continuations
    Yearbooks
    Annual reports, etc

    Indexes help us access the vauable information found in journals, magazines, and newspapers.  These articles are often sources of  information too new or too obscure to be found in books.  Articles in professional journals keep the field informed of current trends and research.  These journals are often peer reviewed (refereed) and usually contain bibliographies.

  Become acquainted with the various indexes that are available in the library.  Every index does not satisfy every need.  Knowing the scope of the index, including the years covered and kinds of periodicals that are indexed, will help you use the right index for your research.

Lesson 8 Newspaper Indexes  pp. 48-52

    How to deal with yesterday's or last week's events can be a problem for indexing.  Paper indexes, such as the Index for the New York Times, are often 3 to 6 months behind.  CD-ROM indexes which might come out monthly are little better.  With publishers now indexing via the Internet, articles in newspapers as well as periodicals are immediately indexed and updated.

Become acquainted with the various newspaper indexes that are available in the library.  Internet access to news can be locate on the Internet Resources page.  Today's news as well as news for the past month are on  NewsBank, Facts on File and SIRS.

Some indexes are searched by subject rather than by newspaper name.  NewsBank is an example of this.  The citation will, however, tell the newspaper from which the article has been taken along with date, page, etc.

Know how to use the microform reader/printers for newspapers and periodicals that are backed-up in microform.

Lesson 6 Dictionaries and Encyclopedias  pp. 60-68

    Dictionaries and Encyclopedias are the reference works in libraries most likely to be found in a home.  Being aware of the various choices can help you evaluate what is available.

Read Ken Kister's "The Big Dictionaries:  Hoards and Hordes of Words," Wilson Library Bulletin. (Feb. 1988): 38-43.  A copy of this is located in Reserve.

What is an abridged dictionary? an unabridged dictionary?  Besides definitions and pronunciation what are some of the other uses of dictionaries?  What is the difference between a prescriptive dictionary and a descriptive dictionary?   What is the OED?  Does the name "Webster" guarantee a good dictionary?

Electronic Encyclopedias have many advantages, but just as with electronic books, there are also disadvantages. Be aware of these as you compare the different formats.  Visit Britannica  for an example of  an online encyclopedia.  Try some of the links to the Internet.

As you compare encyclopedias notice:
    1.  The alphabetical arrangement.  Is it letter-by-letter or word-by-word?
    2.  Usefulness to a research project.
            a. Broad overview of a general subject
            b. Narrower topics and issues
            c.  Study guides
            d.  Bibliographies
            e.  Authorities in the field
    3.  Use of the index gives all related articles dealing with the subject
      Subject or specialized encyclopedias will do all the above but will usually have more in-depth articles on specific subjects.

Locate a specialized encyclopedia by using the online catalog.  Type in your subject along with the word "encyclopedia." If this does not find one, use First Stop: Master Index to Subject Encyclopedias  located at the Information Desk or browse the reference stacks in your subject area.

Lesson 14 Bibliographies  pp. 85-89

    Bibliographies are a way to retrieve precise information needed to locate information on a subject.  Lists of books or study of books which tell the author, title, publisher, time and place of publication and often cost are considered bibliographies..
    Examples:
            a.  Catalogs
                    Union (What other libraries have)
                    Library (What this library has)
            b.  Trade bibliographies
        Books in Print
                    Publisher catalogs
        Publishers Weekly
       c.  Subject bibliographies
        Bibliography Index
       Reference guides
                    Readers' guides

    The online catalog is another way to locate bibliographies on a subject.  Using the general keyword search, type in your subject and the word "bibliograph*."   Those books which have bibliographies will come up.
