Redshirting common at BYU-I


Jim Higgins
scroll staff


College athletics has a slew of terms that can be as confusing as the BCS rating system. One of the trickiest issues to deal with is eligibility.

Every year teams are put on probation and suspension for violating rules of eligibility and recruitment. Who is eligible to play? Who can be recruited? Who practices with the team and who actually dresses on game day?

Any athlete who plays college sports has four years of eligibility. That is, they have four years to be considered a varsity athlete who competes on game day.

Two terms to deal with when discussing eligibility are “redshirt” and “grayshirt.” The definitions of these two terms are just as complicated as the eligibility rules themselves.

In simple terms, a redshirt is an athlete who practices with the team and can suit up for games but not play in them. The only exception is for athletes who play in less than 20-percent of the teams’ games and then suffers a season-ending injury. These athletes can be granted medical redshirts.

The practice of redshirting was adopted to protect athletes who were injured. An athlete can claim one redshirt year in the NCAA and still have an additional four years of eligibility.

Currently, colleges will use a redshirt year with freshmen that have little chance of playing with the team in their first year. During a redshirt season, an athlete must meet eligibility requirements. The athlete may practice with the team, but is not allowed to play in any games.

Redshirting is a common practice in Viking athletics.

With little hope of getting major playing time in their first year out of high school, many male athletes redshirt the year before their mission and come back with four years of eligibility left.

A grayshirt is an eligibility rule associated with the NJCAA. It was designed to let part-time athletes participate with the team without using their four years of eligibility or their redshirt year.

An athlete who is enrolled in less than 12 credit hours can apply the grayshirt rule, and still practice with the team. When a grayshirt transfers, they still have a redshirt season in addition to four years of eligibility left in an NCAA program.

What are the benefits of either of these rules? Grayshirting gives an athlete the chance to practice and develop skills without using eligibility.

“Instead of taking up my redshirt year, [grayshirting] made it possible to practice with the team and still have a redshirt year when I transfer,” freshman guard Aaron Bowman, from Ephrata, Wash., said
The only disadvantage to using a grayshirt is that it can slow down a student athlete’s progress in school.

“We don’t use it unless we absolutely have to,” Garth Hall, athletic director, said. “It can delay what they need to be doing. It can interfere with people who need to move on.”

“The only other drawback is opportunities are prevented from being passed on to others,” Hall said. “At other community colleges it is not as big a deal.”

Grayshirting does not really affect an athlete’s progress anyway because any athlete planning on being in a NCAA program knows they are going to be there for four or five years anyway, Ryan Moats, a grayshirt freshman guard from Highlands Ranch, Colo., said.

“[Grayshirting] is not for everybody. I think it is a good rule, but it can be abused,” Hall said.
