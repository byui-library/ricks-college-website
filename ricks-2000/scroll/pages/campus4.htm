GENETICS AND GENEALOGY COMES TOGETHER AT RICKS

by BONNIE McDONALD
city editor

Brigham Young University researchers presented at Ricks Friday night a new way to uncover genealogy using genetic material.

Stan Graham, a graduate student at BYU, explained the process of molecular genealogy that he and his colleagues have been studying since March.

The Molecular Genealogy Research Project, or MGRP, is exploring the possibility that people will be able to trace their ancestors through blood lines. Genetic "family trees," based on DNA, carry specific molecular markers that are unique to an individual family line. People will eventually be able to have their blood tested for these markers and be compared to those in the database that is now being created.

Because "the only way you could really test [the DNA of ancestors] is to dig up dead people," Graham said, researchers must instead find similar genetic traits in the DNA of living relatives and then correlate the information with written pedigree charts. Graham said the process relies on educated inferences, but they have been accepted so far.

Graham said the program is in two stages. The first includes building the database that will ideally include over 100,000 samples from 400 different human populations. The second stage will begin once the database is complied and people can submit their blood to be compared to the family groups in the database. This will be in approximately four years.

"We hope to make the database available to the world," Graham said.

MGRP's theme is "Welding the world together, link by link." The program's goals include reconstructing personal genealogies, producing unique identifications for people who do not know their name-based genealogy and preserving existing family lines for future reference.

The BYU researchers came to Ricks because Stephanie Chidester, a freshman from San Diego, Calif., found out about it and invited them to campus. She got the program approved through the college's administration, advertised in newspapers and on campus and helped run things at the presentation.

"I was really intrigued by how this actually worked," Chidester said.

Chidester said she became interested in family history last year when her grandmother introduced her to the process.

"I've really gotten into it," she said. "When I get more involved in doing it and helping other people do it, it seems more real."

More than 60 Ricks College students and community members attended the presentation Friday out of curiosity and desire to further genealogical research. Most of them donated blood.

Russell Thurston, a Ricks College biology faculty member, said he felt obligated to be there because he teaches heredity and it was an opportunity to learn about molecular genealogy.

Bonnie and Ray Price, Rexburg residents, said they attended the presentation because genealogy and family history is more than just a hobby for them. Bonnie has been doing genealogy for over 20 years.

"It's a continual thing. It never gets done," she said. "We believe in [genealogy] very strongly. From here on out it will affect our posterity," Ray said.

Ammon Serr, a sophomore from Denver, Colo., went to the presentation because "it seems like [molecular genealogy] has the potential to go to the future, so I figure I might as well start at the beginning."

Students are also interested in genealogy for spiritual reasons.

"Because family history is one of the three missions of the Church, the Lord will continually bring to pass ways to fulfill it. I want to be a part of it, " Ammon Buehler, a sophomore from Yakima, Wash., said.

For more information about molecular genealogy, go to http://molecular-genealogy.byu.edu.
