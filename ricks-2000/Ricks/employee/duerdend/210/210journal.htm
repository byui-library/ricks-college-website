Parenting Learning Journals

Your Learning Journal, shouldn't be difficult, but it will take time and thought. It will be read and discussed by your group members(*) and sometimes by the instructor. Following these guidelines, you should be able to receive full credit.
These are due in your folder at beginning of class each Monday (or Wednesday if there's a Monday holiday), 15 total, readable in dark ink, but not necessarily typed (Format 1 journals must have a word-count) and then a collection of 10 of these journals, typed, turned in when you are ready.
You may chose to do your Learning Journal in either of these two formats.
Format 1) Each week, in a 300 word (minimum) informal essay discuss your "informed reaction" to any of the topics of the week and emphasize their application in your life, or, you may find some "discovery" in the media, internet, etc., related to the week's topics, and submit it with a discussion of it's relevance and application in your own words. The brief article or discovery can be of any length, but your discussion needs to be an additional 200 words. (See more explanation below.)
Format 2) Each week, identify 3 principles of parenting or family relationships related to our readings and study for the previous week. Then briefly state why you think they are universal and meaningful. There is no word count specified for this format, but it requires a little more thought even if it might be less written words. (See more explanation below.)

More explantion for Format 1 -- An "informed reaction" is different from an off-the-cuff "personal reaction" in that the former indicates that you have read or studied something about the topic and are not just "fakin' it" with whatever comes off the top of your head on the assigned topic. Convince us you read or took notes in class and thought about the topic.
With either an "informed reaction" journal or a "discovery" journal, a good plan is to state why the topic is significant to you (what important things you learned), and then, show you understand it by your analysis of the topic, tying it to the readings, discussion, etc. and discuss its application to your life, past, present or future. (Relating the topic to something in "The Family Proclamation" is always appropriate, too.)
It does not need to be typed, but should be easily readable. These journal entries should be much more than just your notes, or some scribbled agreements or disagreements with the teacher. Let it be a college-level, informed analysis and evaluation that shows you understand the topic and that you have thought critically about it's meaning and application - - not just adolescent dreaming or opinion. But, it should not be a "preachment" where you wax eloquent and judgmental. Nor should it be a "report" that reads like something from the book, it should be your best personal insights and applications. You can have strong opinions, but don't be dogmatic or closeminded. Show how the issue/topic has caused you to think and has application in your life -- not what you think everyone else should do.
Be sure your journal entries have your name, are, dated, clear, readable, and at least 300 words (about a full page typed, double-spaced -- or well over a full page, handwritten). INCLUDE A WORD COUNT.
Remember, you will turn in your 10 best entries, typed, near the end of the semester, so it will be easier to type them as you go along.

More explanation for Format 2 -- "Principles" are a little hard to "nail down." They are fundamental, universal truths that are more than just scientific facts or research findings. They are factual, but are more meaningful than just an obvious statement of fact. The Proclamation on the Family is full of principles such as,"The family is central to the Creator's plan for the eternal destiny of His children," and "Happiness in family life is most likely to be achieved when founded upon the teachings of the Lord Jesus Christ." Other principles one might derive from a study of parenting could be such things as, "Children are entitled to, and thrive better in two parent homes," or "How we 'see' children, through our theories and practices, can influence their outcomes and happiness in life." After you conceptualize the principle, briefly explain why you think it is important and meaningful, perhaps with some personal application. These should be your best "capture and synthesis" of the material covered in this course. It should constitute the things you've really learned and really want to retain for your future family. Near the end of the semester you will turn in your 30 best principles, with brief personal expanations of each (or whatever combination of principles and journals you did throughout the semester.)
NOTE: Anything put in the folder after 5 minutes into class will be marked late -- half credit. It is also half-credit if you were not present but turned in your entry early or had someone bring it to class for you. You need to attend class to get full credit. Don't try to have a group member sneak it in to the folder "as a favor" if you are late or not present. This constitutes cheating and is an honor code violation for both of you. It is also half credit if you are writing it in class while others are sharing journals.
(*) If a journal entry proves to be of a more personal nature, and you prefer to not have your group members read it, you may submit it directly to the instructor at the beginning of class.

Group member grading responsibilities ----if it is mostly a "report" or poor quality -- half credit.
Final ones need to be in good gramatical format -- college level, no glaring typos, spell-checked, etc. I'll let a couple go by, but not many without point deduction.
