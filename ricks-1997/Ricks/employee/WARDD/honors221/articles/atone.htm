At-One with Us

David L. Ward
Ricks College 17th Ward
October 31, 1999

        We are drawn to Jesus as we come to understand His expansive willingness to receive and embrace us as we are, that our association with Him may convert us into all we have the potential to become. Another way to say this is that we feel to "come unto Christ" as we begin truly to believe that His greatest desire is to be "At-One" with us, notwithstanding the mistakes we have made, the sins we've committed, or the many times we "fall" in an effort to live true to Him. But this understanding of Christ's readiness to receive us must be the kind that is opened to us by the Spirit through our desire to know. We must desire to know Christ in this way for ourselves, or the knowledge we have of Christ will always be "second-hand knowledge."

        Alma testified of the revelation we can experience when he witnessed the way he gained "first-hand knowledge" of the Savior's power to redeem mankind. Said he,

    Do you not suppose that I know of these things of myself? Behold, I testify unto you that I do know that these things whereof I have spoken are true. And how do ye suppose that I know of their surety?

    Behold, I say unto you they are made known unto me by the Holy Spirit of God. Behold I have fasted and prayed many days that I might know these things of myself. And now I do know of myself that they are true; for the Lord God hath made them manifest unto me by his Holy Spirit; and this is the spirit of revelation which is in me.

    I say unto you that I know of myself . . . that Jesus Christ shall come, yea, the Son, the Only Begotten of the Father, full of grace, and mercy, and truth. And behold it is he that cometh to take away the sins of the world, yea the sins of every man [of every woman] who steadfastly believeth on his name (Alma 5:45-46, 48).

        To believe steadfastly on the name of Jesus we must believe two truths about our heavenly Father, but believe them, not as mere doctrinal concepts or Sunday School lesson-topics; not as traits of God told to us by others at one time or another; not even as religious points made to keep us in line. No we need to believe these truths as two fundamental truths about our real Father who lives in heaven, truths that, if believed, should invite us to "come to Christ" more diligently than we've "come" before. In the words of the Prophet Joseph Smith, the two fundamental truths about our Father are these: "Our heavenly Father is more liberal in His views, and boundless in His mercies and blessings, than we are ready to believe or receive; and at the same time, is more terrible to the workers of iniquity, and more awful in the executions of His punishments, and more ready to detect every false way, than we are apt to suppose Him to be" (TPJS, 257).

        If we hear this brief description as the truth about our heavenly Father, coupled with King Benjamin's declaration (and that of all the prophets) that Christ's "is the only name given whereby salvation cometh" (Mosiah 5:8), we are most likely to be drawn by the influence of the Spirit toward Jesus as "[our] way, [our] truth, [our] life" (John 14:6). God knows more than anyone else how to work with us through the dynamics of our mortal thoughts and feelings while honoring the boundaries of the moral agency he blessed us with. Knowing us as He does, he invites us to come to His Son by revealing to us the eternal possibilities if we do, and the eternal punishments if we don't. But He doesn't reveal the punishments we'll have to suffer if we refuse to come to His Son as a forceful threat, something to wield to coerce us into doing what He wants us to do. He knows with eternal clarity what the demands of Justice will exact from us; He remembers with exactness the instruction He gave to us premortally about those demands; and He rejoices in the fact that we chose to risk the danger of spiritual death in this fallen world for the eternal blessing of choosing once again to fight and live for the cause of Christ-which is, ultimately, our most profound cause (Moses 1:39).

        Therefore, through His prophets, our Father in heaven reveals again the condition we accepted in that Premortal Council by which spiritual death may be overcome in all its dimensions. That condition is to believe in Christ steadfastly.

        The moment we genuinely begin to accept this condition with our hearts, considering introspectively with our minds the awe-full alternatives, something wonderful begins to happen. We begin to feel the possibility that we may be forgiven of our sins. The hopeful message of Christ's living and dying and living again for us begins to be born in our hearts, and we are drawn to Him, notwithstanding our sins. This feeling of possibility, this gentle urging, is the Father's invitation to us to come to His Son and be exempted from the punishments of Justice. It is the promise of Jesus being made real in our lives as recorded in John, wherein Christ said, "No man can come to me, except the Father which hath sent me draw him . . . (John 6:44). As the Father "draws us," we enter into, as it were, our own sacred grove experience, wherein the Father, through the power of the Spirit, says to us, "This is my beloved Son. Hear Him" (JSH 1:17).

        Through steadfastly believing in and listening to Christ through the Spirit, we begin to understand Jesus's love, and why Jesus desires so deeply to be one with us, notwithstanding our sins. In fact, we come to understand that Jesus knows more about the reasons for our sinning and our sinfulness than we do. But he desires to use this knowledge, not to condemn us, but to set us free from our weaknesses that exist deep within our souls, and out of our fallen reach (John 3:16-18; D&C 34:1-4). Whereas we have some understanding of what we've done that's wrong or sinful in relation to the commandments we've been taught and encouraged to keep, He comprehends our sins in relation to the doctrine of the Fall, and the impossible odds of achieving the purposes of God in this life without Him. As such He perceives the Fall, not primarily as a doctrine taught in church or in the scriptures as we may be inclined to do, but as the challenging reality that encompasses each one of us, pulling us down as surely as gravity does, with each step we take to live true to the Christ-cause we fought for in the Pre-mortal world. Understanding the challenging, fallen reality of our lives, Christ continues to reach out to us, to "reach our reaching" ("Where Can I Turn for Peace," #219), so long as His reach and our agency combine to nurture in us a willingness to look to Him more than to ourselves or anyone else, and, in so doing, to get up, and try again.

        In this connection, C. S. Lewis has written:

    We may, indeed, be sure that perfect chastity-like perfect charity-will not be attained by any merely human effort. You must ask for God's help. Even when you have done so, it may seem to you for a long time that no help, or less help than you need, is being given. Never mind. After each failure, ask forgiveness, pick yourself up, and try again. Very often what God first helps us towards is not the virtue itself but just this power of always trying again. For however important chastity (or courage, or truthfulness, or any other virtue) may be, this process trains us in habits of soul which are more important still. It cures our illusions about ourselves and teaches us to depend on God. We learn, on the one hand, that we cannot trust ourselves even in our best moments, and, on the other, that we need not despair even in our worst, for our failures are forgiven. The only fatal thing is to sit down content with anything less than perfection" (Mere Christianity, 94).

        One of the truths that Christ knows so well, the truth that is behind His constant effort and power to lift us higher than we can climb on our own--is this: that "sin," as President Kimball taught us, is wrong, but "sin results from deep and unmet needs on the part of the sinner" ("Jesus the Perfect Leader," 1979 August Ensign, 5). This is a truth we must learn for ourselves by the power of the Spirit if we desire to be perfected in Christ. Christ's comprehension of this truth is what empowers Him to love the sinner while He does everything in His power to make the sin hateful to us. Our understanding of this truth empowers us to repent by submitting ourselves to the promptings of the Spirit even when submission is difficult and painful, and to do so in full confidence that the Savior loves us.

        But Christ knows something more about sin, something that presses Him to the point of heartbreak as He ministers untiringly to nurture in us a desire to choose Him over sin and Satan. He know that sin keeps us ignorant of His deepest desires to love us better and more deeply than anyone else can. He knows that sin turns us away from the truth of His persistent efforts to be with us again and again; that it shuts our hearts to the truth that He is pleading our case against an eternally exacting judge called Justice. Satan would not have us consider the truths of Christ's advocacy in our behalf. Rather he would deceive us into believing that Christ is the Grand Prosecutor against us. But what does Christ Himself say? In the Doctrine and Covenants we read:

    Listen to him who is the advocate with the Father [against Justice], who is pleading your cause before him-

    Saying: Father, behold the sufferings and death of him who did no sin, in whom thou wast well pleased; behold the blood of thy Son which was shed, the blood of him whom thou gavest that thyself might be glorified;

    Wherefore, Father, spare these my brethren [and sisters] that believe on my name, that they may come unto me and have eternal life (D&C 45:3-5).

Again:

    Thus saith the Lord your God, even Jesus Christ, the Great I Am, Alpha and Omega, the beginning and the end . . . .

    I am the same which have taken the Zion of Enoch into mine own bosom; and verily, I say, even as many as have believed in my name, for I am Christ, and in mine own name, by the virtue of the blood which I have spilt, have I pleaded before the Father for them.

    Wherefore gird up your loins and be prepared. Behold the kingdom is yours, and the enemy shall not overcome (D&C 38:1, 4, 9).

        Satan knows too well the tender desires of his Brother to save and exalt us. But he is forever and always against Him. Satan lives to block us from feeling the tender yearnings our Savior feels to be one with us, and does so through manipulating the feelings we experience in sin. That master of illusion and deception would have us believe that the feelings of disgust and estrangement, of repulsion and abandonment we experience when we live contrary to the teachings of Jesus are feelings the Savior has for us. In reality, they are feelings our spirits experience against our fallen selves so that we may "taste the bitter, to know to prize the good" (Moses 6:55).

        Christ knows the purposes of the bitter, and He knows the joy of the sweet. But He also knows how Satan lives to seduce us into settling for the bitter-sweetness of sin at times when God's will for us seems bitter to our natural taste. Christ knows more than we how easy it is for us to turn to whatever source of comfort we can get at times when our needs cry out to be met, but when the means of meeting them seem hopelessly out of reach. He knows what it feels like to be lonely, cast aside, physically and emotionally abused, treated unjustly, ignored, put down, misunderstood, disappointed, betrayed, and despised. He knows that, at such times, our natural inclination is to find friendship whatever the cost may extract from our souls. He understands all too well the natural decision to give up and give ourselves over to some one or some thing--even at the cost of addiction--if it will bring some relief from the turmoil we feel from being bewildered and confused by our neediness as we are ignored by the insensitive apathy of the world. In the face of life's confusions and contradictions, the apostle Paul encourages us to take heart in the fact that Christ suffered greater contradiction in life than any one ever could, and to remember this "lest we become weary and faint in our minds" (Hebrews 12:3) because of what seems so unfair to us, or so impossible to overcome.

        In pointing this out, Paul isn't affirming that Jesus's experience is so much more than we could ever bear, so much more than what we're going through, as if to say, "Buck up. What do you have to worry about? Your burden, your concern, your abuse or injustice is nothing compared with Jesus's." He's not sharing the truth of Christ's suffering to make us feel ashamed of feeling our own as deeply as we do. He's doing so to give us hope in Christ's capacity to redeem us through our suffering if we suffer in Christ's way.

        One of the most sacred truths about Christ's sufferings is that He may have suffered once and for all mankind in the Garden of Gethsemane, and once and for all of us on the Cross at Calvary, but his willingness to suffer such pain then, empowered Him to enter into our personal sufferings now. His willingness to enter the realms of Hell itself, wherein the payment of each and every sin we've committed, the price of each sin of every man and woman who has ever lived, who lives now, and who will ever live; the payment, the remorse, the guilt, the turmoil, self-doubt and worthlessness brought on by every sin known to mankind--all this was compounded upon Christ as one heavy and suffocating debt. And it was precisely this willingness to pay our debt with His life and blood, that equipped Christ with the power to enter into our own personal sufferings, and to strengthen us as once an angel strengthened Him (Luke 22:43).
