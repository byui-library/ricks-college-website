Course Description:

American Heritage 100 is a brief but important overview of major themes that form the core of the American national experience. The course integrates themes from three academic disciplines-political science, history, and economics. Each discipline offers special insight into America's heritage. From political science, we glean perspectives on the nature of political theory, constitutional principles, and government operations. From economics, we glean the essentials of economic theory and the workings of the market economy, as well as perspectives to compare different economic systems. From history, we glean examples of real events that strengthen our understanding of theory and help us evaluate our political and economic heritage from a historical perspective. Studying all three together gives us a better opportunity to understand our struggle for freedom, justice, equality, prosperity, and "The Good Life."

This course offers you an important opportunity that may never again be repeated in your life. You are encouraged to use the opportunity wisely. It is to be hoped that this course will help you better appreciate the miracle of our nation's beginning and the singular nature of our political system. You should also come to better understand the strengths of our economic system and the natural way it has unlocked individual initiative and created prosperity and abundance. The themes presented here will help add depth and meaning to your understanding of our "American Heritage." Hopefully, it will also add depth and meaning to your testimony that God governs in the affairs of men, and that his influence on the development of the United States has had a greater impact on modern man's quest to govern himself in the interests of liberty and freedom than any other event or series of events in the history of the world.

Required Reading:

American Heritage 100 Notebook
 By Gary L. Marshall

American Heritage 100 Reader
By Gary L. Marshall & Eric Walz

It is important that you read the material in the reader and in the notebook. Much of the reading is thought provoking if read with the intent to understand. At the beginning of each topic outline the notebook provides you with a specific reading assignment and reading terms for which you are responsible. Pay special attention to the pages you are asked to read. The readings in the Reader have been carefully selected so that they present the ideas most important to your study. The reading terms are meant to help you focus on the important ideas, concepts, and facts from the reading. You must understand the terms and concepts as they relate to the broad context of American Heritage in order to understand the questions on the exams. About 15-25% of the exam questions will be based solely on the American heritage Reader. You should do the reading assigned for each topic before you come to class. If you will do this, the discussion in class will be more meaningful to you, and you will have more success in understanding the ideas presented in class.

Class Format:

Most class lectures will parallel material given in the texts. The lectures are designed to add insight and understanding to the material that you read. Some lectures will present new material that is not found in the text. It is extremely important that you take good notes in class. Exam questions will come from the lectures and from the text, but at least 75-85% of the questions will be based on the material as it is presented in class.

Lecture Notes and Study Questions:

The course notebook contains an outline of each lecture topic. The various terms and concepts contained in the topic outlines are extremely important. You are encouraged to add notes to the outline during each class period to help clarify concepts and define principles. Understanding each point is important to your doing well on the exams. Most of the questions on the exams are based on these outlines. Study them well.

Exams:

There will be 4 exams given during the course. Each exam will consist of 50 objective questions (approximately 40 multiple choice and 10 True / False). All of the exams will be given in the testing center. The dates the exams will be open are given in the Class Schedule included in this syllabus. None of the exams are comprehensive, including the final. Each of the exams covers only the material for that unit.

The multiple choice and true/false questions will not be easily answered if you have not paid the price to read and study the material carefully. Since the content of the class is based on theories, themes, and concepts, the questions are designed to evaluate the depth of your understanding as well as your factual knowledge. Here are some facts about the exams that you must keep in mind:

--All four exams must be taken. Failure to take any of the four exams will result in a failing grade for the course.
--You must take all of the exams when they are scheduled.
--In order to protect the integrity and validity of the exams, American Heritage exams are not returned to the students. You can make arrangements to come to my office to see an exam after it has closed in the Testing Center.
--Exams are given only once. You may not take the exam a second time.
--If you become ill and cannot take an exam at the Testing Center when it is scheduled, you should contact me before the test closes. We then can make arrangements for you to take the exam.
--If you fail to take any exam because of a conflicting school sponsored activity, you should contact me before you leave so that the exam can be made up at a time convenient to both of us.
--If you fail to take any exam for a reason that is really your own fault, you should also contact me immediately. Remember, you must take all the regular exams. You will be required to make up the exam, but there will be at least a 10% penalty depending on the circumstances.

Attendance:

Attendance is required for all class sessions. A roll will be passed around, and you should initial it each day. It is your responsibility to make sure your attendance has been recorded! Fifty points will be given for attendance. This is part of your regular grade; it is not extra credit. Starting with the third absence, you will begin losing five points for each day you are absent.

Important note: A person who misses more that one-third of the class sessions will be given a failing grade regardless of his or her exam scores. I want you to be in class so that you can learn with your heart as well as with your mind. This means if you miss ten or more days during the summer session, you will not receive credit for the class.
