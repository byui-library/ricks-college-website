The Information Society

We encounter an almost overwhelming number of messages in our everyday lives.

These messages emanate from face-to-face conversations, speeches, televisions, radios, newspapers, records, business reports, mail, telephones, government notices and forms, scientific reports, advertisements, watches and clocks, menus, recipes, billboards, books, video games, videodiscs, video cassettes, pay television systems, and office and home computers.

Whether at work or at home, we are constantly dealing with information. We engage in an ongoing cycle of initiating, processing, patterning or arranging, preparing, transmitting, disseminating, retrieving, receiving, and/or reprocessing information. It has recently been reported that "the combined hours devoted to watching television, reading, and talking on the telephone constitute 39 percent of all of the available hours within an entire year. If the time devoted to sleep is discounted, we spend 58 percent of our waking hours engaged in these media activities".]

We live in an information society!

The Information Economy

Information now functions as the core of the United States economy and labor force. Not only do we use information, we depend upon information for our economic survival. The U.S. economy has shifted rapidly from an agricultural base, to an industrial and service economy, and now to an information-based economy.

Facts that have emerged about the information economy include:

  • The most significant growth in the U.S. gross national product is due to the development of
  • data, exchange of information, manipulation of ideas, and transfer of information.
  • More people are employed in the information sector of the U.S. economy than in any other

  • other sector.
  • A significant portion of the labor force works as information processors in the industrial sector

The Role of the Communication Specialist

Communication specialists play a critical role in the information society. In some cases they generate new information. But, in most cases, communication specialists are trained to devise systems which link new information into ever changing social and cultural systems. For example, communication specialists are likely to identify what is useful information from the vast quantities of available information. Or, communication specialists may attempt to channel useful information to appropriate audiences, determine which media are the most apt channels to convey information, or assess the likely social consequences of information upon particular audiences. Ultimately, communication specialists facilitate the transformation of information into meaningful knowledge for human beings.

Communicating with others is no longer a matter of merely conveying information to others. The growth of the information economy has been matched by a similar growth in cultural diversity in the United States. Women and minorities such as African-Americans and Latinos, as well as a host of other minorities, are increasingly entering professions in the information society. Communicating is complicated. The same piece of information can mean extremely different things to different groups of people. Success in the information professions requires a sensitivity to cultural differences and to the diverse meanings which can be associated with any particular word, nonverbal action, and/or symbol.

Overall, certain conclusions seem inescapable:

  • Regardless of one's chosen occupation, the ability to manipulate information and to communicate effectively has rapidly become an essential component of every sector of the U.S. economy.
  • Virtually every individual requires training and skill as an information processor and communicator in order to exist effectively within the information society.
  • Communication specialists facilitate the transformation of information into meaningful knowledge for human beings.
  • The changing composition of the labor force and consumers in the information society requires that all individuals in all occupations understand and acquire the skills necessary to respond effectively in culturally diverse employment and market environments.

A Communication Program for Every Need

A rich variety of training in communication is offered by college and university departments throughout the United States:
  • Some communication departments focus predominantly on developing the interpersonal skills required for students to function as effective communicators and persuaders.
  • Other communication departments possess a media orientation, preparing students for direct entry into industries such as television, radio, and film.
  • Still other communication departments provide training useful for those working in major corporations or institutions.
  • Some communication departments focus upon communication itself, providing a theoretical framework and research techniques designed to be useful regardless of their specific application.

Within each orientation, departments may differ according to their theoretical or methodological focus, by emphasizing a rhetorical, critical, interpretative, scientific, and/or performance perspective.

Content Emphases of Departments of Communication

A typical two-year or four-year school's department of communication is unlikely to offer majors in all areas of communication. Each school generally specializes in one area or in a small number of fields.

According to a survey of Ph.D. granting institutions with more than one area of study, the ten major concentrations of communication study are:

  1. Broadcasting
  2. Group and/or Organizational Communication
  3. Interpersonal Communication
  4. Theater
  5. Rhetorical Theory/History/Criticism
  6. Film
  7. Communication Education
  8. Journalism
  9. Persuasion/Argumentation
  10. Speech Pathology
If you are interested in majoring in a specific area of communication it is important that you select a school that offers work in your particular area of interest.

Courses Have Different Names

Each department of communication has its own way of labeling and organizing the study of communication

There are also different names used to identify communication departments. Common department titles include: Speech Communication, Communication Studies, Speech and Mass Communication, Speech and Theater, Mass Communication, Communication Arts, and Speech. Consult a catalogue to see how content materials are organized into specific courses at an institution.

Experiential Training

A major or concentrated set of courses in communication is often supplemented with experiential training (i.e., internships, on-site visitations) directly related to the field that a student plans to enter. For example, courses in political science and a public relations internship may be helpful to the person planning to be a lobbyist. Student teaching and graduate school assistantships are excellent supplements for future school and university instructors.

Communicating in All Careers

Communication is a critical feature of all major professions in the United States. Academic courses in communication--in the form of a major, minor, or a focused set of courses--can enhance understanding, performance, and generate success in virtually every profession. Be aware that many industries are now seeking employees with an extensive liberal arts background rather than technical or job-specific training.

Basic Introduction to Communication

A mastery of basic communication is essential in every career. Eight important areas are:

- oral communication
- public speaking
- print communication
- effective writing
- interpersonal communication
- introduction to media systems/mass communication
- principles of audience analysis
- theory, research and methodology in communication

The survey concluded that CEOs "look for graduates who can communicate, in person and on paper. That appears to be the universal need.... They don't seem to want 'salesmen,' lousiness 'intellectuals,' or 'ready-made' lousiness executives (with an M.B.A. in hand). Corporate managers spend about 75% to 80% of their time communicating, and most of that time in oral communication. About 60% of that time is in face-to-face communication within dyadic discussions or in meetings, or via the telephone."
