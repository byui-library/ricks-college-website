ATV's in Montana
© 2000 Allen Hackworth


This September, when Cline Curtis asked me to go with him into the high, wild mountains of Montana to take pictures, I knew the trip would be wonderful, but I had no idea it would be so traumatic. We took our ATV's into a remote area north of Gardiner, Montana and west of the Yellowstone River.

Driving his new, highly polished, red pickup, Cline backed into my driveway. His ATV, a Polaris 400, was already loaded. After connecting my trailer, which carried my Suzuki King Quad, we jumped into his pickup. Cline said, "I always like to start these adventures with a word of prayer." This pleased me. Cline asked God for divine protection. Prayers finished, we headed for the Gallatin National Forest.

Our route took us through Yellowstone National Park. Yellowstone still had many visitors even though our nation's schools had started. Abundant elk and buffalo grazed comfortably in the meadows and near the rivers.

Cline and I shared numerous stories and adventures as we traveled. In Gardiner we bought hamburgers, fries, and a drink. The meat of the thick hamburger weighed a pound, and the toasted buns were stuffed with lettuce, pickles, onions, relish, and tomatoes. We ate while we continued on the road. As the sun lowered in the western sky, it colored purple the mountains across the valley, and it turned pink the low, puffy clouds on the horizon.

After turning off the paved highway onto a dirt road, we began a steady climb toward a remote campground. In the foothills, both rustic cabins and expensive ranch houses added beauty to the land. Near some homes, quarter horses, sleek and firm, stood in fenced pastures, waiting for fall hunting. Also, tall fir, Engelmann spruce, and lodgepole pine grew abundantly on the hills. Soon, as we climbed higher, dusk created its magic making everything soft and muted. As Cline's diesel hummed steadily, we bounced over and floated through this magical land.

Each moment, as we continually changed positions and elevations, new sites caused us to comment on the beauty of this region. By the time we arrived at the campground, it was completely dark. The forest had become mysterious and eerie. Ancient, thick evergreens stood tall and imposing. Even the picnic table was thicker and taller than usual. It looked like it had been built for giant mountain men who gathered here to skin wildcats and grizzlies.

Overhead a few stars flickered through the thick branches. Using the pickup's lights, we set up the tent, broke out the cots, and made our beds. Finally, as I slipped into my sleeping bag, and before Cline turned off the gas lantern, he pulled out a pistol and clicked it ready. He chuckled, "I never camp without my Equalizer."

The next morning, before the eastern sun spilled its autumn luster over the mountains, we ate breakfast, broke camp, and re-packed our belongings in the extended cab. Even in the early light, the camp still seemed dark and foreboding. For convenience in unloaded the ATV's, we moved to a new location. After starting our ATV's, Cline took the lead. For the first few miles, we continued climbing higher, following a seldom-used, logging road.

Soon, however, we bounded left onto a horse/ATV trail which dropped deeper into the forest. We then continued climbing. The crisp, perfumed air, the warm sunlight, and the spacious beauty all told me I was in heaven. Cline drove with gusto, and it was fun chasing him.

After some happy riding, we stopped and hiked through the thick, verdant woods, exploring. Grizzly bear droppings, some fresh and some stale, were scattered in a few locations. We sensed that this land was as wild as the creatures which made their homes here. Soon Cline pointed to a peak and said, "Let's go up there."

I said, "Okay," and we continued our adventure. Because of its many challenges, the trail was perfect for an ATV. Sometimes the trail was steep and rocky. Sometimes we crossed small streams and ravines. Sometimes we climbed over logs and dodged boulders and trees. I could not imagine a better place to ride. Eventually, as we rose higher and higher into the sky, we broke into wide, expansive meadows which were above the tree line. These meadows rolled ever higher to the tops of the mountains and to the peak which was our objective.

After reaching our peak's summit, and after securing our machines, we stood in awe at the beauty before us. The backside of the mountain we had climbed presented rugged, irregular cliffs that dropped a thousand feet below to a valley floor. From our high perch we could see everything: to the west, ski runs at the Big Sky Resort; to the southwest, one hundred miles away, the majestic Tetons; to the south, the 11,000-foot Bear Tooth Range.

In the deep valley below us, a round, liquid eye, a shining, crystal lake, stared into heaven. The lake rested in the middle of a green, soft forest. Additionally, at the crest of the rocky cliff on which we stood was another remarkable sight. Two ancient, petrified tree stumps, 35 to 50 million years old, protruded. How did they get to the top of this mountain? No other trees grew at this altitude.

After drinking cold water and eating snacks, we started our joyful descent. In the winter, this site would make perfect ski runs, but now, we rode our breathing, sure-footed goats off the mountain. (In case you are thinking we hurt the environment, think again. You would be hard pressed even to find our tracks as we gently rolled over the yellow, tough, alpine grasses. We did no harm, leaving fewer marks than would have been left by a horse. However, on the lower, designated, ATV trails, we dug dirt. But these trails were already bare because of the trampling of horses and other off-road vehicles.)

Dropping in altitude, we moved carefully along the challenging trail. At one crossing, the steep bank dropped quickly into a narrow, rocky stream. The other bank was equally steep. Once out of the stream, with only five feet of preparation, the trail climbed immediately for another 300 yards. The first part of this stretch was especially steep. As Cline attempted to climb this section, he felt the front of his Polaris come off the ground. So he cut back on the throttle and stepped off to the left side of his machine. My friend stood on the ground and tried again. But this time the machine bucked on its rear wheels and pitched backward. Cline was thrown to the ground and the the heavy machine crashed into a small tree and onto a large, rotting log. Looking like a turtle on its back, still running but upside down, still smoking exhaust, the Polaris spun its wheels in mid air.

Cline was not hurt, and in time we were able to flip his turtle back into position. Now, with more caution the second time, Cline climbed carefully out of this hole and moved steadily up the hill. I thought, "I'm glad Cline was not hurt, and I'm glad his machine was damaged only slightly." Yet little did I know what would happen just 30 minutes later.

We had traveled most of the way over the trail; however, just 200 yards before the trail joined the logging road, with Cline half way up this last steep hill, his front wheels suddenly jumped into the sky and flipped over backward. This time, Cline was not standing to the left of his machine. This time he had no time to move out of the way. Instinctively, Cline shifted himself slightly left, and he and the machine flew through the air. Because of the height of the hill, Cline fell about 10 feet down the incline and sprawled, face down, on the ground. Luckily, the ATV landed at his side. But then, as Cline continued to slid down the hill, his ATV did a second flip, end over end, and crashed onto its wheels. In the dust and noise, with rocks, machine, and dirt flying, I could not tell if the 800-pound monster had landed on Cline. Although it looked like it had, I hoped desperately it had not.

Cline's machine continued to roll down the hill past me while Cline's body lay motionless on the ground. His head pointed downhill. I secured my machine and rushed to Cline. By now he tried to lift his upper body from the ground. Blood oozed slightly from a cut on his nose. A cut inside his mouth caused blood to form on his teeth. And he had several small cut on the top of his head. The wreck had knocked the wind out of Cline, and he now tried to catch his breath.

Eventually, after some hard work, we drove both machines back to the pickup. We loaded everything. Also, by this time, Cline was back to his normal, positive, robust self. He was even willing to explore another road. In a moment of solitude, I spoke a silent prayer of thanks for God's divine protection. Cline's earlier prayer had been answered. With just a twist of fortune, Cline could have broken his neck or back. He could have broken bones or punctured a lung. A head injury could have easily happened. If I had been following Cline too closely, his machine would have landed on me. I am still thankful to God for Cline's safety and protection. His ATV, however, was not as fortunate, but machines can generally be repaired.

p.s.  Four lessons can be learned from this experience: (1) ask God for His protection; (2) build one's load equally on the front and rear racks on an ATV; (3) buy a King Quad over the Polaris 400; and (4) let Cline go first down the trail to show you what not to do.
