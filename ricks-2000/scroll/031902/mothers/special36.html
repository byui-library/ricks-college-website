Reports of domestic abuse skyrocket

by Lacey Morrill
MOR01031@BYUI.EDU
Scroll Staff

Although domestic abuse is both a national and local problem, more women are finally turning to shelters for protection.

“Statistics show that there are more victims of domestic violence then there has ever been,” Shelly White, director of the YWCA Women in Jeopardy Program, said.

In Utah alone, studies show that more than 40,000 women and 160,000 children live in violent or abusive family conditions. At the same time, the number of women seeking protection from women’s shelters has doubled since 1997, according to the Utah State Domestic Violence Cabinet.

“Shelters are seeing dramatic increases because more women are finally seeking help,” White said.

In Idaho, there are 26 domestic violence shelters. Women’s and Children’s Alliance in Boise saw an increase in the number of women treated at their shelter, executive director Janice Johnston said. An estimated 4,700 calls were made to their crisis hot line and 500 counseling sessions were given to children and teens who had also been affected by violence.

Teenage and child counseling is important because it helps break the cycle of violence, Johnston said. A report conducted last year by the National Association of Attorneys General concluded that the main cause of youth violence is violence in the home.

Counseling sessions also help heal emotional wounds caused by physical violence.

“We work on getting rid of the subtle violence, the psychological and emotional violence just as much as the physical,” Diane Stuart, Utah state domestic violence coordinator, said. “The subtle violence underlies everything.”

Helping victims of abuse is a team effort. Members of this care team work with law enforcement officials and give court, emotional and financial assistance, Margie Harris, executive director of the Upper Valley Help for Family Violence in Rexburg, said

“What we are trying to do is make our jobs unnecessary in their lives,” Harris said.

The number of women who seek protection at the Rexburg shelter fluctuates, Harris said. However, in the past two months, someone has been at the shelter almost constantly. This tells Harris there is a problem with domestic violence in this area, she said.

Shelter officials and campus police are expecting an increase in domestic violence in the next couple of years. This expected increase is because of the school’s transition to a four-year university. More married students are expected to stay in Rexburg and married housing on campus should become more available.

Campus Police has not dealt much with domestic or dating abuse in the past, University police captain Garth Gunderson said. On average, they answer two or three calls a year. However, they have aided the city police in some calls that have dealt with married students, he said.

In preparation for the increased number of married students on campus, Gunderson sent three officers to Burly for a training session on domestic violence. All officers on the department are expected to receive some additional training.

“We hope we never have to use it, but we anticipate with more and more married students that there will be more problems,” Gunderson said. “Even good people have misunderstandings that can result in harsh words. I just hope that they never result in violence.”
