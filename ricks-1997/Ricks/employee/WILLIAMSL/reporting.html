18 RULES OF EFFECTIVE REPORTING:

1- FOLLOW THE MONEY.

Who profits? Why did this happen? County Courthouses. Bids. Bills. OPENSECRETS.ORG. COMMONCAUSE.ORG, SECRETARY OF STATE'S OFFICE IN IDAHO, SEC.GOV and the EDGAR DATABASE.

2- ALL STORIES MUST HAVE MORE THAN ONE SOURCE.

Always ask who is affected by a story. Always ask what the results of a story will be and pursue those issues. Many people affected by the story won't be at the meetings where these are discussed. you must learn to seek them out.

3. MOST GOOD STORIES COME FROM BEATS.

A beat is an organization or issue "area," you write about. It may be science or technology or sports or education or a campus department. Beats start with cultivating human sources from secretaries to administrators. This is the most important part of a beat. Sometimes you call and chew the fat. Other times, you call for information. You attend the meetings of the organization. You learn all organizations in the beat. You learn the process of the organization. You learn what kinds of documents are available. You remember, always, that you are the eyes and ears of the public. Also, good beat coverage includes scanning various publications and ideas. They would regularly scan a website. Go to Reporter.org for useful websites for reporters -- in addition to bro. williams' online sources.

4. MOST GOOD STORIES HAVE NUMBERS IN THEM.

Always make useful comparisons. Useful comparisons are not things like $1 million stretched end to end would go around the world 47 times . . . or whatever . . . say how much it might buy or how much money could be made off interest if it sat in a decent mutual fund -- how many homes could be built -- how many median salaries.

5. MOST GOOD STORIES FOCUS ON A PERSON.

At the heart of every story must be a person. How does what you report affect people. Learning to find people is the heart of every good story and report. This is also useful in public relations. Why did, UP&L, after all fly emily white home -- it was good P.R.

6. MOST GOOD STORIES ARE MORE COMPLEX THAN FIRST GLANCE.

One thing rarely is the only explainable cause of something else.

7. MOST GOOD STORIES HAVE "TO BE SURE" PARAGRAPHS.

Sometimes you will hit people or a program hard in reporting. You must always talk about the good of a program or idea or person -- usually before -- with the bad. This brings balance and expertise. Note how it is done in the Wall Street Journal.

8. MOST GOOD STORIES ARE ORIGINAL.
INSTEAD OF FOCUSING ON JUST THE STANDARD, focus on the how of a story. Rather than doing what everyone else is doing, ask what new things you can. Everything can be a story.

9. GOOD STORIES USE COMPUTERS TO ANALYZE DATA.

Databases and spreadsheets are quick and easy ways to analyze data and give you new stories.

10. MOST GOOD STORIES ARE MADE LOCAL FOR AN AUDIENCE.

If it is a national issue, how is it affecting people here at home? If it is a world issue, how are local people involved? At the very least, try to get quotes from local experts.

11. GOOD STORIES OFTEN COME FROM GRAZING.

Surf the net, read government documents, read periodicals and ask if this is something that matters here. Almost always unique things can -- and do -- happen.

12. GOOD STORIES COME FROM TICKLER FILES.

Keep track of the timeline of forthcoming events. Simply doing that will give natural stories and make it easy to do your job.

13. GOOD STORIES OFTEN COME FROM NOTHING MORE COMPLICATED THAN ACTUALLY READING THE BILLS.

Read government documents. Of a truth, your legislator often doesn't have the time. Your work will lead to numerous possibilities.

14. GOOD STORIES CAN BE FOUND IN SCIENTIFIC JOURNALS -

Read more than the standard journals like Science and don't be afraid to tackle the boring language, but with thousands of articles and hundreds of journals, you can either use them to write of the research on issues of interest to your community to interesting research by local people.

15. GOOD REPORTING ALWAYS STARTS WITH A DOCUMENTS STATE OF MIND.

Everything, especially in government, comes with data available and, if not always handy. Learning to review and to use that data -- such as government documents and court records and correspondence can help enhance a narrative and a factual basis for your reporting. Always ask yourself what sort of documents are available.

16. GOOD REPORTING ALWAYS STARTS WITH GOOD WRITING.

Look for the narrative detail. Remember to get to the heart of what a story is about. Write well.

17. GOOD REPORTING ALWAYS HAS GOOD INTERVIEWING.

Learn to ask polite questions. Ask how? Ask what happened next? Restate an answer. Ask what were you thinking? Be direct. Sometimes wait for an answer.

18. GOOD REPORTING IS ALWAYS ABOUT WHAT IS IMPORTANT TO YOUR READERS.

Remember, good reporting is unselfish. You must serve. You must teach. Good reporting is how adults learn without schools. It can affect their lives and help them care about our nation. What a great opportunity. Take full advantage of the trust given you. READERS MUST ALWAYS COME FIRST.
