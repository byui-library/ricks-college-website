ARISTOTLE 384-322 BC


I. By the 4th Century BC, the Greeks had moved away from representing the forces of nature as traditional deities.

A. Aristotle was the foremost natural philosopher in the ancient world.

B. For two thousand years the arguments and theories of the exact sciences, as well as of natural history, were a long commentary on his work.

II. Why did this begin in Greece, rather than Babylon and Egypt, where precise observations of the stars had been made much earlier than in Greece?

A. These older civilizations were more advanced technically than the Greeks.

III. According to Aristotle, the founder of natural philosophy was Thales (c.640-546 BC) of Meletus.

A. He is supposed to have held that all things come from water, and that earth floats on water.

IV. He had much greater interest in biological questions than any earlier thinker.

A. He felt that human biology could not be understood unless simpler creatures were investigated.

B. No one else before the 19th Century developed such a broad knowledge of marine biology.

C. He dissected marine animals; he talked to fisherman who he asked the character and behavior of squids, octopus, dog fish and fish.

Composition of Matter


V. He developed Empedocles's idea of the four roots or elements: Earth, Air, Fire and Water.

A. Unlike Empedocles, he maintained that the elements could be converted one into another.

B. He did not think of water, earth and air as chemically pure substances but more like solid, liquid and gaseous states.

C. He considered everything to be composed of (proto hyle) or primary matter.

1. All substances consisted of this primary matter impressed with form, which was the hidden cause of the properties of the substance.

2. In any transformation, the primary matter persisted unchanged but the form was altered.

D. Neither matter nor form could exist in isolation, and the simplest combinations of matter and form were the elements.

1. The elements could be analyzed in terms of four quantities: Hot, Moist, Cold and Dry.

2. Earth was dry and cold, water was cold and moist, air was moist and hot, and fire was hot and dry.



















VI. One element could, in principle, be converted into any other by the addition and removal of the appropriate qualities.

A. Every substance on earth was composed of combinations of the four elements.

1. Changes, which we now call chemical, were explained by alteration in the proportions of the four elements.

B. Aristotle made little attempt to produce evidence for his four-element theory.

1. Later supporters pointed out that when wood burns, fire issues from it, water oozes out of it, air is produced (in the form of smoke) and ashes (earth) remain behind.

2. It was evident that many other substances could not be analyzed this way, but in the absence of a better theory, Aristotle's explanation of the material world remained in vogue until at least the 16th Century.

C. His four-element theory was to exert a considerable influence on alchemy.

1. The alchemists were also influenced by Aristotle's ideas on the formation of metals.

2. He believed that metals and minerals were formed from exhalations.

3. One of the two exhalations were a moist vaporous one, which was formed when the sun's rays fell on water.

4. The other was a dry smoky one, which arose from the land.

5. When these exhalations became imprisoned in the earth, they formed minerals or metals, depending on which exhalation was present in greater quantity.

6. When the predominant exhalation was the dry smoky one, minerals were formed, and metals resulted when the moist vaporous exhalation was present in excess.

VII. Aristotle explained the motion of objects by saying each element goes to its proper place.

A. Earth falls downward as far as possible.

B. Water comes to rest above the earth.

C. Air rises above water.

D. And fire rises above air.

1. The four elements were confined to the region below the moon.

2. The fifth element was the material of the heavens, which had a circular motion.

VIII. Aristotle rejected the atomic theory of matter.

A. Democritos had maintained that atoms were in perpetual motion in a void.

1. Aristotle criticized both the concepts of atomic motion and of the void.

B. Aristotle accounted for motion in terms of the natural tendencies of bodies, and the perpetual random motion of atoms did not concur with his ideas.

C. Aristotle rejected the idea of the void partly as a result of erroneous beliefs concerning falling bodies.

1. He believed that the speed with which a body falls to earth is proportional to its weight.

2. He had no understanding of the acceleration of falling bodies.

3. From the observation that bodies fall more slowly in water than in air, he concluded that the speed of fall was inversely proportional to the thickness or resistance of the medium.

4. Since a vacuum would have a resistance of zero, the speed of fall of a body in it would be infinite.

(a) This is impossible, so he concluded a vacuum could not

exist.

IX. While Aristotle's dynamics contained incorrect conclusions based on superficial observations, much of his zoology was sound.

A. He believed the universe was spherical with the earth fixed at the center. The sun, moon, planets, and stars were held on clear crystal spheres surrounding the earth.

X. Aristotle's natural philosophy acquired an immense authority which remained almost unchallenged for nearly two thousand years. His Cosmological Theories became part of the doctrine of the Christian Church.

A. Until the Renaissance, it was common practice to attempt to fit all new facts into Aristotle's scheme of things.
