Interview to Win

Employers Share Their Toughest Questions, Their Pet Peeves, and Advice For Winning Strategies

If finding a job were a foot race, getting an interview places you at the starting line. To get to the finish line and win the job you have to be prepared. The best interview books offer a lot of the same, good advice:
  • Research the company.
  • Practice well thought-out answers to anticipated questions.
  • Dress appropriately.
  • Answer negative questions with positive points.
  • Ask intelligent questions that show you are interested in joining the organization.

What else might impress the gatekeeper at the company you want to join? CPC talked to six employers w-ho recruit on college campuses about interviews and what job seekers should and should not do. Some of the answers were textbook, but some were surprising--which just reinforces the idea that research will prepare you to win this race. Here's what our panel said:

It Takes More Than A Firm Handshake To Make A Good Impression

Burkholder: Good communications skills make a good first impression. Many questions students ask are inappropriate. They ask me about my career and other questions for the wrong reasons. I would ask questions about how a person could contribute more to an organization. Graduates haven't a clue. The business mandate of the decade is more, better, faster, with less, and they need to know that I am interested in their contributions, not in what they want. Students that have a good orientation do much better with us because they want to roll up their sleeves. Those who are inclined to talk about secretarial support are not of interest to us--we're interested in people who will go to copy something if it needs to be done.

I don't really care if they know a lot about my company. It's not bad for them to know what our gross sales are, but I don't give points for that. We spend a lot of time asking candidates what they want to do. They need to think about that before the interview. It is bad when they confuse us with someone else. I don't expect to hear that we're wonderful--I know that.

If there is anything they need to know to help them decide, it's okay to ask. Benefits, insurance, vacation. They should always ask the recruiter if there is anything else the recruiter needs. We want to get a picture of who they are. Don't ask me to dinner. Don't ask me to look at another person's resume.

If you really blow something in the interview, it's important to recognize it early and back up and ask to answer the question again. If you realize you've said something inappropriate or erroneous, I'd address it there rather than in a thank-you letter.

Bashing other companies is no good. We're looking for people who will contribute and grow with us. Make it easy to be recruited.

Durant: I like a solid handshake and a smile. I want to see genuine interest and enthusiasm. It makes good impression if they say, 'Good morning,' first. They don't have to wait for the interviewer to take the first step.

I expect them to know what Chubb's business is and what types of positions interest them. They don't have to be specific, but they should have a general idea of our training program. I also expect them to know the reputation of the company It is immediately apparent if they haven't done their homework about the company.

It's also important that a student know his or her career focus. So many students come in without any idea of where they might fit into the organization. If someone says that they would like to find an opportunity in operations, claims, or underwriting, I know that the candidate has done the research and has figured out, based on his/her skills, abilities, and interests what he/she wants to do.

They should never ask about salary--at least not during the first interview. If a company is interested in hiring a student, the salary will be discussed once a job offer is made to the student. It does not create a good impression if a student asks how much vacation they get. I wonder, "How much does this person want to work?" The first interview is a learning process for the interviewer and candidate and it should be a two-way dialogue.

It is all right [for the student] to ask for clarification if the question isn't understood. It's okay to ask for additional time to think about a question or ask to come back to a question at the end of the interview. It takes confidence to ask for that time.

If a student comes in unprepared for the interview, a second interview will not be granted. Because there is a lot of competition for positions, the student who is unprepared demonstrates that he/she isn't interested in working at Chubb. Since there are many applicants to choose from, I would rather interview another candidate than call back an unprepared student for a second interview.

Giannini: One of the first things I notice about someone is how they come at me. Do they extend their hand? Do they jump up? Are they too eager? Too scared? What's their handshake like? Are they two inches from my face? I want to see a person come at me with a middle of the road comfort level and lack of fear.

I can usually tell within five minutes--the light switch will be on or off--if the interview is going to go well the whole time. It's their poise, body language, attitude, and enthusiasm level. I don't care for arrogance or over confidence.

It's important that candidates know the basics about our company. Who we are, and a little bit of the industry we are in. They could talk to someone about us. I'm concerned with their homework process. It demonstrates initiative and analytical abilities when they ask questions.

Their resume is a good way to guide the interview. The candidate is saying, this is how T choose to present myself. If you let someone else assemble your [resume] thoughts, it's going to be hard for the interviewer and the job candidate to be in sync.

I don't want to hear comments or questions about my ethnic background. I don't want them to ask me how much I make. I want students to be honest. If they say, "I don't like the way this interview went," if I'm there another day, fill give them a second shot. If their resume looks good, but the interview did not go well, I'll write on their rejection a note that says, "I'd like to meet you in the spring" to give them a chance to practice their interview skills and try again.

My advice to a student would be to think about what you've done and what you want to do. Make a list of everything you've done in chronological order, then take your resume and set your list next to it. If there's a major difference, you've got a problem. And if they have no work experience, then I want to know what they've been doing for the last four years, and I want to know why.

A thank-you or follow-up letter is not a forum to criticize the interviewer, the process, or the company. If you didn't like the interview, let it go, and don't write a letter. If the interviewer asked something you find questionable, consult your placement office.

Mottor: I like someone who is at ease in speaking and saying hello. I understand butterflies and nervousness, but it's nice to see someone comfortable in a high stress situation. I like someone who can chat a bit, who has a good sense of humor, and who can relax and maybe make fun of the tenseness of the situation.

One thing people can do is mimic the interviewer. If the person you interview with sits up straight, then you sit up straight. If they lean on the table, you lean on the table. You mirror their body language and it puts them at ease. People would be surprised at how well this works.

Knowledge of the company can help put someone at ease, too. They can practice a bit, so that they're not on pins and needles. They shouldn't have canned answers, though. I want someone who is talking from their heart. If you're honest, it turns heads. Even if you're not as well qualified as another candidate, we have hiring managers who will go out on a limb and take a chance on people who are honest.

I like candidates to read everything they can get their hands on before the interview. I want them to read the newspaper, have the latest stock price, have read the annual report. Unfortunately, only 10 to 15 percent can talk intelligently about the company. Prior research isn't my deciding factor. If all things are equal between one unprepared candidate and another who is prepared, then it might be. But if someone seems like a strong candidate, I might have them in for a second interview.

I don't feel any question [asked by the candidate] is off base. Some employers don't want candidates to talk about salary, but I think that anything on someone's mind is fair game. We're pretty up front about what we are looking for and the salary range. But that's our company culture. We have an open door policy. If salary and vacation is their only question, then I'd be concerned. But if they've asked some really great questions, then by asking about salary, they might be showing me that they are considering their options. That's one reason it's important to do the research, learn about the company culture ahead of time. A job candidate can do that by talking to employees ahead of time, to see if people are open to talking to you about what it is like at our company.

If the interview isn't going well, but at the end the candidate takes the time to sell themselves, then I think they're really interested. If the last thing I hear makes me stop and think, "Should I give them a chance?" they'll get another look. Hardly anyone does it. People forget to sell themselves at the end of the interview. We send out a lot of information on the job and the company, and all a candidate has to do is take the information that we've given them and reiterate, paint a little picture that puts them in the job. It's important to wrap it up, have the last word.

Rizzello: We don't have a cookie-cutter mentality here. When I approach someone, I take the whole scene in--how they're sitting, the way they're dressed, the way their hair is combed. I don't think the candidate should break into rambling comments about what a great facility we have or what a great rug we have on the floor. So I think it's important that people think about how they are going to approach the interviewer rather than just jump in and try to break the ice inappropriately.

The people who impress me are the ones who ask about very recent events at the company. They've taken current events from a magazine or newspaper and thought about the impact on the company. I saw a summer intern recently who wanted to know the impact of a recent Merck merger and exactly how I saw that merger affect our marketing and sales force. It was the current thing at the time and it was an insightful question. It meant that the candidate had put some thought into what was going on in the company.

It's important that candidates know some of the basics and don't have to ask me. I don't think that should be part of the interview at all. They should be thinking about how they're going to fit in, and their questions should reflect their interest in how they are going to fit in.

I don't think there are any questions that we don't want to answer. If someone asks about salary and vacation, we answer. It's not a positive and not a negative.

The thing that will break an interview for me is poor communication skills, having a limited vocabulary. Not being thoughtful with questions. Thoughts that aren't clear and concise. Answers that ramble on. Answers that take off in a wrong direction as if they didn't think about the question before they answer.

Selaratia: Students should try to relax and be a confident in the interview. Students who have a bad attitude or who are abrasive, obnoxious, or cocky are less likely to project a positive, professional image. A student should project the image that they would be a team player, a good person to work with, and contribute to the organization's success.

It's important to know as much as possible about our industry, competition, sales, company structure, and products. And it's important to provide examples that support answers to questions. A student should demonstrate that they have the skills/knowledge to be successful in the company.

Students should never ask about compensation during the first interview.

Even if the interview doesn't go as well as the student would like, if they are highly qualified, they might be invited for a second interview.

Facts gather before interviewing:

  • Key people in the organization
  • Major products or services
  • Size in terms of sales and employees
  • Locations other than your community
  • Organization of the company
  • Major competitors
  • View of the company by clients, suppliers, and competition
  • Latest news reports on the company or on local or national news that affects the company


10 Steps to Showtime

  1. Arrive on time.
  2. Introduce yourself in a courteous manner.
  3. Read company materials while you wait.
  4. Have a firm handshake.
  5. Use body language to show interest.
  6. Listen.
  7. Smile, nod, give nonverbal feedback to the interviewer.
  8. Ask about the next step in the process.
  9. Thank the interviewer.
  10. Write a thank-you letter to anyone you have spoken to.


Tough Questions Interviewers Like to Ask

An interview isn't a game. It isn't a contest to see if the interviewer can stump the job candidate. However, interviewers can ask some thought provoking, tough questions. Here are some questions that our panel like to ask and suggestions for answering:

Tell me about a time when you had to make a decision, but didn't have all the information you needed.

Use a real anecdote from your experience to answer this question. The answer doesn't have to be great and grand. It could be a simple situation that was handled well. It could demonstrate ability in your field. For example: You may want to tell the interviewer how you selected your college from among all the colleges you applied to. Were you satisfied with your choice? Why or why not?

What suggestions do you have for our stores?

Don't answer this one with the expected answer, that is, anything to do with upkeep on the store. Instead, talk about merchandise that you might want to add to the store...or would you rearrange the store and why? Your answer should reflect your creativity.

What is the most significant contribution you made to the company during your internship/co-op?

Tell a story about an accomplishment that added value to the company, demonstrating skills that show initiative or resilience. Tell this interviewer about the options and the outcome of your work.

What are you bad at? What is the biggest mistake you've made?

Be honest. You'll show credibility and integrity. While mistakes aren't rewarded, those who make mistakes aren't shot either. Sometimes making a mistake can show that you've pushed yourself to the limit. Tell what you learned from the mistake.

Was there anything today that you were afraid I was going to ask you? Why did it make you uncomfortable?

The trick here is to keep your cool. Many people blurt out the question that they didn't want asked--and perhaps raise new questions in the interviewer's mind. Some mention an illegal question.

9 Ways NOT to Impress the Interviewer

A recent survey of 100 personnel directors by Robert Half International, a research form, showed a number of unprofessional behaviors that will guarantee you won't get the job. Here's what some personnel directors said job candidates did to ruin their chances:

"Dozed off and started snoring during the interview."

"Wore a Walkman and said she could listen to me and the music at the same time."

"Challenged the interviewer to arm wrestle."

"Said if he were hired, he would demonstrate his loyalty by having the corporate logo tattooed on his forearm."

"Interrupted to phone his therapist for advice on answering specific interview questions."

"Brought her large dog to the interview."

"Balding candidate abruptly excused himself, then, returned to office a few minutes later wearing a hairpiece."

"Chewed bubble gum and blew bubbles. "

"Stretched out on the floor to fill out job application."

Half, president of the research firm, says the most important thing you should do during a job interview is the easiest: Be nice. "An interview is a very tense situation," he says. "Try to think of the word, smile. You'll have a pleasant face, but you won't show a mouthful of Chicklets."

10 Tips For a Positive Interview

  1. Use the interviewer's name--title and last name--from time to time as you speak. Don't use the interviewer's first name unless you have been requested to do so.
  2. Phrase your questions so that you sound sure of yourself. "What would be my duties?" sounds more assertive than "What are the duties of the job?"
  3. Use good grammar and good diction. Say "yes," not "yeah."
  4. Listen to how quickly you speak and look for moderation. Don't talk too fast. Don't pepper the hiring manager with too many facts at once.
  5. Don't fill pauses with "um," "uh," or "ah." Don't punctuate sentences with "you know," '`like," "see," or"okay."
  6. Punctuate your speech just as you would a sentence. Stress the words that are most important. Don't arbitrarily emphasize every third word; don't keep your voice a monotone.
  7. Use active verbs.
  8. Don't use the word "think," "guess," or "feel," which don't sound indecisive; sound positive. Also, avoid "pretty good" or "fairly well." Talk about your skills with positive words.
  9. Watch the tone of your voice. While it might be trendy among your friends to end a sentence with a higher tone of voice, so that sentences sound like questions, this habit will kill your credibility with hiring managers.
  10. Offer examples of your accomplishments. Use illustrations, descriptions, statistics, and testimonials to support your claims.

Illegal Questions. . .

What's the Right Answer

VARIOUS federal, state, and local laws regulate the questions a prospective employer can ask you. An employer's questions--on the job application, in the interview, or during the testing process--must be related to the job that you are applying for. For the employer, the focus must be "What do I need to know to decide whether or not this person can perform the functions of this job?"

Options for answering

If you are asked an illegal question, you have three options.

1. You are free to answer the question--but if you choose to do so, realize that you are giving information that is not job related. And, you could harm your candidacy by giving the "wrong" answer.

2. You can refuse to answer the question. By selecting this option, you'll be within your rights, but you're also running the risk of coming off as uncooperative or confrontational-- hardly the words an employer would use to describe the "ideal" candidate.

3. Your third option is to examine the intent behind the question and respond with an answer as it might apply to the job. For instance, the interviewer asks, "Are you a U.S. citizen?" or "What country are you from?'' You've been asked an illegal question. You could say, however, "I am authorized to work in the United States." Or, if your interviewer asks, "Who is going to take care of your children when you have to travel?" You might answer, "I can meet the travel and work schedule that this job requires."
