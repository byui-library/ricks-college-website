Make your Experience Work for You

A Guide to Explaining the Value of Cooperative Education, Internships, Summer Jobs, and Volunteer Experience

Picture two students--Candidate A and Candidate B--about to graduate and eager to move on to the world of work. Each holds the same degree. Each has a good grade point average--Candidate A has a 3.5 GPA; Candidate B has a 3.0 GPA. Candidate A has been active on campus and served as the president of the professional club on campus. Candidate B hasn't been quite as active on campus, but has held internships in the field each summer during college.

Both have an eye on an entry level spot at the Widget Company. Both interview with the Widget recruiter and each is invited to the Widget plant for a second interview. Everyone at Widget is impressed with Candidate A and Candidate B.

Who gets the job?

Most students looking for jobs already know the answer--the candidate with internship experience, Candidate B, wins the job. These days employers are looking for work-related experience, especially cooperative education and internship experience, and many will overlook the not quite-perfect GPA if they find a candidate with this kind of practical training.

However, suppose the situation isn't quite so clear-cut?

Candidate A and Candidate B want a management training job at the Woofer Company. Candidate A has a 3.0 GPA and was involved in campus activities, including organizing a food drive for the local community cupboard. Summers, Candidate A worked as a temporary office worker in a variety of situations. Candidate B has a 3.0 GPA and three years of summer internships in the field to offer.

Who gets the job?

This time the answer is a little tougher. Because of the internships, Candidate B is still the top contender for the job. However, even though Candidate A's jobs weren't "in the field," this job seeker could be the Woofer Company's first choice.

Why?

Because Candidate A has valuable experience, too. Winning the job will depend on Candidate A's ability to put the experience working as a volunteer and a temporary office worker into words that show the employer the skills Candidate learned.

A student who can put skills learned at a summer job into perspective, so that those skills meet the potential employer's needs, wins. Properly framed, a positive employment record or record of volunteer service can give you a big advantage over students who haven't tested the world of work. Those who have ventured into the workplace can count on a big advantage. According to a 1992 College Placement Council survey, 64 percent of responding employers listed work/co-op experience as an important pre-screening criteria in the selection of new hires.

Let's take a look at some of the skills Candidate A will mention on the resume and again at the interview:

Volunteer job: Organized and managed a group of students to collect canned goods for a local food cupboard. Contacted local authorities and negotiated a local event that would bring in food. Planned and scheduled the event. Designed and distributed advertising for the event. Delegated jobs to Volunteers. Troubleshot and facilitated problems on the day of the event. Talked with the media. Scheduled delivery of canned goods to cupboard. Talked to community leaders after the event to plan for the following year.

Summer job: Worked with the latest word-processing, spreadsheet, and data base software. Talked with customers and solved customer service problems. Researched and investigated case histories. Devised and organized a new filing system.

What skills is Candidate A offering the Woofer Company? Details orientation. Management ability. Communications skills. Problem solving skills. Organizational skills. Technology skills.

Did Candidate A get the job? You bet.

Can you work the same magic with your co-op, internships, summer, or part-time experience?

Sure. Here are some tips from the experts on how to turn your job into resume and interview material:

Write everything down. Keeping a weekly journal is an im portent first step, says Curt Eley assistant dean/director of cooperative education at Southern Methodist University. Take notes on what you do and what you learn to do. Be sure to jot down the names of the people you work with--they may become invaluable network contacts or references when you job hunt. When it comes time to work on your resume or prepare for an interview, pull out your journal and review you accomplishments.

Talk with someone about what you've learned. William Taylor, director of cooperative education at the University of Alabama in Tuscaloosa, says "Students do post-work interviews with us and we talk about what they have gained from their work terms," he said.

"The reason employers are hiring from their co-op graduates is they have a proven commodity. They know what the person can do. In traditional co-op, a student returns to the same employer every other quarter, so the supervisor knows if the student is doing a good job. In an internship, it is a one-shot deal with the employer."

While the co-op may offer the Cadillac of work-related experience, all work experiences have the potential for technical, human relations, and decision-making skill building.

To determine the skills you've learned, answer these questions as you review, suggests Donna Vinton, assistant director for career services at the University of Northern Iowa.

1. What did you learn about (people, managing, this business, yourself, etc.) from this job?

2.. How would you describe the ideal candidate for a position like you held? Note the skills and qualities necessary.

3. What do you know now that you wish you had known before you started the job?

4. If you were to receive recognition for some aspect of your performance on this job, what would the award be for?

Offer to show your project--this is a strategy often used by co-op and internship student. "Students can say, 'Here are the projects I've completed; here are the supervisors I worked with,"' said Taylor. "Students can offer a documented track record with good references of skills, strengths, and weaknesses. The employer sees that this wasn't just 'make work.' They weren't just sitting in front of the copy machine the whole time.

Provide the employer with a copy of your evaluations another method routinely used by co-op students. The former employer can provide information on your work skills and how well you progressed in your job. This will show the employer that you are a "tried and true" employee.

Demonstrating your projects and providing a copy of evaluations also shows the potential employer that you know better what you want to do, that you are more likely to be satisfied with your job because you've tested the field and discovered what you do and don't like.

Learn to talk about your skills in the way they can benefit your potential employer, recommends Rick Kincaid, career services at SUNY Brockport. Even the most ordinary jobs have the ability to tell an employer about your potential. For instance, suppose you spent time as a waiter or waitress. Instead of saying you seated patrons and served food, describe your duties in context to what the employer needs. A waiter/ waitress might say "managed tip pool for eight servers and distributed all tips at the end of each shift." This shows that you were honest and trustworthy. How about a warehouse worker who is thinking of joining a business? Saying "loaded trucks with furniture" doesn't tell the employer that you are detail-oriented or accurate. You can show those qualities, however if you say, "reviewed furniture orders, loaded in proper order for correct delivery truck, responsible for accurate delivery of $70,000 worth of merchandise.''

Experts say that career-related experience is the best experience to have when you are seeking a job. But if your resume is filled with other types of work, learning to present the skills that you have acquired will get you a step closer to the job you want.
