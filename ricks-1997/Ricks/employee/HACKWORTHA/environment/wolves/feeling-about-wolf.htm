Unraveling Feelings about Wolves
by Allen Hackworth

Mythic wolf stories may fill our imaginations with excitement, joy, terror, anger, or indifference. We remember the big, bad wolf that tricked Red Riding Hood. We remember the hungry wolf that was always anxious to huff and puff and eat little pigs. Perhaps our nursery rhymes taught us to fear wolves. Yet in spite of old fears which have been entrenched in our culture for centuries, the wolf’s popularity has been growing. And so have its packs.

In 1995 and 1996, listed under the Endangered Species Act, 66 wolves were reintroduced into the West, some in Yellowstone National Park, some in Central Idaho. Since the introduction, the fertile beasts have reproduced and now number around 500. Some people are pleased with the growth; others feel despair, anger, and regret. Our various feelings stem from complicated factors which differ with each individual.

One factor which alters our feelings about wolves relates to knowledge. Some people, knowing little about wolves, base their conclusions on fairytales and folklore. Consequently they may make false assumptions based on inaccurate information. But faulty, shallow assumptions can be a problem for both the wolf advocates and the wolf critics. Also the facts may not help us because of deeply held convictions and fears. Our deep-core convictions and fears cause us to be selective in the facts we choose to believe and defend.

Our deep-core convictions relate to our feelings about wolves in another way too. How do we feel about the environment and ecosystem? Do we favor natural checks and balances relative to predation? Relating to the land and animals, do we see men and women as stewards and controllers? Do we work with nature or strive to control it? Do we look at life in utilitarian terms asking what is most practical? Or are we starry-eyed idealists?

As we discuss the answers to these questions, and as we discover our deep-core values, we find one thing we already know: we don’t all think alike. The utilitarian may see little value in wolves. The idealist may want a perfectly balanced ecosystem where men are observers rather than controllers.

Another factor shaping our attitude towards wolves is economic. Many people who have nothing to lose financially say, "Let’s have the wolves. Then, once a year, when I go to the mountains, I can hear them howl." But for the rancher who daily experiences real or imagined financial setbacks, the wolf has no place on the range. Additionally, for the non-hunter, the day hiker, the urban dweller, the loss of game means nothing because the grocery store always has plenty of meat. But for outfitters and hunters, a loss of game cripples the quality of one’s life. Wolves cut down on the availability of game because a mature wolf eats approximately 12 deer or elk per year.

One’s proximity to wolves also affects our feelings. Generally, urban dwellers see the growth and spread of wolf packs as a good thing. But the nearer one lives to the borders of the wolves’ territory, the more likely one will look with disfavor on the growth of the pack.

Also, related to proximity is one’s actual experience with predators. The rural dwellers get their experiences with wolves from the Discovery Channel, the zoo, or a college lecture. Ranchers get their experiences by looking at the dead calves which have been killed. Urban dwellers feel their experiences are based on first-hand knowledge of predator behavior. They believe city dwellers make their decisions based on uniformed sentimentality. But the city dwellers accuse urban dwellers of insensitivity towards wolves and an irreverence for the ecosystem.

A last factor relates to how one feels about the government. On forest and wildlife issues, city dwellers may feel a sense of gain because they believe the forests and animals are being preserved for themselves and for their posterity. Yet, those living on the borders of the forests may feel their lives are being controlled unfairly. They may feel the heavy hand of government forcing its agenda, and they may resent the arrogance of the environmentalist who advocate policies which alter the quality of life on the rancher’s lands. Government practices cause some men and women to experience the fear of losing control over one’s life.

In spite of the facts which we may argue with lucid clarity or ignore with smug disregard, our covert motivations relate to our self interests. Yet no matter what our feelings for wolves, we should all soon answer a key question: how many wolves are enough? If we don’t agree soon on the answer, hostilities and conflicts will only increase. More wolves will die; more feelings will be hurt; more money will be wasted. And these conditions will hurt us all. Therefore, let the urban dwellers and city dwellers strive for understanding, for a spirit of compromise, and for a unity which makes us friends.
