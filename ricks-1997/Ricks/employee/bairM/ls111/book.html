Arrangement of Library Material

Organization is needed to manage large collections of information. In elementary school we first learn to organize our homework by subject. We grouped our assignments  in binders and notebooks so that we can find the completed homework. As we have access to more and more information, we may start to group it in files on a computer disc. In the same way the organization of libraries and Internet have certain systems of arrangement.
 
The David O. McKay Library uses four arrangement systems:
 Accession  Alphabetical Library of Congress  Superintendent of Documents
Computer programs have menus, indexes, and search options to help us navigate to the information that we need. Internet has search engines and directories to help organize information for easier access.

Alphabetical arrangement

The McKay Library use alphabetical arrangement in two collections:
    • paperback collection is arranged by author
    • periodicals is alphabetical by title
Many library tools provide access to information through alphabetical order. Dictionaries, encyclopedias, indexes, and many other tools use alphabetical order. Examples of rules used in alphabetizing are found in the list of current periodicals and lists at the end of periodical aisles here.
Alphabetical arrangement requires some filing rules. Rules tell how numbers and abbreviations are treated. Each tool may have its own interpretation of alphabetical filing rules. Some tools alphabetize letter by letter without regard to spaces that separate words. Computers often put all numbers at the first. For example, 101 Dalmatians may be first on the list and on shelf in a video store. Some tools translate abbreviations; putting  Dr. Seuss near Doctor Dolittle. We often get used to ignoring initial "The," "A", and "An" and their foreign equivalents only to discover that a tool did not ignore them and we were unable to locate a title because we forgot the initial articles. Tools that are not consistent with the treatment of these articles can be confusing. If you know a foreign language and think that initial articles are ignored, you might do the same with "La," "Le," and "Les." Cutter numbers are used by some arrangement systems to simplify the complexity of alphabetical rules.

Library of Congress

    As was seen with Cataloguing in Publication or CIP, the Library of Congress assigns several numbers to each book that it receives to copyright and to catalog.
Reminder from Library Materials Unit: "Library of Congress classification numbers (PZ7.S2744Gr 1993) start with letters. Dewey decimal classification numbers starting with numbers are often given. Books for young children may have the letter "E" instead of a Dewey decimal number to indicate that it is an " easy" book. "DC" and a number will tell which Dewey decimal classification edition was used."
    The classification number assigns each book a discipline, field of study, or subject. For example, call numbers M to P make up the Humanities section. P is assigned to Literature. "PZ 7" is assigned to children's literature fiction. As discussed in the next unit on catalogs, public libraries and schools may use another system of arrangement, the Dewey Decimal system.
    Academic libraries started to more away from the Dewey Decimal system as inventions and discoveries made this older system less useful. Ricks College has used Library of Congress classification or subject numbers since 1967. While I was a student the religion books were catalogued using LC. One day I would find a book for my religion class in the 220's and the next in BS or BX. The following table shows single letters assigned to disciplines and fields of study.

Superintendent of Documents

    The Library of Congress and Superintendent of Documents are two separate government agencies which use two separated arrangement systems. Other government libraries may use other systems. The National Library of Medicine and ERIC are two examples of libraries using other systems.
    The arrangement system is called SuDoc and is explained in the GPO Classification Manual (GDF GP 3.29:P88/yr). Basically the Superintendent of Document system or SuDoc arranges materials by the issuing agency. Each department, bureau, and agency is assigned a letter or letter number combination. Cutter numbers alphabetize agencies by a combination of letters and numbers. More will be said about government documents in a separate unit.
    For examples of rules for filing see:
list of call numbers on an aisle
labels on microfiche drawers
List of Classes Available for Selection... (GDF GP 3.24:)

    In summary, the arrangement rules are similar to computerized arrangement:
start at the left
dots, slashes, colons, and dashes just mean next number combination
numbers are whole numbers, not decimal
same stem or starting combination are kept together

Summary and Application

    Shelving order

  • A call number is used to label items for filing or arrangement.
  • The call number starts with an abbreviation for a collection.
  • Match the call number to locations in the library.
  • Match the call number to shelving areas.
  • Match the call number to the spine or label.
Spines and labels may not have all collection abbreviation. (FL1 and FL2 do not appear on the spine.)
In addition FL1 books may also have a label PORTER which was a separate group years ago.

    Trouble shooting questions

  1. Is the material misfiled? CHECK NEARBY
  2. Could the numbers or letters be misread? CHECK POSSIBLE CHOICES
Could an 'S' look like a '5?'
Could a '2' look like a 'Z?'
Could an '8' look like a '3'?
  • Did you copy the information correctly?
CHECK the shelves nearby see if they are for related subjects. If not, LOOK up call number again.
  • Did you include all the collection abbreviation?
Copies may be found in different locations. LOOK at copy record on catalog.
  • Are you looking for the right type of material?
Government documents often come as microfiche, computer files, and paper.
LOOK at description on catalog record.
  • How many copies are listed as checked in?
A copy on reserve may be available. LOOK at copy record on catalog.
A copy may be due soon. "Make request" is an option that allows you to request that a copy be held at Circulation when it is returned.
  • Does someone who works near the collection know where it might be? ASK
It may be in shelving units between users.
Someone may be using the book in the library, if it is popular.
Someone may have stolen or misplaced it. Circulation can find out when it was last used.

Internet

    Many people believe that a computer has more power than traditional tools to help them find information. Computers can locate information quickly. However, the user needs to evaluate the information more carefully than they would books found in a library.
    To help Internet users categorized lists or Web Directories have been developed. Many people have their own favorite directory. Search engines systematically search the Internet but will not include sites that require passwords. Meta searches combine several search engines in the query and create an index of the results. Several Internet sites rate, rank and review Internet sites. Gateways, Portals and Locators link to certain resources to provide speed and reliability for many topics. These gateways or locator services are intended to increase efficiency for the organizations, universities, libraries, and government agencies which host them. Internet resources has several gateways listed under government documents and medical topics.
