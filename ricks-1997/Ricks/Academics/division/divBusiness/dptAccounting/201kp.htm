Course Description & Objectives:
A. Description - This rigorous course provides a basic foundation for other courses in accounting as well as in business and information processing. The course is generally accepted by most colleges and universities through transfer credit. The course will consist of lectures and discussions relative to text assignments and problems, emphasizing basic accounting theory and practices. Homework will be reviewed in class so that students may check their work and questions can be discussed and answered.

B. Objectives:
1. Develop an understanding of and ability to work with accounting principles relative to the theory of debit and credit; the worksheet; financial statements; payroll; journalizing; posting; special journals and ledgers; negotiable instruments; adjusting, closing and reversing procedures; assets valuation; and corporate accounting.
2. Develop an understanding of the accounting cycle and to analyze transactions and apply them to the cycle and to compile financial statements and interpret from the contents.
3. Provide the foundations for advanced work in accounting and business related subjects. (Accounting is the language of business.)
4. Develop a working vocabulary of accounting terminology.
5. Gain exposure to the ethical responsibility of accountants and to emphasize writing skills for accountants.
6. Gain exposure to accounting/business sites on the Internet and to develop interactive skills by working in groups.

Homework: Homework is essential to developing a working understanding of accounting. Be prepared to spend many hours in solving homework problems. Time and effort are just as important as the correct solution. Accounting is something that usually has to be experienced to gain the most from it, although some memorization is essential, too. One cannot learn to play the piano by merely reading and memorizing from a book. Accounting works the same way. For most chapters, students should expect to spend a minimum of 4 to 6 hours working on their homework assignment. Homework is due at the end of class on the last day spent on each chapter, unless otherwise directed by your instructor. Homework is to be done in pencil, with any subsequent corrections made in class to be done in ink. This allows the instructor to see the efforts made out of class and to evaluate student progress. Student errors will automatically be highlighted in ink for your preparation for exams. Also, initial solutions should be attempted in pencil as erasures are common in accounting. An assignment sheet will be given for Monopoly at a later time. For the Study Guide assignment, students will read "A Study Guide For Accounting Students" handout and will type a one-half page summary of something they learned, or of something they think will help them from that handout.

Exams: Four regular exams will be given as well as a final exam. The exams consist of multiple choice questions and work problems. Both subjective and objective content is provided on the exams to test student memory, understanding, and ability to analyze and apply concepts covered in class. The final is 1/3 comprehensive to test student retention of previously covered topics. Students should read each question carefully and should attempt to answer all problems on the exam. With the exception of the final, all exams will be administered at the testing center. Although no time limit penalties will be assessed, students should be aware that speed is important too, not just accuracy. (The CPA exam is a timed exam.) Therefore, the student should learn to pace him/her self throughout the exam. Each student is responsible for taking the exams at the testing center during the allotted time. Be sure you allow yourself sufficient time to complete each one.

Group Activities:
Accountants and other business people are expected to regularly work with a variety of individuals in groups or in teams. Five group activities will be assigned during the course by the instructor. Students will work on these activities in groups of three or four people. The groups will be established by the instructor at the beginning of the semester. It is expected that all group members will actively participate together with their group. Each group activity is worth 5 points. Most of these activities will involve the use of the Internet. If your group activity is done and turned in on time, it will earn the full 5 points. Late group activities will be accepted, but will be assessed a penalty equal to that of late homework.

Ethics Write-ups: One of the biggest complaints against recent accounting and business graduates is their inability to express themselves in writing. Accountants are frequently called upon to express opinions and findings in an accurate, clear, thorough, and concise manner. Because accountants subject themselves to a code of professional conduct, students should be exposed to ethics in the accounting curriculum. Students will be expected to write three papers discussing ethics or accounting ethics cases provided by the instructor. Each write-up should be one to one-and-a-half pages in length. They should be typed with proper attention given to grammar, spelling, organization, and clarity of expression. They will also be graded on the soundness and thoroughness of the content. These are formal papers and should be treated as such. Here is a chance for students to interrelate their English course skills with ethics in the accounting area. Each write-up is worth 10 points. Late papers will be assessed a penalty equal to that of late homework, so make sure yours are turned in on time.

Mortimer Adler said, "Why is marking up a book indispensable to reading? First, it keeps you awake. (And I don't mean merely conscious; I mean wide awake.) In the second place reading, if it is active, is thinking, and thinking tends to express itself

in words, spoken or written. The marked book is usually the thought-through book. Finally, writing helps you remember the thoughts you had, or the thoughts the author expressed." He further stated, "You buy a steak..., but you do not own the steak in the most important sense until you consume it and get it into your bloodstream. I am arguing that books, too, must be absorbed in your bloodstream to do you any good."
