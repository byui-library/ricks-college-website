Principles of Good Writing
 
J.V. Coat is a memory trick to help you remember the key words.
 
J = Jargon
V = Voice and Vigor
C = Clear, Concise, Concrete, Creative, Correct
O = Organized
A = Audience
T = Tone
 
1.  Avoid jargon. Jargon includes words that are peculiar to a particular group or organization.  When writing, use words, expressions, and phrases that can be understood by an intelligent but uninformed reader.
2.  Prefer the active to the passive voice.  For example, "The cherry tree was chopped down by George Washington" (passive voice) is not as effective as "George chopped down the cherry tree."  (active voice).  With active voice the verb takes a direct object.
3.  Gain vigor in your writing through variety.  Vary the word order of your sentences, as well as your sentence lengths and types.
4.  Be clear.  Use familiar words and word combinations.  A speech writer for President Roosevelt wrote, "We are endeavoring to construct a more inclusive society."  FDR changed this text to "We're going to make a country in which no one is left out."  President Roosevelt's sentence is better.
5.  Be concrete. Use concrete rather than abstract language.  (This concept is discussed later.) Give sound evidence. Cite authorities if you wish and don't use illogical arguments.
6.  Be concise. Shorter is better; however, brevity should not be gained at the expense of clarity and completeness.   Essay length should be like some women's skirts, long enough to cover the subject but short enough to be interesting.
7.  Be correct. Mistakes are offensive.
8.  Be creative. Avoid stale, over-used, trite phrases (cliches).  Use your creative powers to say something in a new way.  Be original.
9.  Be organized and stick to the point. This means plan and outline. Organize on the basis of your main points. To think about what and in what order you are going to write something does not detract from good writing.  It actually helps produce good writing.  Most novice writers resist outlining, but you must not.  For longer papers it is useful to write your points on 3 x 5 cards.  You can then arrange your cards (points) in the most logical, effective order. Early in your paper, give a clear, definite statement of what you intend to prove or discuss.
10.  Know your audience. It is possible to write down to your reader, treating him or her like a twelve year old.  It is also possible to write over your reader's head.  Just consider, "How much does my reader know about the subject?"   Aim to communicate effectively with your reader, not to prove that you are smarter than he or she.
11.  Follow an appropriate tone.  Be certain but not overbearing or conceited.  Don't slant too much.  Don't exaggerate.  Avoid loaded words.
