Appendix A / The Essay Assignments

Essay #1 / A Synthesis
Due October 8--at the beginning of class

"Facts are stupid things until brought into connection with some general law."
Louis Agassiz as quoted by Samuel Scudder

No scientific theory is a collection of facts. . . . Science finds order and meaning in our experience."
Jacob Bronowski

"Data, data everywhere, but not a thought to think"
Theodore Roszak

"The student who merely cows, robs himself, without knowing it, of his education and his soul. The student who only bulls robs himself, as he knows full well, of the joys of inductive discovery."
William G. Perry, Jr.

When you synthesize, you discover the general law that makes sense of "stupid" facts (Agassiz). You find order and meaning among data and information that may appear at first to be only randomly ordered (Bronowski). You discover the thought buried in the sterile heap of data (Roszak). You infer the proper relationship between "cowing and bulling" (Perry). However a writer may describe this synthesis process, you are making an inference–that is, you are exploring evidence, data, facts, examples, illustrations, statistics, and information to discover the ideas, conclusions, laws, hypotheses, theories, and theses that can reasonably be derived from them. As you well know from experience and our class reading, this is not easy.
Whenever you need to write a paper that demonstrates you are capable of making your own inferences–thinking your own thoughts–a strategy like the one described below may help you.

1. Read in the assigned area or an area of personal interest.
For your paper, I would like you to read assigned essays from Thinking about Thinking. These essays are your data bank. Plan on using ideas and information from at least three Thinking essays.

2. As you read, identify principal and sub-issues.
Every non-fiction text—essay, article, editorial, chapter, book—addresses questions. The main question the text addresses is the writer’s principal issue. (The answer to this question, by the way, is the writer’s thesis.) As you read each assigned essay, look for this principal issue. Also look for the writer’s sub-issues, other interesting questions the text implies. Keep a list of these questions as you move from essay to essay. (Try to pull at least five questions from each essay.)

3. From your list of questions, select a question you find intriguing.
This question will become the principal issue of your paper. Make certain you care about this question even if you don’t yet know how to answer it.

  

4. Take your issue into your source texts.
With your question in mind, ask how each source text addresses this question. As you re-read, mark passages that illuminate your question. You may want to keep track of these connections on note cards. Perhaps not every text addresses every question, but as gems sparkle differently in various lights, so texts yield different meanings when seen from different perspectives. Your source essays, in a similar way, yield different ideas in relationship to different questions. They provide information to help you answer your question: examples, facts, data, definitions, comparisons, anecdotes, evidence, contrasting views.

5. Answer your issue question.
After considering the information your texts yield, answer your issue question in one sentence. (What idea does the evidence of your texts imply? What inference can you reasonably make?) The answer to your issue question is your paper’s thesis.

6. Map or sketch a plan for your essay.
Draw a picture of the essay you intend to write. As models, use some of the mapping strategies we have discussed in class.

7. Draft your essay.
Using your map as a guide, write through your ideas to see what you have. (Your paper should be five pages, double spaced.) Your supporting material–"the cow"–is what makes your idea–"the bull"–interesting, memorable, and persuasive. If you need more evidence, expand your research and reading.

8. Macro-revise your essay.
Most often a first draft is rough. Completely rework your initial draft. Re-order your ideas, add information where you find holes, delete information that takes you away from your thesis, connect your paragraphs with transitions, re-work your introduction to capture your reader’s attention, make certain your final paragraph packs a punch.

Step 9. Try your paper out on an audience.
Read your paper aloud to peers. Encourage their critical analysis of your ideas.

Step 10. Revise and edit your paper.
Based on responses from your readers, make final those revisions you think will make your presentation more effective. Make certain your essay is focused, coherent, and developed. Re-work your sentences for precision and variety. Edit your punctuation, mechanics, spelling carefully. Submit your final draft.

Essay #2 / A Critique
Due November 19

Your critique essays is essentially an analysis of someone else’s argument. Since we often encounter opinions and arguments about which we are uncertain, the purpose of our second unit is to learn how to analyze such arguments so that we can find good reasons to agree or to disagree.

Begin looking immediately for a "target argument" you would enjoy analyzing. As you search, please keep these two criteria in mind: Look for an argument of interest to you and about which you could honestly change your mind. (Avoid the workhorse topics like abortion, gun control, capital punishment–ugh! I’ll share some topics your classmates have explored that are far more interesting than these–topics like Which pickup truck is better, Chevy or Ford? Or should old black and white movies be colorzied?!)

After you find your "target argument," I am going to show you how to analyze it against ten critical reading questions–each derived from Asking the Right Questions. Your written response to each question will comprise your critique paper. (Please include these questions in your final paper as headings for each section.)

1. What is the argument?
(To answer this question, please present a precis summary of the "target argument" you plan to critique. Appendix C will show you how to write a precis–precis, by the way, is the French word for precise. Chapters 2 and 3 from Asking the Right Questions also provide information that will help you write an effective summary.

2. Is the argument ambiguous?

3. What value conflicts and assumptions are present in the argument?

4. What descriptive assumptions are present in the argument?

  

5. Does the argument illustrate any fallacies of logic?

6. Is the evidence persuasive? (If statistics are present, are they deceptive?)

7. Are there rival causes for the conclusion?

8. Is the argument lacking any significant information?

9. What other reasonable conclusions are possible?

10. Is the argument acceptable?

These additional guidelines may help you better understand the assignment:

1. As you begin each of the ten sections of your critique essay, answer the question at hand in the first sentence of your response. Let that answer provide you with a "topic sentence" or "mini-thesis" focus for that section of your critique.

2. With this focusing sentence in mind, explain your answer using ample evidence from the "target argument." This evidence will show your reader how you arrived at your inference.

3. Remember that your critique is an assessment–a judgment about the strengths and weaknesses of your "target argument"; therefore pronounce your judgments clearly and confidently, but remember to back up each of your opinions with evidence and discussion.

4. A critique is a visible track of your thinking. Work hard to connect your points so your reader can clearly see how you come to your conclusions.

5. Your critique paper will grow naturally as you respond to each question. Don’t worry about the overall length; rather, concern yourself with answering each question thoroughly.

Essay #3 / An Argument
Due December 10

For your final paper I will ask you to write a formal argument–5 to 6 pages. As you think about researching a topic about which to write, please keep these suggestions in mind:

1. Research an issue you are genuinely interested in. Please avoid the workhorse topics–gun control, abortion, capital punishment, you know the ones I mean.
2. Research an issue about which you don’t know the answer. Discover your answer as you read and explore.
3. If you’d like, consider the same issue your critique essay illustrated.
4. Begin your search for materials related to your issue early in the semester. You’ll need to have your sources in hand by November 19th.
5. You’ll need at least five sources for your paper–library sources, interviews, internet information, and so forth.
6. Once you’ve collected your information, take your issue question into your source materials. Given this information, what is the best answer to the issue at question. That answer will become the thesis/conclusion of your argument paper. Now that you have discovered your thesis, organize your argument according to this format–a format based on the rhetorical structures advocated by ancient Greeks and Romans.

Part One / The Background
The background section of an argument considers this question: What information should I share with my readers that will help them better understand the argument I’m about to present? For example, you may want to include an historical overview of the problem you’re addressing, definitions of key terms you’ll be using in your argument, information that demonstrates your topic is serious and worthy of your readers’ consideration.

Part Two / The Opposing View
As you have researched, you have come to believe that your answer to the issue at question is a better position than the one some others may advocate. In fairness to your opposition, however, you need to clarify what their views are. In this section you consider the question What is the argument of those with whom I disagree? Here you will want to carefully and objectively summarize their argument–their conclusion and at least one or two reasons for their view.

  

Part Three / The Critique
In the critique section of a formal argument, you consider the question: What are the strengths and weaknesses of my opponent’s position?
Now that you have summarized your opponent’s view, explain what you are willing to concede (and why) and what you are unable to accept (and why). Here you can apply to your opponent’s argument the most relevant critique questions you learned about in Asking the Right Questions. Obviously, you will not need to consider all of the critique questions; rather, you will want to apply those most useful as you explain the potential weaknesses of this opposing view.

Part Four / Your Thesis
At last it’s your day in court, and you’re ready to answer this question: What is my conclusion? State your thesis and your reasons for it. For emphasis, isolate your thesis as a separate paragraph, even it’s short.

Part Five / Your Argument
Now consider Why do I believe my conclusion is better than my opponents? Lay out each reason for your conclusion and with each one, share ample evidence to defend your view. Remember the testimonies of experts, research studies, and other kinds of evidence that give you credibility as a careful thinker.

Part Six / Your Concluding Paragraph
Finally consider the question: How can I climax my argument? You may wish to suggest a solution to a problem you have identified, challenge your reader to action, or remind your readers how they will benefit if they adopt your view.

Part Seven / Your Sources
Attach an annotated Works Cited and Precis Summary of your essay. You’re done!
