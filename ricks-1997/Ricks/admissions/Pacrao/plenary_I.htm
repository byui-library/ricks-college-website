Dr. Paul G. Risser, Opening Session Keynote Address

When Michelle Sandelin asked me if I would speak here, I said, AI would be glad.  I've never heard of PACRAO, but with an acronym like that it must be a good organization.  And so I called my younger brother who is the head registrar at Rogue Community College in the southern part of Oregon, and I said, "Ted, tell me about PACRAO."  And he said, "Well you should do it.  It is a really fun group; all they do is have lots of fun.  And when I got here, I discovered the gavel is a glass.

As Bob Bontrager indicated, I am one of those meddling presidents, but I do so because Bob is such a wonderful colleague and really is important, but not just to the university, but to me as well.  I also want to acknowledge the fact that introducing the head table is not quite as easy as you think it is, so it is not a matter of always remembering right or left hand.  I could recall one of the most embarrassing moments of my life.  I was the president of Miami Ohio before I came to Oregon State and their commencement is a very large commencement and there are about four rows of people sitting on the head table.  The podium was way out in front of the people on the end table and so it was impossible to read names and turn around to see who you were introducing.  So one day I was doing this commencement, and I was going along reading the names.  Unfortunately, the student body president's name was omitted and they all stood up when they were introduced, and here sat this poor student body president sitting there and never getting introduced, and so I've always been embarrassed about that event, so you, Saundra, did a really good job.

I had a chance to look at your program; in fact, I looked at it pretty carefully.  What I found was a discussion on FERPA, lots of topics on technology and using the web for admissions and for enrollment.  I saw discussions on diversity, student services, student retention, and all those topics that you find of importance and in fact, are important.  But what I want to do this evening is suggest there is even more that you can do -- that you have an even bigger role to play than the topics in your program.  I am going to talk about this in terms of opportunities that you have which are now available to you and quite special.  Frost Johnson, who I think all of you know who is part of the organizing committee, he and I were together in New Mexico, and New Mexico is the "Land of Opportunity."  I want to talk this evening for a few minutes about what I think is a "Land of Opportunity" for admissions officers and registrars.  You are saying to yourself, "oh no, here we go, another talk about all the change that is going on."  And you are thinking to yourself, "we're already changing" or you are thinking to yourself, "somebody else can handle that."  Or, you are thinking to yourself, "it really isn't as much change as they say it is," or perhaps “This is a fad and I don't need to worry about this."  Well, all that may be true, I just want to describe in four areas why I think what you do is so important and why you have such a large opportunity.  We'll talk about it in terms of organizations and how they change and how they flatten.  I'm going to talk about marketing and the role you can play in marketing, which is perhaps what is different from what you are doing for the moment.  I'm going to talk about more participation in higher education, and lastly, I'm going to talk about the student culture and how it is changing on campus.  I think all these topics are equally applicable whether we are talking about a community college, four-year college, public institution or private institutions.

This week I had the pleasure of being in New Jersey where I talked with the graduate board and the executive vice president of a company called Lewis Dreyfuss, and this particular company is a company which does international commodities marketing.  They sell around the world, livestock commodities and petroleum commodities . . . and the conversation was interesting because I was actually thinking about tonight and we struck up this conversation, and what he said was his business was changing so rapidly because of the past, his business has collected information on markets on prices, put all those in one place and then he can go to seller or buyer and offer those services with all that information and tell them when to sell and when to buy, what the future might look like.  Today, all that information, the very same information he has is available on the kitchen table on the computer for every family.  So the information itself is no longer of value.  The value is the fact how we can use that information to synthesize it in ways that the person sitting at the kitchen table could use it more rapidly.  To do it in a way that is packaged to be helpful for the decision maker in making the decision.

I think that particular example is exactly the same example that we are seeing in organizations, in fact, in universities today.  With the electronic ways that we share information, organizations have been squashed flat, that is everybody has available the same amount of information.  It is absolutely incredible what has happened.  Think about the web and look at the penetration of that technology.  The numbers the web has achieved is a 25% market penetration in only seven years.  Seven years ago we didn’t talk about this, we did have the web.  But in seven years, 25% market penetration.  That has been compared to personal computers and personal computers did not achieve that market penetration until 15 years after they were introduced.  It took the telephone 35 years to reach that same level of penetration.  The web had an incredible and very rapid penetration into our organizations.  If you realize all college students in this country, 92% have access to a computer somewhere in their college.  In fact, over half, 65%, own a computer themselves.  This is such an important change in terms of how information is available.

Furthermore, it is not just available information, but there are lots of tools of how you can go on the web and find different statistical techniques, you can do modeling and look at projections, and that is all public access software.  It is all right there.

Now, what are the implications to those of us in this room?  The future is not just to think about web based applications and enrollment procedures, but to look across the institution at the kind of information you can provide to the rest of your institution, that is, at a minimum, you ought to know who applies to your college, who comes, who stays, who leaves, who graduates.  Put yourself in the place of a university president.  Can you imagine, any more important data than those?  I can't.  One of your responsibilities, in my opinion, is to think about the data that you have from students who comes, who doesn’t who graduated, how long did they stay, all that information just like the international commodities market I told you about.  Put it in a way that presidents can understand exactly what is happening at your institution.

So I think one of the great opportunities you have is to insert yourself right square in the middle of the strategic planning of your university, as you have the key data to make that change.

Now that is the easy part, because the next step, in my opinion, is how do you then use that flow of information to enrich your job opportunities.  So in the future, what you need to do is to mind your web site. Know what every department is doing.  Know what special programs are out there.  If the department has a wonderful success, you need to capture that because we talk to prospective students.  You can share this wonderful information that you just got off the web.  So the web not only becomes an opportunity for you to share key information and make strategic decisions for the university, but also becomes the key source of information for you to help the next generation for what you do in terms of admissions.

So far I've just talked about data or numbers that can be analyzed, for you to share or use.  But in organizations, there is a wonderful commodity called tacit knowledge.  This is information that isn't written.  It is information you know because you talked to a dean, a departmental chair, or a faculty member or a staff member who knows something really neat about one of your students or about the organization.  One of the opportunities you have is to try and capture that.  Maybe electronically, maybe not, capture that and use that in your responsibilities.

And so, organizations now have become flattened, and for me provide wonderful opportunities for this group -- to guide the university, to take a university and share it with prospective students and current students.

Let me talk about marketing for just a minute.  It is very interesting to me that the people in this room are the most important marketers that universities have.  Think about what you do.  You talk to prospective students, you talk to their families, and how perceptive are these students and families?  Incredibility perceptive.  They are the ones who have all their senses out there attuned, trying to decide where they want to go to college or where they want to their son or daughter to go.  You already have an audience which is already attuned to what you are going to say, and in many cases, you are the absolute first person to talk to an individual about your institution, or in fact, about higher education and its importance.  Every one of us has the responsibility to talk about, not only our own institutions, but in fact the value of higher education.  You have a chance to do it in the most receptive of all possibilities.  You are also the first ones to begin to fill that bridge of loyalty of that student and your institution.  So what you say is incredibly important, not just to your institution, but to those students and their families.  You have an enormous responsibility.  You'll be surprised how many times I talk to students; they refer to something Michelle has said, that is because you are the first voice of higher education to these students and their families.  So when you think about marketing, it is not just how you position the institution for the market, but the role you play in marketing higher education.

To me, that raises some very complicated issues for you as it does for me.  There are two ways to think about marketing.  One of them is to think about marketing in a straightforward sense, that is, you are trying to market an institution.  You have a series of goods and services that you want to provide, in this case, to a buyer.  So you craft your marketing scheme in such a way that you can help the buyer find it attractive. That is the straightforward marketing approach.  It seems to me we in higher education have a higher level of responsibility when it comes to marketing.  It is part of our marketing that can't be strictly in the buyer and seller approach.  We have to recognize that there are some societal obligations as well.  Societal marketing hardly recognizes not just goods and services, and buyers and sellers; it also recognizes that we have some higher order responsibility which is good for society.  When we are in fact selling, if it turns out that for this particular student, this isn't the right institution; I think we have the responsibility to recognize it.  Furthermore, I think we have the responsibility to recognize that if higher education is not providing the right kinds of opportunities for our students, then we have the responsibility to help our institutions to understand that. 

So I guess what I am asking you in terms of marketing is that you not think about just buyers and sellers in a straight, sort of Fifth Avenue approach, but to recognize that we have the responsibility to those students to try and do what is best for them and we need to make sure our institutions are as responsive as they can be.

Now what is happening in higher education in terms of marketing is not quite that simple either.  We are all seeing an enormous change in the emphasis in learning from a system that used to be largely 18 to 25 years old traditional students, and yet more than half of our students today are more than 25.  I am not going to spend the next few minutes on how one might work differently with that older group, but what it says is, the term "traditional" is pretty hard to defend any more when half our students are not traditional in that sense.  What it means to us, that we have not only a broader clientele, but also a clientele, which is different in some interesting ways.  First of all, the traditional, if I may use that term, of students 18-22 or 25 grew up in a very different kind of world, and it is pretty hard to convince a student that it is an exciting classroom with a chalkboard or an overhead transparency -- this generation grew up with a joy stick in each hand.  I think as we go through thinking about our institutions, we have to recognize this.  It is also important to think about the consequence of having the 25 years or older as part of our community, and that is, these are individuals who may not be quite so tolerant in standing in line to do a process in front of the Registrar's window.  They may not be quite so comfortable in going through multiple forms, and remember, these are the parents of the traditional students.

So, as we think about marketing, I think it is important to recognize that as we have all said, the traditional students are changing in lots of different ways.  Not only is the population of students expanding, but also the older students have specific requirements and expectations, and furthermore, they are frequently with families.  Who can make that story better than you?  Who spends more time talking to either students on your campus or prospective students?  It seems to me that you have a wonderful opportunity to incorporate those ideas and those thoughts in your institution.

The third area has to do with increased participants in the whole area of higher education.  In some ways, this is maybe the most useful to me because it is relatively new, and because it crosses the boundaries in the sense between societal values and marketing values.  If we look at the sort of education and training market across the world, in an economic sense, it is a two trillion dollar business -- two trillion dollars for a global education and training program in post-secondary education.  You all know the numbers, but the big gap between those who have a college education and those who don't, has widened since 1988.  It used to be a 50% gap, and now by the year 2000, it is expected to be 111% difference between those who have college degrees and those who don't.  A different way to put this is: A 32-year-old male with a high school diploma earns two-thirds of what he earned 25 years ago.  So it is not a matter of not increasing, it is losing earning ability.  And yet in spite of what I just said, only 21% of American adults ages 18-25 now have a bachelor's degree.  Now I'm leading to a different point from what you are expecting.  You are expecting that I am going to talk about the value of post-secondary education.  I'm going to make a different point, and the point is, five years ago, virtually none of the Fortune 500 Companies had a knowledge officer, that is someone who looked after education and training.  Five years ago, essentially none of the Fortune 500 companies had a person like that.  Today, 55% of those companies have someone at the very top level looking after education.

Motorola calculates that for every dollar kids spend on education and training translates into an increase of $30 in productivity in three years.  That kind of calculus says that education and training is important in an economic sense, and yet our whole system is going a different way from tradition.  If you look across this country today, there are now over 1200 charter schools in 27 states.  There are now 1.2 million students at the secondary level and primary level being home schooled.  There are 5 million students in private schools.

Here is the summary of what I am saying.  If you look at the gross domestic product, GDP, that right now, education is 10% of the GDP but it is .2% of the US capital numbers.  What is the point in that?  There was a book published in April called the Knowledge Book put out by Merrell-Lynch that is full of the kind of information I just gave you.  The argument is that there is an enormous economic market in post-secondary education.  We will see more and more of the private sector beginning to offer both pre-secondary and post-secondary education just on a purely economic basis.  There is this pull between persons who see the importance of education and the sort of push from industry who wants their employees to be successful.  So both of them are now crying for more education. The private sector sees it as a way for them to basically make money.  So what we will see in the future is a much greater penetration in terms of a private sector in education.  Now what does that mean to us as admissions officers?  It means two of things: 1) the kinds of markets in which we are dealing will be very different and that instead of competing among ourselves in most cases, we will compete with the private sector.  Talk about standing in line, they won't have you stand in line.  Talk about complicated procedures, it won't happen.  So it is going to change rapidly, the environment in which we have registration and other activities. 

I think that is an important point for us to look out in the horizon and see what is coming.  I think that one of the topics that should be in next year's conference, is to think about that broader environment which we are conducting our activities.

Lastly, lets talk a minute about the student experience.  I think we all recognize that at some kind of abstract level colleges and universities are changing, that we used to have sort of a focus on degree programs and if you took this degree, you take these courses, fulfill the requirements of a chemistry degree or an economics degree, in the end the Registrar signs the form and you have the degree.  But now that is changing.  In fact, what students want is not just to fulfill a degree, but they need to have a set of skills.  About 25% of the jobs we have today didn’t exist 15 years ago.  That means we need to give students a set of skills as well as knowledge, and that is a different kind of process. 

The second point is in the past we had kind of a site-based education, and now we have education for any person, any place, and any time.  We recognize in the past we have had a very standardized curriculum and in the future, the students will want to have specialized curriculum.  If we look at this calendar that we all have either quarters or semesters and we march through that, but I can tell you that the private sector is not going to be held to semesters or quarters . . . they'll put courses in just the right sized modules to meet the needs of the students.  We talked about four year degrees or two-year degrees, when in fact there is nothing sacred about that.

Let me just stop right there and say, "what does that mean to each of us?"  It means a lot because when we begin now to talk about our admissions policies, and we talk about our recruiting activities, that changes in range of activities.  When we talk about Registrar activities, how easy it would be if your president came and said to you, AI want to teach courses here, not on a quarter or semester, I want them in one month modules.  I want to be able to divide those even into quarters and I want to track every student, and I want to give a student a chance to make a decision after every month.

I'm not being hypothetical here.  What I want to do is finish reiterating what I said at the beginning, you in this room have opportunities to mold a university by sharing information, to make decisions, and also by listening carefully to your university so you can meet your opportunities effectively.  If you have a chance now to participate in marketing in ways you've never had in the past, because the marketing plans are changing so rapidly, and you have the best pulse on that change.  To recognize, thirdly, that in the future, higher education is going to be changing, we'll have different partners, different sets of resources, and different kinds of opportunities.

Lastly, the change we've been talking about for five or ten years now, is really beginning to happen, and you, perhaps are the most key persons to make those cultural changes within your institution, that is, if you can go to your institution and say, "I can manage courses of any length of time at any place, and do it conveniently" think of the power that would mean to your institution.  As you can tell from my comments, I have a great admiration for what you do, and I think you have enormous opportunities.

Thank you.

President Springfield recognized and introduced William R. Haid, AACRAO President

Thank you very much, Saundra (Springfield), and hello PACRAO.  It is nice to be here, and as Saundra told you, this is where I started.  I wouldn’t have thought about it if I had not had a chance to visit PACRAO this year as your AACRAO representative, but my first institution was Arizona State University.  I was fortunate that my boss thought enough of professional development to bring me to a PACRAO meeting.  I think that meeting was in Newport, but that was my first professional experience with a regional association and then we went on to AACRAO.

It is nice to be back, and I am enjoying seeing all my friends, colleagues and acquaintances and it really is fun to be part of a larger family in our world of education and in our profession.  In that family, it never seems to end because tonight, as we are sitting at the head table and listen to Dr. Risser talk about his message, of course, I'm sure unless you remember too, Dr. Risser, but I was at New Mexico also when you were there, and it is amazing that we see how our colleagues have progressed as well.  I remember so clearly Dr. Risser was vice president in New Mexico and I had a few opportunities to observe him in action, and I'll just never forget one session that he had before the faculty assembly.  It is not an easy group to go to, I'm sure you know . . . and I forget what the issue was, but he was so capable of seeing and presenting, he certainly represented the administration to the faculty at that point in time, but he took his coat off, rolled up his sleeve and just was himself.  I thought, this is a man who is going someplace.

PACRAO is an important association to AACRAO, and that is why I asked if it would be okay if I came and visited during your annual meeting.  I wanted to bring you a message from AACRAO, and I want to let you know you are important to AACRAO and not just because AACRAO is coming to your region next year and that is an important reason, but because you are leaders in AACRAO.  You do represent an important membership to the national association and I wanted to encourage your continued participation at AACRAO because that is what makes AACRAO great.

I am here tonight to bring you greetings from the Board of Directors and staff in Washington, and we are at these meetings because we feel it is a priority to be here and to be part of your meetings and to listen and have the opportunity to find out what is important for you and to be able to have interaction.  I want to do an update on what is going on in AACRAO.  There is a lot going on, if you have been following our Data Dispenser: and electronic news as to what we have been doing for you.  I promise this is not going to be a second keynote speech.  They've already told me that I need to remember that mine is a greeting to give to you.  We are adding a section to the program where I will be providing a real in depth AACRAO update to those who are interested and want to get some of the inside story as to what we are doing, how our finances are doing, and some of the work that has been going on.  But just to let everyone know what is going on, I want to talk a little bit about the activity in our governance process, what we are doing in Federal Relations, member services, technology, upcoming meetings, and communications.  You are probably aware that in the governance arena, we have been working hard to incorporate AACRAO.  We realize that AACRAO was an unincorporated, non-profit association for 90 years and in that kind of status, our association assets are very much at risk as are the members who serve as volunteers, both on the Board of Directors, committees, and task forces.  Incorporation is the right thing to do and we have taken the steps necessary for AACRAO to become an incorporated non-profit member association.  In doing so, we have filed for incorporation in the District of Columbia; we are now an incorporated association.  We have been working on bringing our bylaws up to date so we are in compliance with all the requirements for an incorporated association.  We have a task force that met and worked hard in September, three of the committee members came from PACRAO and represented you very well, and those were Janet Ward, and Ken Servis and Beverly Lewis.  I very much appreciated their participation.

We have a new set of bylaws we are looking at right now and the board will be looking to adopt those bylaws in December at their meeting.  They are available for your review now on our AACRAO web page as well as the articles of incorporation we used.  We really do want you to take time to read these bylaws and look at them and if you have any comments, as we want to know what your thoughts are, especially if you are interested, please come to the extra forum that we will have after tomorrow's afternoon session.  We'll talk about it in some detail.  What we will do is in December the board will take action on it and will begin to publish and promote the new bylaws for AACRAO that are not really new, they are just improved.  All the things we care about for AACRAO have been brought forward in a new set of documents that will be our governance documents, and we want to have discussion and feedback on this process and on those documents, leading up to the annual meeting at which time we will ask the membership to approve those bylaws and they will become our official documents.  But, they are available now for you to look at on the AACRAO web site, and I do encourage you to look at them.

Most importantly, we think, what we are doing for you, is to provide you with information on what is going on in the federal government because so much legislation is taking place in Washington today that impacts us at our institutions, and we don't have to go very far down the list before all of you will nod your head, but we are trying to get federal relations updates out weekly to you via our e-mail broadcast.  We have been doing that since July.  We are trying to tell you what is going on with legislative mandates in education, appropriations and budgets, trying to let you know what is going on in the Department of Education with their activity, their proposed rules, and their negotiated rule making.  We will try to keep you up to date on hot topics such as affirmative action and retention rates and reporting requirements, and a nice feature, our staff has been tracking what presidential candidates are saying on education, so you've got a nice little summary plate you can watch and track those presidential hopefuls and what they are saying on education.  We've also established a link to congressional connections on our web page.  This is a new service and I hope you'll take a look at it.  It is a way for you to make direct contact with what you are interested in and a direct line of communication to your member of congress.  You can do that right through the AACRAO web page. 

One of the pieces that seem to be on all of our plates is the voter registration deadline.  You'll have to do certain activities to make sure we are in compliance.  You can look through the AACRAO web page or information on voter registration legislation and get deadlines for your state and to get contact information for your state board of elections.  We are trying to improve our communications.  The "Data Dispenser" is now a document you can't read in one sitting, and it's really quite a useful document now.  We've got ten issues  that are planned for this year.  The last one was sent out electronically.  The next one will be paper; we've heard you like paper and you also like the timeliness of getting it electronically so there will be a combination of that.  We are doing member surveys that are using the web site to track your responses, a new way to get feedback from you about what you think is important on issues and on characteristics of your institutions and on the services we will be providing.  We said we wanted to find out what you thought was important and that is how we are doing that.

In terms of our meetings and workshops that are coming up, we are really excited.  Later this week we'll have the ninth annual Strategic Enrollment Management conference in Orlando and that is now the premier conference on enrollment management. We are sold out for our meeting in Orlando, and we are very pleased about that.

The EDI conference is being held in Indianapolis also later this week.  We are continuing our FERPA workshops, one is scheduled in Florida in February, and we'll keep you apprized of the locations for those. And of course, the one we are most excited about is our annual meeting in New Orleans April 9-12 at the New Orleans Hilton.  The hotel rooms will go very fast if you are thinking of going, you should be making room reservations now even though we haven't started registration for the conference.  Now is the time to be making room reservation plans.  It will be a conference to remember.  The LAC is working very hard to make New Orleans 2000 the meeting you'll never forget in terms of program, entertainment, support, everything they are planning to do is first class, first rate and you can believe that from the folks down there.

New publications are coming out in international education and we have a new one coming out called "Navigating Change in the New Millennium, Enrollment and Leadership Strategies" by Jim Black.  That is available now.  Look for a new look from College and University.  We are now back to four issues a year.  We have cut back during the time we were trying to restore financial stability to the association.  We made real good progress there and College and University is now back to four issues.   We are also using our web page to get information out to you more frequently and more completely.  I want to end this by telling you that AACRAO is coming to your neighborhood in 2001 and we are very excited about that.  It is a great city, Seattle, for that meeting, we've got a wonderful chair, co-chair and team put together.  

I really do want to interact with you and find out what is on your mind and what your questions are about AACRAO and how we can serve you better because we want our association to be meaningful to you.  So please, talk to me, let me know what is on your mind, and I look forward to seeing you again during this conference.

Thank you very much.
