BYU-IDAHO IS COMMITTED TO PRESERVING THE 'SPIRIT OF RICKS'

Finding ways to preserve and enhance the same feelings and friendliness that people have always loved about Ricks College weighs heavily on the minds of administrators, faculty and student leaders as they "rethink education" at BYU-Idaho. Often called the "Spirit of Ricks," BYU-Idaho leaders are determined to retain the phrase that describes a feeling that has been experienced by many people down through the years.

As the university expands and changes, those things that have bonded students, faculty and administration for more than 100 years at Ricks College will be retained, says BYU-Idaho President David A. Bednar. These experiences will continue to come from a tradition of service, hard work, friendliness and compassion. "There is an overarching spiritual dimension to the 'Spirit of Ricks'," he says. "The 'Spirit of Ricks' comes from an environment where students are living in harmony with God's laws."

The president adds, "The 'Spirit of Ricks' is not found in a building. It is not found in a place. It is found in the people, the students, the teachers. The 'Spirit of Ricks' will not be diminished--in fact, if we do this transition properly, it will be greatly enhanced. We will continue to foster a nurturing, spiritual environment, which will continue to be referred to as the 'Spirit of Ricks.' "

Part of the "Spirit of Ricks" comes from the friendliness experienced on campus among students, faculty members, employees and others. "We want to continue to foster an environment where people are concerned and caring toward each other," President Bednar says.

Ric Page, assistant vice president of Student Life, believes that deliberately preserving the phrase "Spirit of Ricks" says a lot about the university's commitment. "This is a way we will honor the legacy that Ricks College has left for BYU-Idaho."

NEW FACULTY EXPLAIN WHY THEY CAME TO BYU-IDAHO

An unprecedented 37 new faculty members have been hired this year to help gear up for changes at BYU-Idaho. Twenty-five positions were added as part of an estimated 100 faculty members who will be hired during the next four years as the new university begins offering programs leading to bachelor's degrees, while 12 came through normal attrition due mainly to retirements.

We randomly selected three of these new faculty members to ask why they came to BYU-Idaho. Here are their responses:

Marion Johnstun was born and raised in St. Helens, Ore., a city about the size of Rexburg between Portland and Mt. St. Helens. He attended Ricks College as a young man and met his future wife here. After college, he returned to teach elementary education in his hometown of St. Helens.

But Marion says that for about the past 10 years he had felt it was time to do something else with his life. He earned master's and doctorate degrees while continuing to teach third grade. He inquired about teaching at Ricks College, only to be told no one would be retiring from the Elementary Education Department for several years.

When the announcement was made that Ricks would start offering bachelor's degrees in elementary education, everything changed and jobs opened up. He says his son was accepted to BYU-Idaho before he was - he will be a freshman here this fall. Marion's wife, Marie, is also excited about coming back to the Upper Snake River Valley since she was raised in nearby Lewisville. Marion will teach physical education methods for elementary teachers and educational psychology.

Bill Holman is coming to BYU-Idaho from BYU in Provo where he has been teaching in the Music Department for four years. "It was the right decision at the time," he says. "You can't script your life."

He is excited about "getting in on the ground floor of a new four-year university." Bill will teach classes in clarinet along with courses such as "Music Theory" and "Music and the Humanities." His wife, Scarlett, will teach music curriculum on a part-time basis in the Elementary Education Department.

The Holmans joined the Church in 1975 while living in Bogota, Colombia, where he was a member of the National Symphony Colombia. Raised in Iowa and Florida, he taught in public schools in Florida for 18 years before going to BYU.

It's like coming home for Beth Hendricks, a Rexburg native who taught on a part-time basis at Ricks College for several years before transferring to Eastern Idaho Technical College in Idaho Falls to teach marketing and advertising on a full-time basis. The Rexburg native will teach public relations and advertising in the Communications Department.

"I think the changes are great," she says of the change to offer bachelor's degrees. "There will be so many opportunities for the students." As a young woman, she attended Ricks College and says she had a bad experience when it came to transferring to BYU and having to repeat about a semester's worth of work. "Students can stay here for four years and will be so much better off," she says.

AMERICAN FOLK DANCERS AND BLUEGRASS BAND WIN PRIZE AT POLISH FESTIVAL

American pioneer dances and music may have lifted the spirits of weary travelers as they moved westward but they also won the hearts of the judges and audiences as the Ricks College American Folk Dancers and Bluegrass Band were awarded the prize for best traditional dance in the 38th Annual Beskidy Highlander's Week of Culture in Poland held July 28 - August 5.

The Beskidy Highlander Week is often referred to as the largest folklore gathering in Europe. Participating this year were 70 Polish folk ensembles, 26 performing foreign folk groups from 21 countries and four continents. About 3,600 group participants performed 42 concerts.

According to festival director Anna Stachowaska the council was impressed with the rising upward tendency of artistic level of participating groups. Stachowaska said the American team made a good impression on the audience with their "presentation of settlers' culture from West America." She said, "They met with the best response of audience. There were many encores of [the] American group for demands of our spectators from performances on every stage and good echoes of American performances in our mass media."

The American Folk Dancers and Bluegrass Band consists of 28 dancers and 6 musicians under the direction of Charles West. Russell Bice is traveling with the group as tour coordinator. The musicians were prepared by Eilene Wilcox. The team goes prepared to be ambassadors of goodwill as representatives of the school, the Church and the USA.
