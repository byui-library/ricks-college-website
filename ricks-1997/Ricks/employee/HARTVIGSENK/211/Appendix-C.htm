Appendix C

The Precise Summary

 

1. Main Point?

In the first sentence of your precis summary identify these five bits of information:

 For example . . .

In his essay "Drinking Hemlock and Other Nutritional Matters," Harold J. Morowitz, a molecular biophysicist at Yale, argues that students should be taught the art of inquiry at all levels of the curriculum.

 1) the writer’s full name
2) the writer’s credential
3) the title of the text If the text is an article, place the title within quotation marks.
If the text is a book, place the title in italics or underline it.
4) the writer’s purpose (abbreviated) Use verbs like these to capture the writer’s purpose: asserts, argues, explains, claims, etc.
5) the writer’s conclusion (sometimes called the thesis, main point, or main idea) Begin this part of your sentence with a that cause to make certain you capture the writer’s complete main point.
2. Reasons?

Summarize briefly the writer’s reasons supporting the conclusion.

For example . . .

He supports his argument by showing how most everyone comes to believe ideas that are unfounded, even silly, because they have never thought about where those ideas come from or how they were established. In reality, well-founded information is arrived at through painstaking research.

 This part of the precis may require several sentences depending upon the complexity of the text your are summarizing.

Be careful to connect ideas.

 time: eventually, finally, next, first, second, subsequently
addition: also, besides, in addition, moreover, too
contrast: nevertheless, however, even though, but, on the other hand, still
result:: therefore, hence, as a result consequently
example: for instance, for example, to illustrate, namely
emphasis: indeed, in fact, certainly
summary: in short, in conclusion
3. Purpose?

Write a statement that elaborates the writer’s purpose; include an "in order to" phrase.

 For example . . .

Morowitz writes in order to convince his reader that students need to understand how valid knowledge is established to avoid the risk of being fooled by quacks and hucksters.

General Guidelines for Summary Writing

1. A good summary is brief. Summaries achieve their brevity by eliminating details, examples, facts, and by reporting the "sum" of what the supporting material adds up to.
2. A good summary is objective. Report your author’s ideas in third person (he, she, they, and nouns like the writer, the researcher, etc.– any noun naming a person is third person).
3. A good summary is mostly paraphrase. Think of yourself as a translator: you are translating the author’s language into your own. Keep direct quotations brief and always place them within quotation marks.
4. A good summary is written in the present tense. Write as if the idea is unfolding for your readers as they read, unless you are recounting a story that is truly historically past. Past tense is then acceptable.
5. A good summary is coherent and clear. Too often summaries sound like lists of ideas. Use transitional expressions like those listed above to clarify the logical relationship among the ideas you are reporting.

4. Audience?

Describe the audience the writer intends to address.

 For example . . .
As a researcher who knows well the difficulty of determining valid knowledge, Morowitz writes to anyone who confronts television, classroom, or cocktail party "drivel," and needs to ask, "How do you know what you are saying is indeed a statement of fact?"
