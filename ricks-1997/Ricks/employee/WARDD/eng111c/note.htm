Note of Expectation

Consider these statements on Intellectual Courage:

        Intellectual courage is the power and determination to follow loyally and reasonably one's own beliefs and principles, irrespective not only of the disapproval of neighbors [friends, family, or enemies], but also of painful disturbance in one's own mind.Intellectual courage is undoubtedly the most difficult and, therefore, the rarest of human achievements. Few will seek a new intellectual position. Fewer will maintain it even if reached by honest effort because to do so will usually require the additional courage to reconstitute one's old ideas in the light of new knowledge.

                                     (Alice Gardner, Encyclopedia of Religion and Ethics, ed. James Hastings, Vol. III-IV, 206)

        I want you to do a lot of thinking about intellectual courage, because it is the basis upon which I will teach all the principles and perspectives introduced to you during this course. Indeed, intellectual courage is the only basis upon which genuine education can be enjoyed. It is also the only basis upon which honest communication may be shared. This course is dedicated to both, for as the title of the course implies, educated people write to learn. In doing so they learn what they really know and what they need to know in order to be confident in themselves. Having learned how they were limited in their past "understanding," they then work to share what they've discovered with others--others who they suppose are limited as they were, before they learned what they discovered. This is the basis of meaningful writing in college and in life. It is the focus of this course. It is education in the highest sense of the word. I am committed to it; I hope you will become committed to it yourselves.

What You Can Expect from this Course

        As you know this course is a writing course--a computer writing course at that. In one way or another, you all have had writing experience before: from learning the alphabet in elementary school through struggling to write good, or at least, passable papers in Jr. high and high school. In these classes you have been taught what writing is, and most of you haven't questioned what you've been taught--though you've questioned why in the #@* you've had to learn how to write those essays--let alone do all those #@* grammar assignments. Many of you will confess that you're not very good writers, that you don't even like to write. Some of you will secretly admit that you're not bad; that, frankly, you enjoy the experience of writing. In short, you all come to this class with varying experience, expectations, and understanding as to what "writing" is.

        Regardless of what you've learned, I can promise you that this class will be different from what you've experienced before. Rather than studying how to write essays for an English class, we're going to study how to write ideas for ourselves and others--and do so with one criterion as our central focus. This criterion may be stated in the form of question:

What principles of thinking and rethinking, writing and rewriting, viewing and reviewing, feeling and re-feeling enable educated writers to re-present on the page, not only their ideas, but the significance of their ideas?

This question gets at a fundamental truth--a truth, I might add, that many students are quite ignorant of: the truth being that it is possible to learn how to enjoy more and more insight in our lives through studying and practicing fundamental principles of rewriting and rethinking.

        Sad, but true, these principles are often neglected in traditional writing courses. What is taught instead are rules and regulations of how to write conventional essays, using conventional writing styles grounded in proper grammar. You know what I'm talking about, don't you? If you're like me, you probably learned that your grammar must be good; that the mechanics of your sentences must not be flawed; that your punctuation must be correct; that your essay must have a thesis sentence at the conclusion of the first paragraph; that each of your body-paragraphs must begin with a topic sentence; that your topic sentences must be supported by concrete detail--usually examples derived from what your topic sentences imply; that your introduction should "grab" your reader; and that your conclusion should summarize what you've said in the paper. Most students relate to what I've just mentioned in the same way. They nod in recognition of these elements while they close their eyes to sleep just like they did all the way through their primary and secondary writing instruction.

        Now don't misunderstand. These elements have a place in good writing. But they are best learned by studying principles by which the significance of personal insight may be represented effectively on the page. In time you will come to understand and appreciate these principles--if you care to. As you do, you will understand for yourselves why a course in writing must be devoted to the highest standards of intellectual courage, and why the students of that course must commit themselves to those standards if they are to write to learn and learn how to share the significance of their writing with others.

Class Attendance:

        You should consider what we cover in class as the only instruction you will receive to be able to succeed in this class. As you will soon find out, there is no text for you to read to be able to learn the principles and perspectives we will cover during the course. In fact your notes will be the only means of instruction to draw upon when you are writing or otherwise studying for the next class. For this reason missing any class will greatly decrease your chances of doing well in this course.

        In addition to physical attendance, decide now to participate actively in class or group discussion. Do so to learn both for yourself and to contribute to someone else's learning. The questions you raise or don't raise; the confusions you voice or don't voice; the frustrations or anxieties you share or don't share--all these will effect your own and your classmates' understanding of the material. You cannot decide to be "neutral" in this course. For whatever you do will effect us all for good or for ill. Therefore, decide right now that this class will be compellingly interesting, because of your commitment to attend each class prepared and willing to contribute through your questions and comments. Decide not to sit back and "learn" what the teacher has to say--assuming that it's my responsibility to make the course interesting. The truth is the most interesting classes are those in which the teacher and the students commit to honestly doing their best each day, working hard against the tendency to procrastinate their own learning. When students do not procrastinate their learning, they are less likely to keep their thoughts to themselves during class time. Instead they are willing and even anxious to share what they think in relation to what the teacher says or in response to their classmates' opinions. If you will commit to this I can promise you that this class will be one of the best classes you will take this semester. Why? Because you will make it so.

A Word about Computers

        From the start you should use the computers available on campus to become comfortable with word-processing techniques. I am not so interested that you learn all the "ins-and-outs" of high-tech programming, along with all the ways to link computers into networking configurations. In fact, I will not teach these things. I am more concerned with constantly giving you access to computers in class in order to practice how to "ride the cursor" in an effort to get down what you are thinking in the process of coming to know what you think and why. As a word processor the computer offers you one central luxury: it allows you to shape and hack away at and import thoughts and notions on a "flexible page." If you learn to use the computer's flexible page in this way, you will witness over and over the miracle and ease of idea-transformation and idea-shaping made possible only upon a computer screen. If you are nervous, welcome to the class. Learning to use the computer is based upon learning how to risk making mistakes--or what I prefer to call "developmental errors." When faced with risk we are always nervous. But working through nervousness to understanding is one of the important activities involved in real education.

The Role of Error in this Course

        To do well in this course you must be courageous enough to be responsible for your own education. Whether this has to do with learning the principles we're studying or learning to use the computer, it's all the same. Taking responsibility for your own learning is possible only by taking responsible risks. Responsible risks are taken when an engaged and committed mind is trying to break away from unthinking habits, but has not, as yet, discovered or understood the ideas and principles necessary to accomplish the escape. Risking in this way is likely to produce error. I call it "responsible error." Genuine education is based upon responsible error. But it is not to be confused with "irresponsible mistakes." Such mistakes are committed by those who are not engaged in learning much at all, but who go through the motions of completing assignments or contributing to class discussion just so the teacher has something to base a grade upon. Students who make irresponsible mistakes spend their effort and time pretending to be interested in education. The truth is they are here at Ricks (or where ever) to prove they already know the principles or perspectives of the course, or to show they don't have to learn them to get by. They learn to make-do with what they already know, learning how to comfortably ignore the challenges of present possibilities by secretly holding onto instruction, experiences, and opinions of their past.

        Fight this tendency to pretend to learn. Fight this tendency to conform your present life to habitual patterns of thinking, feeling, and writing. This will be challenging to you, and will take the kind of intellectual courage spoken of before. It will require you to trust yourself more than you have trusted yourself before. Such trust will enable you to discover who you really are, who you really have been, and who you can become. For this purpose listen in class, and read and write, to discover ideas that are new to you, or ideas you've heard before, ideas that are good, but to which you haven't as yet committed yourself. Write in and out of class to explore what you think, what you feel, and why. As you do, your responsible errors will eventually lead you to genuine knowledge, competency, and confidence. Carl Jung, a noted psychologist, observed: "Knowledge rests not upon truth alone, but upon error also." This is a great truth.

Learning to Write Ideas

      The goal of this course is to teach you to learn to use writing to gain personal insight, then to use writing to share the significance of that insight with others. As such we will spend much of our time studying and writing and rewriting ideas. You will start writing ideas that will cover a half page in length. You will learn what these pieces entail each time you are assigned to write. These pieces will increase in length the more you come to understand the various components of educated thinking that make up significant ideas. I intend to teach you an entire writing process by instructing you how to effectively rewrite and rethink these shorter idea pieces (shorter as contrasted with traditional papers--at least at the outset of the course). Don't assume, therefore, that shortness in length means less work in the course than what might be expected of you in a course that requires you to write essays. I will expect you to rework and rewrite and edit and organize these shorter pieces in ways more exact than what you might otherwise be required to do in a longer essay. The reason for this is that you will have less text to "work over" during your rewriting. So what you turn into me should be the best writing you are capable of. I'll grade it as such. And though the standards will demand much from you, I guarantee you will learn a lot. But let me caution you: Since each assignment builds on previous assignments, procrastinating doing your best from the outset will only keep you from accomplishing your best work in the long run--no matter what your midterm or final effort may be.

Your Grade in this Course:

        Your grade will be determined by adjusting the grades received on various work you complete in the course.  The work to be graded includes 1) 5-Question Quizzes, 2) preparation drafts, 3) midterm and final exams, and 4) idea responses in which your understanding of the principles and principle patterns of thinking and writing will be evaluated.  I will adjust your GPA by a standard you'll come to know as your "learning pattern."  Quizzes will be worth 15% of your grade; preparation drafts, 5% of your grade; midterm and final exams, 20% of your grade; and idea responses, 60% of your grade.  Unless an exception is made, you will have a quiz every other class period on the principles and subject matter discussed.   Therefore, it is in your best interest to take detailed notes of what we do each class period so you can study effectively for the up-coming quiz.  Midterm and Final Exams will be developed by using most of the quiz questions asked earlier, and by adding new questions appriopriate to the material we have studied since.

The Rationale

        The purpose of the rationale is to enable you to critically evaluate your work. It should be written as a separate sheet following the final piece of writing turned into me to date. The rationale should be at least two paragraphs in length. Write to me as though you were writing a letter. Begin with "Dear Brother Ward," or "Bro. Ward," or even "Oh brother, Ward!" Then care enough about your education to talk to me on paper about two things. The first is how your life is going in general. Share appropriately what your concerns are; what your stresses are; how college life is treating you; and how you're feeling about your life. Then move to the second part of the rationale. Talk to me about your experience in doing the assignment. Talk to me about questions you still have on what we've discussed; about ways you have tried to solve your problems in the course; or about your joys, successes, or insights born of your personal effort to learn. Give yourself a grade and finish up by writing your name. The points you choose to discuss are up to you. What you think is appropriate is what I'll accept. But remember: I will be empowered to evaluate your work more objectively as you enable me to appreciate what you have accomplished in light of what you have gone through.

PLEASE NOTE THE FOLLOWING CLASS POLICY: Any paper submitted late or without a rationale will be returned to you unread. It will receive a zero. Don't test me on this policy. Tears have been shed because I wouldn't grant an exception to well-intended, but negligent students.
